Ananya works on the payments team of a Bengaluru fintech, in a TypeScript monorepo of about 40,000 files. Today's job is small and tedious. A function called `formatAmount(paise)` turns an amount in paise into a string like "₹1,250.00", and now that the company takes payments in dirhams and dollars, it needs a second argument, `currency`. She opens `money/format.ts` in Cursor, types `, currency: Currency` into the signature, and before she has reached for the next file a faint grey suggestion appears further down: the line that builds the string, already rewritten to use `currency`. She presses Tab. The cursor jumps to the next line that needs changing, with that change also suggested. Tab again.

Then she opens the chat panel and types: *update every caller of formatAmount to pass the currency; INR unless the order says otherwise.* The agent finds 31 call sites across 19 files, some of which never mention the name `formatAmount` directly because they go through a wrapper. Diffs appear in each file within a second or two of each other, the type checker goes quiet, and she reviews the changes file by file.

It feels like a text editor with a clever friend inside it. But every part of that session was a hard engineering problem. The grey suggestion had to be computed by a model in a data centre, from about 13,000 tokens of her code, between two keystrokes, and four hundred million times a day across all users. A 600-line file had to be rewritten in a few seconds, not a minute. The agent had to find 31 call sites in 40,000 files, which means someone had to index a codebase they promised never to store. And the edits had to be checked against the type checker without touching her files until she agreed.

So the question this case study answers is: **how does an editor put a model's suggestions into your code fast enough to keep up with your typing, and correct enough to trust, in a repository far too big for the model to read?** We'll start from the simplest AI editor, find where it breaks, and work through the hard problems Cursor's makers (a company called Anysphere) solved, in roughly the order they hit them. For each one we'll see why it's hard, how the obvious approach fails, what Cursor did and when, and what it cost. Chapter 78 covered the agent loop, compaction, prompt caching and sandboxing for Claude Code; we'll lean on that chapter and spend our time on what Cursor does differently.

<SectionHeading n="01" id="requirements">What we're building, and how big</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="1.1" title="What it has to do">

Cursor is a code editor. It began in 2023 as a fork of Microsoft's open-source VS Code, so it looks and behaves like VS Code, with the same extensions, and adds AI features on top. Its job comes down to this list:

1. **Predict the next edit as you type** and offer it inline, so pressing Tab accepts it. This feature is called **Tab**.
2. **Answer questions and make changes from a chat panel**, from a one-line fix to a task across many files, carried out by an agent.
3. **Apply a model's proposed change to a file** quickly and exactly, as a diff the developer can review.
4. **Know the codebase**, including the parts the developer hasn't opened, without the developer pasting them in.
5. **Check its own work** against the compiler, type checker and tests.
6. **Run several agents at once**, locally or in the cloud, without them trampling each other's files.

And the qualities it needs:

- **Fast at keystroke speed**: a suggestion that arrives after the developer has typed the next character is worthless.
- **Quiet when unsure**: a wrong suggestion interrupts the developer and trains them to ignore all suggestions.
- **Private**: companies won't let an editor upload their source code to be stored, so whatever is kept on servers can't be the code.
- **Affordable**: a flat monthly price has to cover models that charge per token.

That first quality separates this case study from chapter 78. Claude Code's agent works for minutes; Cursor's Tab has a few hundred milliseconds, on every keystroke, and much of this chapter's first half is about that budget.

</Sub>

<Sub n="1.2" title="How big is it?">

Cursor grew faster than almost any software company before it. Its own funding announcements give the milestones: in June 2025 it raised $900 million at a $9.9 billion valuation and said it had passed $500 million in annual recurring revenue, with customers including over half of the Fortune 500. In November 2025 it raised $2.3 billion at a $29.3 billion valuation and said annualised revenue had crossed $1 billion, with a staff of over 300. Bloomberg reported in March 2026 that the run rate had doubled again to over $2 billion in three months. In June 2026 SpaceX agreed to buy the company for $60 billion in stock, and SpaceX's quarterly filing records that the merger completed on 14 August 2026.

For the engineering, the number that matters most comes from a September 2025 Cursor post: Tab was "handling over 400 million requests per day". In January 2025, Cursor said its new Tab model took about 13,000 tokens of context per request, up from 5,500.

<Think
  q="Take 400 million Tab requests a day, each carrying about 13,000 tokens of context and producing a suggestion of, say, 50 tokens. How many tokens per second does the model read, on average, and how does that compare with what it writes?"
  hint="A day has 86,400 seconds. Then multiply by the tokens each request reads, and separately by the tokens each writes."
>
400 million ÷ 86,400 is about 4,600 requests a second on average, and probably two or three times that during working hours in the big time zones. Each reads about 13,000 tokens, so the model reads around **60 million tokens a second**, and writes about 4,600 × 50 ≈ 230,000. That's more than 250 tokens read for every token written.

Two things follow. Like chapter 78's agent, Tab is dominated by reading (prefill), so its cost and its latency are decided by how much of each prompt can be reused from the previous one. And because a person typing sends a new request every keystroke, most of those requests are almost identical to the one before, differing by one character near the cursor. That's both the problem and the opportunity of section 3.
</Think>

(A **token** is the unit a language model reads and writes, a word or a piece of a word, as chapter 54 explains. **Prefill** is the pass in which a model reads its whole input before writing anything; chapter 54 covers it in detail.)

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="02" id="v1">Version 1: a chat panel beside the editor</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="2.1" title="The obvious design">

Picture the simplest AI editor: an ordinary editor with a chat panel bolted on. When Ananya asks a question, the editor sends the open file and her message to a frontier model's API, the most capable general-purpose model it can buy, and shows the reply. If the reply contains code, there's a button to paste it in.

This design breaks in four places, and each one is a section of this chapter.

- **It's too slow for typing.** A frontier model takes a second or more just to start answering. That's fine for a chat, and useless for a suggestion between keystrokes (section 3).
- **It doesn't know when to keep quiet.** If we did ask for a suggestion on every keystroke, most would be wrong or unwanted, and each one pulls Ananya's eyes away from what she's writing (section 4).
- **It can't apply its own answer.** The model's reply is a snippet or a sketch with "... rest of the file unchanged". Turning that into a correct edit of a 600-line file is a problem of its own (section 5).
- **It sees one file.** The callers are in 19 other files. Finding them means searching a codebase the model can't read in full (sections 6 to 8).

We'll start with the first, because it shaped the company. Chapter 78's agent could afford to be slow. Tab couldn't.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="03" id="tab">Hard problem 1: a suggestion between two keystrokes</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="3.1" title="Predict the next edit, not the next word">

Early AI completion tools, such as GitHub Copilot in 2021, predicted the text that comes next at the cursor: you type `function isEven(n) {` and it suggests the body. That only helps when you're writing new code at the end of a line.

Ananya isn't writing new code. She's changing existing code, and the change she just made in the signature implies other changes further down the file: the body has to use `currency`, the JSDoc comment has to mention it, the call inside the same file needs a new argument. None of those are at her cursor. Cursor's makers described the goal in their 2024 interview with Lex Fridman as eliminating "all the low entropy actions you take inside of the editor": once her intent is clear from the first edit, the rest is predictable, and the editor should "jump you forward in time".

So Tab predicts a **next edit**: given the file, the cursor position and the edits she just made, what change, anywhere nearby, comes next? The suggestion can delete and replace text on several lines, not only insert at the cursor. In May 2024 Cursor extended this to predicting the next *location* as well, so pressing Tab can move the cursor to the line that needs changing and show the change there. In the interview one founder described it as pressing Tab and having the editor "go 18 lines down and then show you the next edit". Cursor calls these moves **jumps**.

Predicting edits means training on sequences of edits, which a model trained on finished files has never seen. That's the first reason Cursor couldn't just call someone else's API.

</Sub>

Cursor published the server side of that budget when it released a new Tab model, which it called Fusion, in January 2025: the median server latency fell from 475 ms to 260 ms, while the context grew from 5,500 to 13,000 tokens and the suggestions got "over 10x longer". Network time comes on top. So the whole round trip lives in a few hundred milliseconds, about the time between two keystrokes for a fast typist.

<Why>Why not use the best model available and accept the wait?</Why>

Because the shape of the work is wrong for a big model. Section 1.2 showed that Tab reads about 13,000 tokens and writes a few dozen. With so few decode steps, the latency is dominated by prefill, the time to read the prompt, and prefill time grows with the size of the model. A frontier model with maybe hundreds of billions of active weights would spend most of the budget just reading. The founders explained their answer in the 2024 interview: Tab sees very long prompts of the user's code and needs very low latency, so "you need to train small models on this task", and "the perfect fit for that is using a sparse model, meaning an MOE model".

A **mixture-of-experts** (MoE) model splits each layer's feed-forward network into many smaller networks called experts, and a small router picks a few of them for each token. The model holds many weights, so it knows a lot, but each token passes through only a fraction of them, so it costs about as much to run as a much smaller dense model. That fits a workload that reads a lot and must answer fast. Cursor hasn't published the size of its Tab models; judging by the latency, they're probably a small fraction of a frontier model's size.

</Decision>

<Sub n="3.3" title="Reusing the work between keystrokes">

Even a small model has to read 13,000 tokens. So the trick is to avoid reading them from scratch every time.

Chapter 54 explained the **KV cache**: as a model reads a prompt, it computes for every token and every layer a key and a value vector, and a later request that starts with exactly the same tokens can reuse those vectors instead of recomputing them. Chapter 78 showed how Claude Code arranges its prompts so each call shares a long prefix with the one before. Tab's case is more extreme. Between two of Ananya's keystrokes, almost everything in the prompt is the same: the same file, the same related snippets, the same recent edits. Only the region around the cursor changed.

In the 2024 interview the founders described three ways Cursor exploits this:

- **Caching-aware prompts.** "You need to design the actual prompts you use for the model such that they're caching aware". In practice that means the stable parts (other files, snippets from elsewhere in the repo) go first and the parts that change with every keystroke (the lines around the cursor) go last, so a new keystroke invalidates only the tail of the cached prefix.
- **Warming the cache.** As soon as Ananya starts typing in a file, the editor can send the file's contents ahead of any suggestion request, so the model servers build the KV cache for it in advance: "you can immediately warm the cache with let's say the current file contents". When the real request arrives, most of the prefill is already done, which "will significantly lower" the time to the first token.
- **Predicting ahead.** When a suggestion is shown, Cursor can already request the *next* suggestion, assuming she accepts this one, and cache it. If she presses Tab, the follow-up suggestion is ready at once. That's what makes a chain of Tab, Tab, Tab through a file feel instant.

<Callout label="Design implication">
When requests from one user are nearly identical and arrive many times a second, the cache is the product. Order the prompt so the volatile part is at the end, start computing the stable part before anyone asks, and spend spare capacity guessing the next request. All three only work if the same user's requests reach the machine that holds their cache, the sticky routing that chapter 78, section 10.2, describes.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.4" title="Throwing away stale requests">

There's one more source of waste. Ananya types `, currency` as ten keystrokes in maybe a second. If the editor sent a request on every keystroke and every request ran to completion, the model would compute ten suggestions, nine of which are out of date before they arrive: they're predictions for a file she has already changed. They can't even be shown, because they'd be suggesting edits to text that no longer exists.

Two standard fixes come from old user-interface practice. **Debouncing** waits until the typing pauses for a moment before sending a request at all. **Cancellation** sends requests freely but tells the server to stop working on a request as soon as a newer one from the same editor arrives. Cursor hasn't published its exact policy, and it's probably some mix of the two, so here's what each one buys, on a simulated burst of 30 keystrokes with each suggestion costing 250 ms of model time (an illustrative figure):

<TryIt what="Keystroke requests: send every one, cancel stale ones, or debounce" lang="python" toolchain="Python 3.14">
```python
import random
random.seed(11)

# Ananya types 30 keystrokes, 60-220 ms apart, then stops to read.
t, keys = 0, []
for _ in range(30):
    t += random.randint(60, 220)
    keys.append(t)
WORK = 250   # ms of model time to produce one suggestion (illustrative)

def run(debounce=0, cancel=False):
    sent = gpu = 0
    for i, k in enumerate(keys):
        nxt = keys[i + 1] if i + 1 < len(keys) else float("inf")
        start = k + debounce
        if start >= nxt:              # another key arrived during the wait: never sent
            continue
        sent += 1
        gpu += min(WORK, nxt - start) if cancel else WORK
    return sent, gpu, debounce + WORK  # the last request is the only one shown

print(f"{'strategy':28} {'sent':>4} {'model ms':>9} {'wait after last key':>20}")
for name, kw in [("every key, no cancel", {}),
                 ("every key, cancel stale", {"cancel": True}),
                 ("debounce 50 ms + cancel", {"debounce": 50, "cancel": True}),
                 ("debounce 150 ms + cancel", {"debounce": 150, "cancel": True})]:
    sent, gpu, wait = run(**kw)
    print(f"{name:28} {sent:>4} {gpu:>9,} {wait:>17} ms")
```

Read the columns as a trade. Without cancellation, the model does 7.5 seconds of work for one useful suggestion. Cancelling stale requests cuts that by about 40% at no cost to Ananya, because the suggestion she sees still arrives 250 ms after her last key. Debouncing saves much more compute, but every millisecond of debounce is added to her wait. A 150 ms debounce cuts model time by almost 90% and makes every suggestion feel 60% slower.

That's why the techniques in 3.3 matter so much: cache warming and predicting ahead cut the 250 ms itself, which is the only way to save compute *and* time together. A cancelled request's prefill isn't wasted either, if its KV cache stays around for the next request, which shares almost the same prefix.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="04" id="quiet">Hard problem 2: knowing when to stay quiet</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="4.1" title="A wrong suggestion has a cost">

Suppose Tab is fast. It still has to decide, on each of those 400 million requests, whether to show anything. A suggestion that Ananya rejects isn't free: she has to read it, judge it wrong and keep typing around it, and enough wrong ones teach her to stop looking at grey text, at which point even the good ones are wasted. So the system needs a policy for when to speak.

An obvious design is a separate filter: train a small classifier that predicts, from the context and the candidate suggestion, whether the user will accept it, and show the suggestion only above some threshold. Cursor's September 2025 post on Tab sets its own approach against that one.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.2" title="Training on accept and reject">

Cursor's approach is **reinforcement learning** (RL): instead of learning from examples of correct output, the model produces outputs, receives a numerical reward for each, and is updated to make high-reward outputs more likely. Cursor's method is a **policy gradient**: nudge the model's weights in the direction that raises the probability of the actions that earned more reward than average, and lowers the others. Here, the "action" includes the option of suggesting nothing.

Cursor's post gives an example of the reward: 0.75 for a suggestion the user accepts, −0.25 for one they reject, and 0 for showing nothing. (The production reward is more involved, it says.)

<Predict
  q="With those rewards, when does the model maximise its expected reward by showing a suggestion instead of staying quiet?"
  options={["Whenever it has any suggestion at all", "When it thinks the chance of acceptance is above 25%", "When it thinks the chance of acceptance is above 50%", "When it thinks the chance of acceptance is above 75%"]}
  answer={1}
>
Above 25%. If the model believes the acceptance probability is p, showing earns 0.75p − 0.25(1 − p) = p − 0.25 on average, and staying quiet earns 0. Showing is worth it exactly when p is above 0.25. Cursor's post makes the same point: the policy should suggest only "when it estimates the accept chance is at least 25%". Nobody writes that threshold into the code. The model learns it, because that's what the rewards pay for, and moving the threshold means changing the rewards.
</Predict>

Here's the hard part: policy gradient methods need **on-policy** data: rewards for suggestions made by the current model, not by some older one, because the update is about what *this* model would do. Cursor's users generate those rewards with every accept and reject, so the loop is to ship a new model, collect a few hours of accept and reject signals, train on them, and ship again. According to the post, rolling out a checkpoint and collecting the data for the next step takes "1.5 to 2 hours", so new Tab models went out several times a day.

So Ananya's Tab, Tab, Tab is fast and mostly right. The chat request is another matter: the agent has decided what to change in `checkout/summary.ts`, and now that change has to land in a 600-line file.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="05" id="apply">Hard problem 3: applying an edit to a big file</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="5.1" title="Diffs, snippets or the whole file?">

When a model proposes a change, there are three ways it could express it, and each has a failure Cursor ran into.

The model could write a **diff**: the lines to remove and the lines to add, with line numbers. That's compact, but models are bad at it. Cursor's May 2024 post on applying edits lists the reasons: line numbers are hard for tokenizers and models to get right, models have seen far fewer diffs than whole files in training, and "most models fail to output accurate diffs, with the exception of Claude Opus" (at the time). A diff with one wrong line number corrupts the file.

Or it could write a **sketch**: the changed parts, with comments like `// ... existing code ...` for the rest. That's what chat models naturally produce, and it's what Ananya would get in Version 1. Something still has to work out where each changed part goes.

Or the model could **rewrite the whole file**. That's the most reliable format. Cursor's post argues that more output tokens give the model "more forward passes to determine the correct solution", and that diffs "force the model to think in fewer tokens". But frontier models were slow and "lazy" on large rewrites, dropping chunks of the file or summarising them, and a 600-line file is several thousand tokens: at the 50 to 100 tokens a second of a big model in 2024, that's a minute or more.

Cursor split the job in two. The chat or agent model, a frontier model, writes the sketch: what should change. A second, specialised **apply model** takes the original file and the sketch and writes out the complete new file. Then the editor diffs old and new to show Ananya the change. Writing a whole file is now the apply model's only job, and the remaining problem is speed.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.2" title="Speculative edits">

Chapter 54, section 11, described **speculative decoding**. Generating text is slow because a model produces one token per forward pass, and each pass has to read all of the model's weights from memory. But a pass that checks several tokens at once costs about the same as a pass that produces one. So a fast **draft** guesses the next few tokens, the big model checks them all in one pass, keeps the ones it agrees with up to the first disagreement, and adds its own token there. Normally the draft comes from a small second model.

Cursor's observation, in the May 2024 post, was that an apply model doesn't need a draft model at all: "With code edits, we have a strong prior on the draft tokens at any point in time". When rewriting a 600-line file to change three lines, the output is the original file almost everywhere. In the founders' words, the prior "is literally the same exact code". So the draft is the original file itself, and they called the technique **speculative edits**.

Fireworks, which served the model, spelled out the server-side mechanics in a June 2024 post by Fireworks, which served the model. The editor sends the original text in a `speculation` field of the request. The server decodes greedily, always taking the most likely token, and "will find the longest prefix of the 'speculation' field that matches the model's generation", then continues normal generation from there. Greedy decoding matters: with temperature zero there's exactly one correct output, so checking the draft is a plain comparison, and the speed-up can't change the answer. In the 2024 interview the founders added that after a disagreement, once enough of the model's tokens match the original again, it resumes speculating on the following chunks.

```python
import random
random.seed(3)

# The old file: 2,000 "tokens". The edit rewrites lines 1,200-1,239 (40 tokens);
# the model's job is to output the whole new file.
old = [f"t{i}" for i in range(2000)]
new = old[:1200] + [f"n{i}" for i in range(40)] + old[1240:]

def plain(new):
    return len(new)                    # one forward pass per output token

def speculative(old, new, k):
    """Draft = the old file. Each pass checks up to k draft tokens at once and
    keeps the matching run plus one token of the model's own."""
    out, j, passes = [], 0, 0           # j: where in the old file the draft continues
    while len(out) < len(new):
        passes += 1
        draft = old[j:j + k]
        ok = 0
        while ok < len(draft) and len(out) + ok < len(new) and draft[ok] == new[len(out) + ok]:
            ok += 1
        out += new[len(out):len(out) + ok]
        j += ok
        if len(out) < len(new):
            tok = new[len(out)]         # the model's own token where the draft was wrong
            out.append(tok)
            if tok in old[j:]:          # back in old text: re-align the draft there
                j = old.index(tok, j) + 1
    return passes

print(f"output tokens:              {len(new):,}")
print(f"token by token:             {plain(new):,} passes")
for k in (8, 16, 32, 64):
    print(f"old file as draft, k = {k:>2}:  {speculative(old, new, k):,} passes")
```

<SectionHeading n="06" id="index">Hard problem 4: finding the right code in 40,000 files</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="6.1" title="Search by meaning">

Ananya's request, "update every caller of formatAmount", has an easy part and a hard part. Its easy part is the 24 files that contain the string `formatAmount`; a text search finds them. The hard part is the rest: a `PriceTag` component that receives a formatter as a prop, a `useMoney()` hook that wraps it, an email template that calls a helper defined in another package. The agent also has to answer vaguer questions on other days, such as "where do we round currency amounts?", where the code it needs may not contain any of the words in the question.

Chapter 78, section 3.3, laid out the two ways to find code: build an index ahead of time and look things up by meaning, or let the model search step by step with grep and file reads. Claude Code chose the second. For most of its life Cursor chose both, and in July 2026 it changed its mind. To see why each choice made sense when it was made, we'll build the index first.

This index works by meaning. An **embedding model** turns a piece of text into a vector, a list of a few hundred or a few thousand numbers, trained so that pieces of code that do similar things get vectors that point in similar directions. Embed every piece of the codebase once, embed the question when it arrives, and the code most likely to be relevant is the code whose vectors are nearest to the question's. That's **semantic search**, and chapter 58, section 5, explains how a nearest-neighbour index finds the closest vectors among billions without comparing against all of them.

</Sub>

<Sub n="6.2" title="Cursor's index, 2023 to 2026">

Each piece of code is a **chunk**. Cursor's docs (October 2025) said files were split along "functions, classes, and logical code blocks", instead of at arbitrary points, so a chunk is something meaningful on its own. Cutting at syntax boundaries needs a parser that understands each language, and the open-source **tree-sitter** library is the usual choice for that in editors, though Cursor hasn't said which parser it used.

Cursor's security page (June 2025) described the rest of the pipeline in unusual detail. The server chunks and embeds the files and stores the vectors in **Turbopuffer**, a vector database built on object storage. With each vector it keeps the chunk's line range and an obfuscated file path, and nothing else: no code. Cursor trained its own embedding model. A November 2025 post explained how it was trained: from real agent sessions, a language model ranked which pieces of code would have helped at each step, and the embedding model was trained "to align its similarity scores with these LLM-generated rankings".

<Sub n="6.3" title="An index for every codebase">

A web search engine has one huge index. Cursor needed millions of small ones: every user's copy of every codebase they open, each changing as they type, most of them idle at any moment.

Turbopuffer's case study (as of late 2025) gives the shape. Each (user, codebase) pair was a separate **namespace**, an independent index, and there were over 10 million of them holding over 100 billion vectors, with write peaks of 10 GB a second. Turbopuffer keeps every namespace in object storage like S3 (chapter 32), the cheapest durable storage there is, and pulls the active ones into memory and NVMe SSD caches when they're queried. Inactive codebases "fade into object storage", costing almost nothing while nobody uses them. Cursor moved to Turbopuffer in November 2023 from a vector database with a "traditional storage architecture", where every index had to live on some server's disk whether it was used or not. The case study claims a 95% cost reduction; Cursor's Aman Sanger is quoted on "saving an order of magnitude in costs".

When you have millions of small, mostly idle indexes, what matters most is what an idle index costs, more than how fast one search runs. Keeping each one on object storage and caching only the hot ones turns that cost from "a slice of a server per index" to "a few cents of storage", and that change made per-user codebase indexes affordable.
</Callout>

An index is only useful if it matches the code. Ananya's codebase changes every few seconds while she works, and sometimes by thousands of files when she switches branches. How does the server learn what changed, without being sent the code?

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="07" id="sync">Hard problem 5: keeping the index fresh, cheap and private</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="7.1" title="Which files changed? A Merkle tree">

A naive way to keep the server's index current is to ask, every few minutes, "here are the hashes of all my files; which ones don't you have?" For Ananya's 40,000 files that's 40,000 hashes per check, per user, all day. Cursor's January 2026 post on indexing gives the size: for a 50,000-file workspace, the file names and SHA-256 hashes come to about 3.2 MB. Multiply by every open editor every few minutes and the founders' description of the naive approach in 2024, "absolutely ginormous network overhead", sounds about right.

A better fix is the data structure chapter 66 used for Dropbox, applied to folders. A **hash** turns any amount of data into a short fixed-size fingerprint that changes if any byte changes. Give every file a hash of its contents. Give every folder a hash computed from the names and hashes of its children. Then the root folder's hash summarises the entire codebase: if any file anywhere changes, its hash changes, so its folder's hash changes, and so on up to the root. A tree of hashes built this way is a **Merkle tree**. One of the founders described the sync in 2024: "for every folder you can keep a hash, which is the hash of all of its children", and "you just try to reconcile the single hash, which is at the root of the project".

<Picture
  src="/images/designs/cursor/merkle-tree-proof-path.png"

  caption="A Merkle tree over four blocks. Each box above the bottom row is a hash of the boxes below it, so the top hash changes if any block changes. The highlighted path shows how little of the tree you need to follow to reach one changed block: one hash per level, plus its siblings to compare."

  href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hash_Tree_proof.svg"

Reconciling works top down. The editor and the server compare root hashes. If they match, as they usually do, nothing has changed and the check is over after one comparison. If they differ, compare the root's children, and descend only into the folders whose hashes differ. The changed files are found at the bottom of a few narrow paths. Cursor's security page (June 2025) described exactly this: compute a Merkle tree of all files, ignoring anything in `.gitignore` or `.cursorignore`, sync it to the server, and "every 10 minutes, we check for hash mismatches, and use the Merkle tree to figure out which files have changed and only upload those". (The docs in October 2025 said every 5 minutes; the interval seems to have been tuned.)

This program builds Merkle trees for a 40,000-file repo before and after Ananya edits three files, and counts the hashes compared to find them:

<TryIt what="Find three changed files among 40,000 by walking a Merkle tree" lang="python" toolchain="Python 3.14">
```python
import hashlib, random
random.seed(7)

def h(data):
    return hashlib.sha256(data.encode()).hexdigest()

# A repo of 40,000 files in nested folders: 20 top dirs x 20 subdirs x 100 files
repo = {f"src/m{a}/p{b}/f{c}.py": f"code {a} {b} {c}"
        for a in range(20) for b in range(20) for c in range(100)}

def tree(files):
    """Merkle tree: a file's hash is its content's hash; a folder's hash is
    the hash of its children's names and hashes, so it changes if anything below it changes."""
    root = {}
    for path, text in files.items():
        node = root
        *dirs, name = path.split("/")
        for d in dirs:
            node = node.setdefault(d, {})
        node[name] = h(text)
    def seal(node):
        kids = {k: (seal(v) if isinstance(v, dict) else (v, None)) for k, v in node.items()}
        return h("".join(k + kids[k][0] for k in sorted(kids))), kids
    return seal(root)

def diff(client, server, path="", counter=None):
    """Walk both trees from the root, going down only where hashes differ."""
    counter[0] += 1
    if client[0] == server[0]:
        return []
    if client[1] is None:                    # a file whose hash differs
        return [path]
    changed = []
    for name, child in client[1].items():
        other = server[1].get(name, ("missing", {}))
        changed += diff(child, other, f"{path}/{name}" if path else name, counter)
    return changed

server = tree(repo)                          # what the server indexed last time
edited = dict(repo)
for p in random.sample(sorted(repo), 3):     # Ananya edits three files
    edited[p] += " # changed"
client = tree(edited)

n = [0]
changed = diff(client, server, "", n)
print(f"files in repo:            {len(repo):,}")
print(f"naive: hashes compared    {len(repo):,}")
print(f"merkle: hashes compared   {n[0]:,}")
for p in sorted(changed):
    print("  re-upload", p)
```

  re-upload src/m12/p8/f87.py
  re-upload src/m18/p2/f29.py
  re-upload src/m2/p8/f77.py

382 comparisons instead of 40,000, roughly a hundred times fewer, and the three changed files come out by name. Where do the 382 go? At each folder whose hash differs, the walk compares all of that folder's children: 20 at the top, 20 in each of the changed subfolders, 100 in each changed leaf folder. So the cost is roughly (number of changed files) × (depth) × (children per folder), and it doesn't grow with the size of the repo at all. When nothing has changed, the cost is one comparison. A real implementation sends hashes over the network a level at a time, so the comparisons become round trips and bytes saved.

Only the three changed files are uploaded, chunked and embedded again. And even those are mostly unchanged: if Ananya edited one function in a 600-line file, most of the file's chunks are identical to before.

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.2" title="Embed each chunk once, ever">

Embedding is the expensive step. In the 2024 interview Aman Sanger said "embeddings are the bottleneck", and described the fix: a cache "on the actual vectors computed from the hash of a given chunk". Before embedding a chunk, the server hashes its text and looks the hash up; if any user anywhere has embedded identical text before, the stored vector is reused. The security page says the cache lived in AWS, "indexed by the hash of the chunk".

That cache does two jobs. When Ananya edits one function, only that function's chunk is new. And when her colleague opens the same repository for the first time, nearly every chunk is already in the cache, so "the nth person at a company" gets an index almost at once, in Sanger's words. It's the same move as the content-addressed blocks in chapter 66: name data by its hash, and identical data is stored and processed once.

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.3" title="Paths that hide their names">

Each vector carries a file path, so that a search can be limited to a folder and so the editor knows which file to read. But a path like `payments/upi/refund_fraud_rules.ts` says a lot about a company. Cursor's security page described the scheme: "the path is split by '/' and '.' and each segment is encrypted with a secret key stored on the client and a deterministic short 6-byte nonce".

Each piece of that sentence is a design choice:

- **Per segment**, so `payments/upi/refund_fraud_rules.ts` becomes four encrypted pieces joined the same way. The server can still filter by folder, because every file under `payments/` shares the same first encrypted segment.
- **Deterministic**, so the same name always encrypts to the same value. That's what makes filtering possible, and it's also a leak: the server can see that two files share a folder, and how deep the tree goes. Cursor's page says so: it "leaks information about directory hierarchy".
- **The key stays on the client.** The server can't decrypt paths. For teammates to share an index they need the same key, so later versions derived it "from hashes of recent commit contents", something everyone with a copy of the repository can compute and nobody without one can.

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.4" title="Borrowing a teammate's index without seeing their code">

One problem remained. When a new engineer opened a big repository, indexing it from scratch "can take hours", Cursor wrote in January 2026, even with the embedding cache, because every file still had to be uploaded, chunked and looked up. Yet someone in the same company had almost certainly indexed nearly the same code already: Cursor measured that "clones of the same codebase average 92% similarity across users within an organization".

Reusing a teammate's index raises two questions. How do you find a similar index without comparing Merkle trees file by file against every index in the company? And how do you make sure the newcomer can't see search results for files they don't have, such as a folder their teammate has access to and they don't?

For the first, the editor computes a **simhash** of its Merkle tree. An ordinary hash changes completely if one input bit changes; a simhash is built so that similar inputs give similar hashes. Roughly: hash each file, and for each of the 64 bit positions add one if a file's hash has a 1 there and subtract one if it has a 0; the simhash has a 1 wherever the total is positive. Two repositories that share 92% of their files get simhashes that differ in only a few bits, so finding a similar index becomes a nearest-neighbour search on simhashes. The server searches the simhashes of the team's existing indexes and, above a similarity threshold, copies the closest one as a starting point in the background while the Merkle sync fixes the differences.

For the second, the Merkle tree doubles as proof. The editor uploads its whole tree, which ties each encrypted path to the hash of its contents, and the server keeps it as a set of **content proofs**. A search over the borrowed index might return a chunk from a file the newcomer doesn't have; the server checks it against the proofs, and "if the client can't prove it has a file, the result is dropped". Once the newcomer's own index has caught up and the Merkle roots match, the proofs are deleted.

<SectionHeading n="08" id="grep">Hard problem 6: grep that keeps up, and a change of mind</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="8.1" title="When grep takes fifteen seconds">

Cursor's agent never relied on semantic search alone. Its November 2025 post said: "Our agent makes heavy use of grep as well as semantic search, and the combination of these two leads to the best outcomes". That post's numbers were the case for keeping the index: with semantic search available, agents answered questions about codebases 12.5% more accurately on average (6.5% to 23.5% depending on the model), and the share of agent-written code that users kept rose by 0.3%, or 2.6% on codebases of 1,000 files or more.

But the other half of the combination was getting slow. Agents call grep far more often than people do, and in a big monorepo each call scans every file. Cursor's March 2026 post on regex search said: "We routinely see `rg` invocations that take more than 15 seconds". A model waiting 15 seconds per search, maybe a dozen searches per task, spends most of its time waiting.

</Sub>

<Sub n="8.2" title="An index for regular expressions">

A textbook way to make text search fast is an **inverted index**, the structure chapter 62 builds for search engines: a map from each term to the list of documents that contain it, called its **posting list**. Code search can't use words as terms, because a regular expression like `format[A-Z]\w*\(` matches inside words. So code search engines index **trigrams**, every run of three consecutive characters. The file containing `formatAmount` has the trigrams `for`, `orm`, `rma`, `mat` and so on. A query for `formatAmount` must match a file that contains all of that query's trigrams, so intersecting their posting lists gives a short list of candidate files, and only those are scanned with the full regular expression. Google Code Search worked this way in 2006, and GitHub's code search is built on the same idea.

Trouble comes from common trigrams, like `ion` or `   ` (three spaces), which appear in nearly every file, so their posting lists are huge and filter nothing. Cursor's March 2026 post described its fix, **sparse n-grams**: give every pair of adjacent characters a weight from a fixed table, built from how often pairs occur across "a couple terabytes of Open-Source code", and cut text into n-grams of varying length at the points the weights choose. Rare sequences end up in long, selective n-grams; the boundaries depend only on the text, so the same string is always cut the same way in the file and in the query. At index time the editor extracts every n-gram a file could need; at query time it picks the few that cover the query. "We're paying a high upfront cost when indexing so that we can have very fast queries at query time."

This index lives on Ananya's machine. Its file format is a posting-list file and a sorted table of n-gram hashes with offsets into it; only the table is memory-mapped and binary-searched. Storing hashes instead of n-grams can't give wrong answers: a hash collision only makes a posting list a little longer, and the final regex scan removes any false candidates. The index is anchored to a git commit, and "user and agent changes are stored as a layer on top of it", so it doesn't rescan the repo as files change. Cursor calls the feature **Instant Grep**.

</Sub>

<Sub n="8.3" title="July 2026: turning the semantic index off">

In July 2026 Cursor's staff explained on its forum why the semantic index was disappearing from users' settings. one wrote on 21 July that models had become very good at using grep and indexed search, so the dedicated semantic search path "was rapidly no longer helping in a meaningful way"; another said the next day that "semantic/embeddings indexing is being turned down in favor of grep-based retrieval". Cursor "no longer needs to build a semantic index of your codebase which means we're no longer computing embeddings of your code or storing them on our servers for search". The Instant Grep index is kept per repository, under `.git/cursor/`, at about half the size of the source it indexes. Cursor's docs now say Instant Grep "builds and queries its index on your machine".

Cursor's change of mind is instructive because nothing about the index broke. It worked, it was fast, and in 2025 it measurably helped. What changed was the model on the other end. An agent that can run twenty precise searches in a few seconds, and is smart enough to try `useMoney` after reading the definition of `formatAmount`, gets most of what semantic search offered, and the large, privacy-sensitive server-side index stopped paying for itself. Claude Code (chapter 78) reached the same conclusion from the other direction in 2025, choosing agentic search from the start. Cursor's contribution was making grep fast enough that the choice cost nothing.
</Decision>

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Treating a design decision as permanent because it was right when made. Cursor's index was the right call in 2023, when models searched badly, and a liability by 2026. Write down *why* a component exists, in terms of what the rest of the system can't yet do, so you notice when that stops being true.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="8.4" title="Assembling the prompt">

Whatever finds the code, something has to decide what goes into each model request, and a recurring theme of Cursor's design is that it decides on its servers. Its security page said that requests "always hit our infrastructure on AWS even if you have configured your own API key", because "our prompt-building happens on our server". That's where the system prompt, tool definitions and the team's rules are combined with what the editor sent.

For Tab, the founders described in 2024 a prompt renderer they called **Preempt**, in which prompts are written like React components, each piece of context with a priority. For the lines around the cursor, "that line has the highest priority, and then you subtract one for every line that is farther away". The renderer fills the token budget in priority order, so whatever the budget, the most important context is always in and the least important is cut first.

For the agent, the team's standing instructions come from **rules** files. A project keeps them in `.cursor/rules` as Markdown files with a small header that says when each applies: always, when the agent judges it relevant from a one-line description, only for files matching a pattern (`money/**/*.ts`), or only when mentioned by name. Cursor also reads `AGENTS.md` files, the cross-tool equivalent of Claude Code's CLAUDE.md. A January 2026 post described **dynamic context discovery**: long tool outputs and terminal sessions are written to files the agent can search instead of being pasted into the window, and MCP tool descriptions sit in per-server folders with only their names in the prompt. For runs that used MCP tools, that cut total agent tokens by 46.9% in an A/B test. It's chapter 78's "handles instead of contents" principle, reached independently.

Privacy is enforced on the same servers. Every request carries a header saying whether the user is in privacy mode (if it's missing, the server assumes yes), and a proxy sends each service's traffic to one of "two near-identical replicas", one for privacy-mode requests, where logging is switched off, and one for the rest. In June 2025 Cursor said more than half of its users had privacy mode on.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="09" id="checking">Hard problem 7: checking the AI's work without touching your files</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="9.1" title="Lints for an edit nobody has saved">

The agent has rewritten 19 files. Some of the rewrites are wrong in ways a compiler would spot at once: a caller that passes `order.currency` where `order` can be undefined, a wrapper whose type now needs a second parameter. Ananya's editor knows about these errors within a second of a file changing, because a **language server** is running beside it: a separate process that understands one language (the TypeScript server, `gopls` for Go, `rust-analyzer` for Rust) and reports errors, warnings and definitions to the editor over the **Language Server Protocol** (LSP). The red squiggles in her editor are its output. An agent that could see those diagnostics before showing its edits could fix its own mistakes.

But a language server reports on files as the editor has them. If the agent writes its proposed edits into Ananya's files to get them checked, she sees half-finished changes appearing in her editor while she's typing, and her own edits and the agent's can collide. Cursor's September 2024 post on the problem put the requirement bluntly: "independence is absolutely critical". The AI needs to see lints for its version of the code while Ananya keeps working on hers.

</Sub>

<Sub n="9.2" title="The shadow workspace">

Cursor's post walks through the options it considered and rejected, which are a good lesson in how many layers a simple-sounding requirement touches.

- **Copy the file in the editor's memory and lint the copy.** The language server would then see two versions of the same file and get confused: go-to-references, Go's package scope and Rust's module system all assume one file per path.
- **Start a second copy of each language server.** That means separate processes for `tsc`, `gopls` and `rust-analyzer`, each with its own configuration, for every language anyone uses.
- **Copy the folder on disk.** `cp -r` is far too slow for a project with a `node_modules` folder; hard links and copy-on-write clones risk the agent changing the originals.
- **Fake the folder in the kernel.** Present a virtual folder that looks like the real one with the AI's edits laid over it. On Linux, FUSE does that. macOS and Windows have no built-in equivalent, and shipping a kernel extension to every user wasn't practical.

"The core code required to hide the window is just one line," the post notes.

Memory was the cost: a second window "naively comes with a 2x increase in memory usage". Cursor cut that by running fewer extensions in the hidden window and killing it after 15 minutes idle, and made the feature opt-in. Rust wasn't supported, because `rust-analyzer` checks code by running `cargo check` on files on disk, and the shadow workspace never writes to disk. </Sub>

<Sub n="9.3" title="Later: sandboxes and terminals">

That later answer arrived as agents grew more capable. An agent that runs tests needs a real copy of the files, and by Cursor 2.0 (October 2025) its agent's terminal commands ran sandboxed by default on macOS, with read and write access to the workspace and no internet for commands not on an allow list. Chapter 78, section 5, covers how such sandboxes are built from operating-system features. For running several agents without them editing each other's files, Cursor used separate copies of the repository, which is the subject of section 11.


</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="10" id="models">Hard problem 8: paying for intelligence</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="10.1" title="A flat price on a per-token bill">

Every chat and agent request in Cursor's early years went to a frontier model bought by the token, while users paid Cursor a flat subscription. Chapter 78, section 8, showed how quickly agent tokens add up: one 50-step task can read five million. As agents got longer, a $20-a-month user could probably cost Cursor more than $20 in a week.

Cursor's first plan counted requests: in mid-2025, Pro included 500 a month, with some models counting double. On 16 June 2025 it changed Pro to "$20 of included usage" at the model providers' API prices, plus unlimited Tab, and added a $200 Ultra plan. Heavy users of expensive models ran through the allowance in days and were charged for more, many without understanding the change. On 4 July 2025 the CEO, Michael Truell, published an apology, "We recognize that we didn't handle this pricing rollout well, and we're sorry", and offered refunds for unexpected charges between 16 June and 4 July.

That episode exposed a structural problem: a company that resells other companies' models at a flat price is squeezed from both sides, by its suppliers' prices and by its users' growing appetite. One way out is to own more of the intelligence.

</Sub>

<Sub n="10.2" title="Composer: training an agent model in real repositories">

On 29 October 2025, with Cursor 2.0, the company released **Composer**, its own model for agent work. Its post describes a mixture-of-experts model (section 3.2) trained with reinforcement learning on "real-world software engineering challenges in large codebases". During training the model works through tasks with the same tools the product's agent has: reading and editing files, semantic search, grep and terminal commands. Cursor said it generates code "four times faster than similar models" and completes most agent turns in under 30 seconds.

<Why>Why train in real environments instead of on examples of good code?</Why>

Because an agent's skill is in the sequence: which file to open next, which search to run, when to run the tests, when to stop. Examples of finished code don't contain any of that. RL lets the model try whole tasks and rewards it for outcomes, the same idea as Tab's accept and reject in section 4, stretched over hundreds of steps. But each attempt needs a real machine with a real repository, its dependencies installed, and a terminal the model can run commands in. Cursor's post says training needed "hundreds of thousands of concurrent sandboxed coding environments in the cloud", which it built by adapting the infrastructure it had made for its cloud agents (section 11) and rewriting the scheduler for virtual machines to cope with training's bursts.

</Decision>

<SectionHeading n="11" id="parallel">Hard problem 9: many agents, one repository</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="11.1" title="Worktrees and cloud machines">

Once agents work for minutes at a time, developers want several at once: one updating the callers of `formatAmount`, another writing tests for the new currency logic, a third trying an alternative design. On one copy of the repository they'd overwrite each other's files and break each other's test runs.

Cursor 2.0 (October 2025) answered with the tool git already has. A **git worktree** is a second working directory attached to the same repository, checked out on its own branch, sharing the object store so creating one is quick. Cursor 2.0 could run "up to eight agents in parallel on a single prompt", each in its own worktree or on a remote machine, "to prevent file conflicts". Running several models on the same problem and keeping the best result, its post said, "significantly improves the final output". Cursor 3.0 (April 2026) added an agents window to manage them, and commands such as `/worktree` and `/best-of-n`.

For work that should continue while the laptop is closed, **cloud agents** (launched as background agents in a May 2025 preview, available to all users in June 2025) run in isolated virtual machines with a full development environment, cloned from the team's GitHub or GitLab, set up from a snapshot or a Dockerfile, and they push a branch when done. Those are the same machines that, multiplied by hundreds of thousands, became Composer's training environments.

| Isolation | Separate copies for separate actors | Hidden editor window; git worktrees; sandbox VMs |

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="13" id="breaks">What goes wrong, and what it cost</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="13.1" title="Failures this design has to survive">

| What happens | What the user sees | What the design does |
|---|---|---|
| Ananya types faster than suggestions arrive | Nothing, or a suggestion for text already changed | Stale requests are cancelled; only a suggestion for the current text is drawn |
| The Tab model is unsure | No suggestion | RL has taught it to stay quiet below about a 25% chance of acceptance |
| The apply model's rewrite diverges from the original | A wrong hunk in the diff | Greedy verification keeps output faithful to the model; the user reviews the diff |
| A vector database is breached | Some information about code leaks | Only vectors, encrypted paths and line ranges were stored; no plaintext |
| A newcomer borrows a teammate's index | Could see results for files they lack | Content proofs drop any result for a file they can't prove they have |
| The agent's edit doesn't type-check | Red squiggles | The shadow workspace shows the lints to the agent first |
| Two agents edit the same files | Conflicts, broken test runs | Each agent gets its own worktree or VM and branch |
| Heavy users outspend their plan | Surprise charges (June 2025) | Usage-based plans, an apology and refunds; cheaper own models |

</Sub>

<Sub n="13.2" title="The tradeoffs, in one table">

| Decision | Chosen | Given up | Why it was worth it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inline suggestions | Own small sparse model | Frontier-level reasoning | Hundreds of ms with a 13,000-token prompt, trained on edits |
| When to suggest | Online RL on accept/reject | A simple, explicit threshold knob | 21% fewer suggestions, 28% higher accept rate |
| Applying edits | Full rewrite with speculative edits | Fewest output tokens (diffs) | Reliable output, cost proportional to the change |
| Finding code (2023–2026) | Server-side embeddings plus grep | Simplicity, some privacy | 12.5% more accurate answers when models searched poorly |
| Finding code (2026 on) | Local regex index plus agentic grep | Search by meaning | Models search well now; nothing leaves the laptop |
| Sync | Merkle tree, every few minutes | Instant freshness | One hash compared when nothing changed |
| Checking edits | A hidden second window | Memory (up to 2×) | Real lints without touching the user's files |
| Agent model | Train Composer with RL | Training and sandbox costs | Speed, price and margins under Cursor's control |

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="14" id="summary">Summary</SectionHeading>

1. **An AI editor's hardest budget is the keystroke**: Tab reads about 13,000 tokens and writes a few dozen, 400 million times a day, in a few hundred milliseconds.
2. **Tab predicts edits and jumps, not the next word**, which needs models trained on edit sequences, so Cursor trains its own small sparse models.
3. **Consecutive keystrokes share almost the whole prompt**, so caching-aware prompt order, cache warming and requesting the next suggestion ahead turn reuse into speed; cancelling stale requests saves the rest.
4. **Knowing when to stay quiet is learned**: with rewards of 0.75 for accept and −0.25 for reject, a policy-gradient model learns to suggest only above a 25% chance, retrained every couple of hours.
5. **Rewriting the whole file is the most reliable edit format**, and speculative edits make it cheap by using the original file as the draft, so cost tracks the size of the change.
6. **Cursor's semantic index stored vectors, not code**: chunks embedded on servers, kept in Turbopuffer namespaces on object storage, and read back from the user's own disk by line range.
7. **A Merkle tree finds changed files in a handful of comparisons**, and a cache keyed by chunk hash means each piece of code is embedded once for everyone.
8. **Privacy shaped every detail**: per-segment deterministic path encryption, content proofs before sharing a teammate's index, and simhashes to find that index.
9. **In 2026 Cursor turned the semantic index off**, because better models with a fast local regex index (sparse n-grams over a git commit) found code as well, converging on Claude Code's choice.
10. **The shadow workspace checks AI edits in a hidden window**, giving the agent real compiler errors without touching the user's files.
11. **Owning the high-volume intelligence is the economic spine**: Tab from the start, the apply model in 2024, and Composer, trained by RL in hundreds of thousands of sandboxes, from 2025.

<SectionHeading n="15" id="build">Build this</SectionHeading>

**A small next-edit engine and an index that syncs.**

- Take any small open code model you can run locally. Write an editor plugin (or a script that replays a recorded editing session) that sends the file and cursor position after each keystroke. Measure time to first token with and without keeping the KV cache between requests, with the file at the start of the prompt and the cursor region at the end.
- Add cancellation: when a new keystroke arrives, abort the running generation. Log how much generation time is saved, and compare with a 100 ms debounce.
- Implement speculative edits: given an old file and an instruction, run greedy decoding but feed chunks of the old file as draft tokens and accept the longest matching prefix. Count forward passes for a three-line change in a 500-line file.
- Build a Merkle tree over a real repository, store it, make some edits and switch branches, and walk the trees to find the changed files. Count hashes compared against the number of files.

<SectionHeading n="16" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="Why can't an AI editor use the most capable model for inline completions?">
Inline suggestions must arrive between keystrokes, in a few hundred milliseconds, and each one reads a long prompt (around 13,000 tokens for Cursor's Tab) to write a short suggestion. Reading the prompt dominates the time, and it grows with model size, so a frontier model is far too slow and too expensive at hundreds of millions of requests a day. A small sparse model trained specifically on edits, with its KV cache reused between keystrokes, fits the budget.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="How does speculative decoding work when there's no draft model?">
When a model rewrites a file to make a small change, most of its output is identical to the original file. So use the original as the draft: feed a chunk of it, check the whole chunk in one forward pass with greedy decoding, keep the longest prefix that matches the model's own choices, and generate normally where they differ. Once the output matches the original again, re-align and resume checking in chunks. Cost becomes roughly proportional to the size of the change, and the output is exactly what the model would have written token by token.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="How can a server provide semantic search over code it never stores?">
Store only embeddings, line ranges and encrypted file paths. At query time, embed the query, find the nearest vectors, and return paths and line ranges to the client, which decrypts the paths and reads the text from its own disk before sending it with the model request. Encrypt each path segment deterministically with a client-held key so folder filtering still works, accepting that the folder structure leaks. Cursor ran this design until 2026, and noted that embeddings can still reveal something about the code.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Cursor built a large semantic code index and then turned it off. Was the index a mistake?">
No. In 2023 to 2025, models used search tools poorly, and semantic search measurably improved answers (12.5% on Cursor's tests). The index cost a lot: Merkle sync, embedding pipelines, a vector database with millions of namespaces, path encryption and content proofs. As models became good at running many precise searches, the marginal benefit shrank while the cost and privacy exposure stayed, and a fast local regex index made agentic grep quick even in monorepos. A good lesson: tie each component to the limitation it compensates for, and to remove it when that limitation goes away.
</QA>

<SectionHeading n="17" id="resources">Go deeper</SectionHeading>

<Quiz>
  <Q q="Tab's rewards are changed to +0.5 for an accepted suggestion and −0.5 for a rejected one. Above what acceptance chance will the model learn to show suggestions?">
    50%. Showing earns 0.5p − 0.5(1 − p) = p − 0.5 on average, which is
    positive only when p is above 0.5. The model would suggest less often, and
    be right more often when it did.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Ananya switches to a branch that touches 3,000 of her 40,000 files. Does the Merkle tree still help?">
    Less. The walk has to descend into every folder that contains a changed
    file, so it compares many more hashes, though still fewer than 40,000 if
    the changes are concentrated in a few folders. The upload of 3,000 files is
    the larger cost, and the chunk-hash cache helps there: chunks that exist on
    other branches have been embedded before.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Why does speculative editing use greedy decoding?">
    With greedy decoding the model's output is fully determined, so checking a
    draft token is a plain comparison with the model's top choice, and the
    accepted output is exactly what token-by-token decoding would produce. With
    sampling, verification needs a probabilistic acceptance rule, as in
    chapter 54's speculative decoding.
  </Q>
</Quiz>
