Most of what goes wrong in production isn't one component breaking. It's a
slow dependency, a burst of traffic or a bad deploy, and then the rest of the
system reacting to it badly: clients retry, queues fill, threads block, and a
problem in one service becomes an outage in ten.

Reliability engineering is the discipline of making that reaction good. It has
two halves. The first is arithmetic: deciding how reliable a service needs to
be, measuring it, and alerting when you're spending reliability faster than you
can afford. The second is mechanism: the timeouts, retries, circuit breakers
and admission controls that decide what a service does when something it
depends on stops keeping up.

This chapter covers both. It starts with SLOs and the nines, builds error
budgets and burn-rate alerts from them, then works through each defence with
the defaults of real implementations (Envoy, gRPC, Resilience4j) and two
simulations that show what happens without them.

<SectionHeading n="01" id="slos">Putting a number on reliability</SectionHeading>

"The service should be reliable" can't be tested, alerted on or traded off.
So the first job is to turn it into a number.

<Sub n="1.1" title="SLI, SLO, SLA">

Three terms, often mixed up, each built on the one before:

| Term | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **SLI** (indicator) | A measurement of good events over valid events | Share of HTTP requests that returned non-5xx in under 300 ms |
| **SLO** (objective) | A target for the SLI over a window | 99.9% of requests good, over a rolling 30 days |
| **SLA** (agreement) | A contract with consequences if the SLO is missed | Service credits below 99.5% in a calendar month |

<Why>Why measure requests instead of uptime?</Why>

Because "the server was up" isn't what users experience. A service can answer
health checks all day while 5% of real requests time out. An SLI counted from
real requests, ideally at the load balancer or the client, sees what users
see.

A good SLI is a ratio of events, not an average. "p99 latency under 300 ms" is
hard to budget against. "99% of requests completed in under 300 ms" is a count
of good events you can add up across servers and over time.

<Callout label="Design implication">
Set the SLA looser than the SLO, and the SLO looser than what you actually
achieve. The gap between them is your warning time: you want to know you're
missing the internal target long before a contract notices.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="1.2" title="What each nine costs">

Availability targets are quoted in nines. Each extra nine cuts the allowed
failure by ten. Here's what they mean in minutes:

| SLO | Bad time per year | Per 30 days | Per week |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99% | 3.65 days | 7.2 hours | 1.68 hours |
| 99.9% | 8.77 hours | 43.2 minutes | 10.1 minutes |
| 99.95% | 4.38 hours | 21.6 minutes | 5.04 minutes |
| 99.99% | 52.6 minutes | 4.32 minutes | 1.01 minutes |
| 99.999% | 5.26 minutes | 25.9 seconds | 6.05 seconds |

<Why>Why don't teams just aim for five nines?</Why>

Because of what the last row implies about people. Twenty-six seconds a month
is less time than it takes a human to read a page. At four or five nines,
detection and recovery have to be automatic, rollouts have to be gradual
enough that a bad one hurts a sliver of traffic, and every dependency has to
be better still. Each nine is a different engineering organisation, not a
tweak.

</Sub>

<Sub n="1.3" title="Dependencies multiply">

If your service needs five other services on every request, and each fails
independently, your availability is at most the product of theirs:

<Estimate
  rows={[
    { label: "One dependency at 99.9%", math: "0.999", value: "99.9%" },
    { label: "Five in series", math: "0.999⁵", value: "99.50%" },
    { label: "Ten in series", math: "0.999¹⁰", value: "99.00%" },
    { label: "Two replicas at 99%, either will do", math: "1 − 0.01²", value: "99.99%" },
  ]}
  result={{ label: "ten 99.9% dependencies leave you at", value: "two nines" }}
/>

Serial dependencies multiply their failure rates in. Redundant replicas
multiply them *out*, but only if failures really are independent. Two replicas
behind the same bad config push, or in the same zone during a power event, are
one replica.

Google's rule of thumb, from *[The Calculus of Service Availability](https://cacm.acm.org/practice/the-calculus-of-service-availability/)*
(Treynor et al., 2017), is the **rule of the extra 9**: a critical dependency
should offer one more nine than the service that depends on it. When it can't,
you have to change the dependency from critical to something you can survive
without, with a cache, a fallback or graceful degradation.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Publishing an SLO tighter than the product of your critical dependencies.
If your database is 99.95% and your auth service is 99.9%, a 99.99% promise
is arithmetic you can't meet, however good your own code is.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="02" id="error-budgets">Error budgets and burn rates</SectionHeading>

An SLO of 99.9% says something more useful than "be reliable". It says 0.1% of
requests are *allowed* to fail. That allowance is the **error budget**.

<Sub n="2.1" title="Spending the budget">

Over 30 days, a 99.9% SLO on a service taking 1,000 requests a second allows
about 2.6 million bad requests. That's the budget, and it's meant to be spent:
on deploys, experiments, migrations and the occasional bad day.

<Why>Why is a budget better than "as reliable as possible"?</Why>

Because it turns an argument into a measurement. When the budget is healthy,
the team ships faster. When it's nearly gone, the team slows releases and
spends time on reliability. Nobody has to win a debate about whether the
service is "reliable enough"; the counter says.

A budget also caps how reliable you should be. A service at 99.99% against a
99.9% SLO is leaving budget unspent, which usually means it's shipping more
slowly than it could.

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.2" title="Burn rate">

The **burn rate** is how fast you're spending the budget relative to the rate
that would use it up exactly at the end of the window. A burn rate of 1 spends
a 30-day budget in 30 days. A burn rate of 10 spends it in 3.

For a 99.9% SLO, burn rate is just the error rate divided by 0.1%:

| Error rate | Burn rate | Budget gone in |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1% | 1 | 30 days |
| 0.6% | 6 | 5 days |
| 1.44% | 14.4 | 50 hours |
| 10% | 100 | 7.2 hours |
| 100% (full outage) | 1,000 | 43 minutes |

<Predict
  q="Your service has a 99.9% monthly SLO. A bad deploy starts failing 1.44% of requests. What share of the month's error budget is gone after one hour?"
  options={[
    "About 0.1%",

>
Burn rate is 1.44% ÷ 0.1% = 14.4. One hour is 1/720 of 30 days, so the hour
consumes 14.4 ÷ 720 = 2% of the budget. That's the number the Google SRE
workbook uses for its fastest page: 2% of a month's budget in one hour is
enough to wake someone for, even though 98.56% of requests are still fine.
</Predict>

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.3" title="Multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts">

The [SRE workbook's chapter on alerting on SLOs](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)
walks through several alerting schemes and ends on this one. Each alert fires
only if the burn rate is high over both a long window and a short one:

| Severity | Long window | Short window | Burn rate | Budget consumed when it fires |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Page | 1 hour | 5 minutes | 14.4 | 2% |
| Page | 6 hours | 30 minutes | 6 | 5% |
| Ticket | 3 days | 6 hours | 1 | 10% |

Each budget figure comes from one formula: burn rate × window ÷ SLO period. For
the first row, 14.4 × 1 h ÷ 720 h = 2%.

<Why>Why two windows per alert?</Why>

The long window stops the alert firing on a blip: 14.4× burn over a whole hour
is real. The short window, a twelfth of the long one by the workbook's
guideline, makes it *stop* firing soon after the problem is fixed, instead of
paging for the rest of the hour while old errors age out of the long window.


<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Alerting on raw error rate with a fixed threshold like "5xx above 1% for five
minutes". It pages at 3 a.m. for a blip that cost 0.03% of the budget, and
stays quiet for a slow leak that eats half of it. Alert on how fast the budget
is going, not on how the graph looks.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="03" id="timeouts">Timeouts and deadlines</SectionHeading>

Every defence in the rest of this chapter starts with a timeout. Without one, a
slow dependency doesn't fail; it holds your thread, your connection and your
memory until it answers, and it may never answer.

<Sub n="3.1" title="Every call needs one">

A call with no timeout turns a dependency's latency into your resource usage.
By Little's Law (covered in [chapter 16](/topics/16-contention-tail-latency)),
requests in flight = arrival rate × time each one takes. At 500 requests a
second, a dependency that goes from 20 ms to 10 seconds takes your in-flight
count from 10 to 5,000. If your pool has 200 threads, you're down.

<Why>Why not just set every timeout to something generous, like 30 seconds?</Why>

Because a generous timeout is almost no timeout. It lets a stuck dependency
hold resources for 30 seconds each, which is exactly the pile-up above.
A timeout should be close to the latency a healthy dependency actually has.

A workable rule, roughly: start from the dependency's p99.9 under normal load, add a
margin, and check what share of calls would have timed out last week. If a
healthy dependency trips your timeout often, it's too tight; if a stuck one
can hold a thread for many times its normal latency, it's too loose.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.2" title="Deadlines, not timeouts">

In a call tree, independent timeouts at each hop don't add up to anything
sensible. The frontend gives up after 1 second, but the backend it called is
still waiting 2 seconds on the database, doing work nobody will read.

The fix is a **deadline**: an absolute time by which the whole request must be
done, passed down with each call. The
[SRE book's chapter on cascading failures](https://sre.google/sre-book/addressing-cascading-failures/)
gives the arithmetic: if server A picks a 30-second deadline and spends 7
seconds before calling B, "the RPC from A to B will have a 23-second deadline."

gRPC does this for you: a client deadline travels in the `grpc-timeout` header,
and a server that makes its own calls with the incoming context passes on
what's left. Over plain HTTP you have to carry it yourself, in a header, and
check it before starting expensive work.

<Callout label="Design implication">
Check the deadline before doing work, not only while waiting. A request that
sat in your queue past its deadline should be dropped when it's dequeued,
without touching the database. Section 6's simulation shows that single check
turning a permanent outage into a short dip.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="04" id="retries">Retries, backoff and jitter</SectionHeading>

A retry is the cheapest reliability feature there is. It hides a transient
failure (a dropped packet, a restarting pod, a leader election) at the cost of
one more request. It's also the most common way a small problem becomes a big
one.

<Sub n="4.1" title="What's safe to retry">

Before *how* to retry comes *whether*. A retry is only safe if doing the
operation twice is the same as doing it once.

| Operation | Safe to retry? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `GET`, reads | Yes | No side effects |
| `PUT` of a full value, `DELETE` | Yes, by design | Idempotent: the second call leaves the same state |
| `POST` that creates or charges | Only with an idempotency key | The first call may have succeeded before the response was lost |
| Call that timed out | Only if idempotent | A timeout means "unknown", not "failed" |

For the third row, the standard fix is an **idempotency key**: the client
generates a unique ID per logical operation and sends it on every attempt.
Stripe's [design](https://stripe.com/blog/idempotency) (Brandur Leach, 2017)
has clients send it in an `Idempotency-Key` header; when a retry arrives for a
key that already succeeded, "the server simply replies with a cached result of
the successful operation".

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.2" title="Retries multiply">

Here's the problem with retries in a layered system. Each layer that retries
multiplies the load on everything below it.

These numbers are the SRE book's own example: with backend, frontend and
JavaScript layers each issuing 3 retries, "a single user action may create 64
attempts (4^3) on the database." Its advice is to "avoid amplifying retries by
issuing retries at multiple levels."

<Why>Why not just retry at the top?</Why>

Retrying at the top is safest for load, but it's a bit slow: the whole call tree is
redone for a failure in one leaf. The usual compromise is to retry at exactly
one layer, the one closest to the failure that knows the operation is
idempotent, and to have every other layer pass errors straight up.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.3" title="Backoff, and why it needs jitter">

A client that retries immediately adds load at the worst moment. So retries
back off: wait, and wait longer after each failure. Marc Brooker's
[Exponential Backoff And Jitter](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/exponential-backoff-and-jitter/)
(AWS Architecture Blog, 2015) is the standard reference, and it defines the
variants:

| Scheme | Sleep before attempt n | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Exponential | `min(cap, base × 2^n)` | Spreads retries out in time, but every client waits the same amount |
| Full jitter | `random(0, min(cap, base × 2^n))` | Same ceiling, uniformly spread below it |
| Equal jitter | half the exponential value, plus `random` over the other half | Never retries very early |
| Decorrelated jitter | `min(cap, random(base, previous × 3))` | Each client's delay depends on its own last delay |

<Why>Why isn't plain exponential backoff enough?</Why>

Because clients that failed together retry together. If a thousand clients hit
an error at the same instant, exponential backoff moves the whole thousand to
1 second later, then 2, then 4. The spike isn't spread, it's postponed, and
each wave meets the same capacity limit as the first.

A simulation makes that concrete. A thousand clients send at once to a
server that accepts 10 requests per millisecond and rejects the rest. Rejected
clients back off with base 1 ms and cap 1 second, and retry until they
succeed:

<TryIt what="1,000 simultaneous clients, a server taking 10 per ms, six retry policies" lang="python" toolchain="Python 3.14.6, Apple M4; median of 5 seeds">
```python
for c, attempt, last in arrivals_this_ms:     # shuffled
    if accepted < CAP: accepted += 1; continue # CAP = 10 per ms
    exp = min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt)        # base 1 ms, cap 1000 ms
    sleep = {
        "none":   base,
        "exp":    exp,
        "grpc":   exp * uniform(0.8, 1.2),
        "equal":  exp / 2 + uniform(0, exp / 2),
        "full":   uniform(0, exp),
        "decorr": min(cap, uniform(base, last * 3)),
    }[policy]
    schedule(c, attempt + 1, at=now + max(1, round(sleep)))
```

<Output>
```text
policy   total calls  all done after
none          50,500          99 ms
exp           50,500      90,023 ms
grpc           7,410         531 ms
equal          7,029         230 ms
full           7,226         311 ms
decorr         7,341         189 ms
```
</Output>
</TryIt>

The ideal is 1,000 calls finishing at 100 ms, the server's capacity. Retrying
every millisecond with no backoff finishes on time but sends **50 times** the
necessary calls. Unjittered exponential backoff is the worst of both: the
same 50,500 calls, and the last client waits **90 seconds**, because all
thousand stay synchronised and keep colliding. Every jittered variant cuts the
work to about 7,000 calls and finishes in under a second.

Brooker's own simulation, of optimistic-concurrency clients, reaches the same
verdict: jittered backoff "should be considered a standard approach for
remote clients," with full jitter using the least work and decorrelated jitter
finishing slightly sooner.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Adding a tiny jitter to a big exponential. gRPC's retry policy in
[gRFC A6](https://github.com/grpc/proposal/blob/6342be729b96478a2897ceb208a8cddcd832a17b/A6-client-retries.md)
now multiplies each backoff by `random(0.8, 1.2)`, which in the simulation took
the last client from 90 seconds to half a second. Full jitter did better
still. If your library's jitter is a few percent, it's barely jitter.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.4" title="Retry budgets">

Backoff controls *when* a client retries. It doesn't limit *how much* retrying
a fleet of clients does in total, and under a real overload that total is what
matters. A **retry budget** caps retries as a share of normal traffic.

Three real designs:

| System | Rule | Default |
|---|---|---|
| Google (SRE book, [Handling Overload](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/)) | Up to 3 attempts per request, and retry only while retries are under 10% of a client's requests | 10% per client |
| Envoy `retry_budget` | Active retries ≤ a percentage of active plus pending requests | 20%, minimum 3 concurrent |
| gRPC retry throttling | Token bucket: failures cost 1, successes earn `tokenRatio`; no retries at or below half full | Set per service config |

gRPC's version is small enough to read whole:

<SourceRead
  file="clientconn.go"
  repo="grpc/grpc-go"
  tag="v1.66.0"
  href="https://github.com/grpc/grpc-go/blob/v1.66.0/clientconn.go#L1586"
>
```go
type retryThrottler struct {
	max    float64
	thresh float64   // set to max / 2 from the service config
	ratio  float64

	mu     sync.Mutex
	tokens float64
}

// throttle subtracts a retry token from the pool and returns whether a retry
// should be throttled (disallowed) based upon the retry throttling policy in
// the service config.
func (rt *retryThrottler) throttle() bool {
	/* ... nil check ... */
	rt.mu.Lock()
	defer rt.mu.Unlock()
	rt.tokens--
	if rt.tokens < 0 {
		rt.tokens = 0
	}
	return rt.tokens <= rt.thresh
}

func (rt *retryThrottler) successfulRPC() {
	/* ... nil check ... */
	rt.mu.Lock()
	defer rt.mu.Unlock()
	rt.tokens += rt.ratio
	if rt.tokens > rt.max {
		rt.tokens = rt.max
	}
}
```
</SourceRead>

<Why>Why does a budget beat a per-request retry limit?</Why>

Because a per-request limit of 3 still allows 4× load when *everything* is
failing, which is exactly when the backend can least afford it. A budget
scales retries with success: when most calls succeed, the odd failure gets
retried; when most fail, the tokens drain and retries stop, and the backend
sees roughly its normal load.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="05" id="circuit-breakers">Circuit breakers</SectionHeading>

A retry budget stops you making a failing dependency worse. A **circuit
breaker** goes further: when a dependency is clearly failing, it stops calling
it at all for a while and fails fast instead.

<Sub n="5.1" title="Three states">

This pattern comes from Michael Nygard's *Release It!* and Martin Fowler's
[CircuitBreaker](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/CircuitBreaker.html) write-up.
Resilience4j, the common JVM implementation, has the canonical three states
plus some special ones, and its
[documented defaults](https://resilience4j.readme.io/docs/circuitbreaker) make
the state machine concrete:

    [0, "If the trial calls' failure rate is under the threshold, the breaker closes and traffic resumes. If not, it goes back to OPEN for another wait."],
  ]}
/>

<Why>Why fail fast instead of waiting for the timeout?</Why>

Two reasons. For you, a failed call that returns in microseconds frees the
thread, where a call that waits out a 2-second timeout holds it. For the
dependency, zero traffic is often what it needs to recover: an overloaded
database with its queue drained can come back, one still receiving retries
can't.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.2" title="Envoy's circuit breakers are concurrency limits">

Envoy uses the same name for something different. Its "circuit breakers" are
per-cluster caps on concurrency, and they trip per request, not per state
machine:

</SourceRead>

When a limit is hit, Envoy fails the request at once, increments a counter
such as `upstream_rq_pending_overflow` or `upstream_rq_retry_overflow`, and (for
`max_requests`) sets the `x-envoy-overloaded` header, per its
[circuit breaking docs](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/upstream/circuit_breaking).

Envoy puts the state-machine behaviour in a separate feature, **outlier
detection**, which ejects individual hosts, not the whole cluster. Its
defaults in the same release: eject a host after **5 consecutive 5xx**, sweep
every **10 s**, eject for **30 s** multiplied by the number of times it's been
ejected, and never eject more than **10%** of the cluster
([`outlier_detection.proto`](https://github.com/envoyproxy/envoy/blob/v1.31.0/api/envoy/config/cluster/v3/outlier_detection.proto)).

| | Resilience4j breaker | Envoy circuit breakers | Envoy outlier detection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit | One dependency | One upstream cluster | One host in a cluster |
| Trips on | Failure or slow-call rate | Concurrency above a cap | Consecutive errors, or success-rate outliers |
| While tripped | All calls fail fast | Only calls over the cap fail | That host gets no traffic |
| Recovers | Half-open probes | As soon as load drops | After the ejection time |

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Leaving Envoy's defaults on a high-traffic cluster. 1,024 concurrent requests
is probably plenty for a small service and too few for a big one: a service doing 20,000
requests a second at 60 ms has about 1,200 in flight (Little's Law) and will
see `pending_overflow` 503s on a normal day. Size the caps from your own
arrival rate × latency, with headroom.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.3" title="What to do when the breaker is open">

A breaker that opens and returns an error has only made the failure faster.
What the caller does instead is the real design question:

| Fallback | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Serve stale data from a cache | Profiles, catalogues, config | How stale is acceptable, and does anyone know it's stale |
| Degrade the feature | Recommendations, ratings, non-essential widgets | Hiding a failure nobody notices for weeks |
| Fail open | Rate limiters, feature-flag lookups | Security checks must never fail open |
| Queue for later | Writes that can be delayed: emails, analytics | The queue needs its own limits |
| Return an error | Payments, anything that must be correct | This is the honest default for critical paths |

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" id="load-shedding">Overload and load shedding</SectionHeading>

Retries, timeouts and breakers protect a *client* from a bad dependency. Load
shedding protects a *server* from its clients. When more work arrives than the
server can do, something has to be refused. The only choice is whether you
refuse it deliberately and cheaply, or accidentally and expensively.

<Sub n="6.1" title="Goodput collapses before throughput does">

An overloaded server with an unbounded queue keeps its CPU at 100% and still
serves nobody. Every request waits in line longer than the client is willing
to wait, so by the time it's processed the client has timed out, and the work
is thrown away.

The measure that matters is **goodput**: requests completed within the
client's deadline. Here's a simulation that shows the difference. One server
handles 100 requests a second (exponential service time, mean 10 ms). Normal
load is 80 a second. From t=60 s to t=90 s a burst pushes it to 150 a second,
then it returns to 80. Clients give up after 1 second.

<TryIt what="A 30-second overload, four queue policies, with and without 3 retries on timeout" lang="python" toolchain="Python 3.14.6, Apple M4; discrete-event simulation, median of 3 seeds">
```python
def start(t):                       # called whenever the server is free
    while q:
        if policy == "lifo" and t - last_empty > 0.1:
            req = q.pop()           # queue busy for 100 ms: newest first
        else:
            req = q.popleft()
        if policy != "fifo" and t > req.deadline:
            continue                # expired: drop it without doing the work
        serve(req, t)               # takes expovariate(1 / 0.010) seconds
        return
# "bounded" also rejects on arrival when 50 requests are already queued.
# Retrying clients resend immediately when their 1 s timeout fires, up to 3 times.
```

<Output>
```text
goodput, requests/s        0-30  30-60 60-90 90-120 120-150 150-180 ... 270-300
                                        burst
fifo      no retries          80    81    11     0      0      76  ...    79
fifo      3 retries           80    81    11     0      0       0  ...     0
deadline  no retries          80    81    73    82     79      78  ...    79
deadline  3 retries           80    81    31    48     79      82  ...    79
bounded   3 retries           80    81   101    80     79      79  ...    80
lifo      3 retries           80    81   101    84     79      78  ...    79

fifo, 3 retries: offered 535/s during the burst, then ~320/s for the rest of
the run; ~100/s of completed work wasted every window after t=60
```
</Output>
</TryIt>

Read the table row by row:

- **Plain FIFO** keeps the server 100% busy through the burst and serves almost
  nobody for over a minute. The burst leaves about 1,500 requests queued (50 a
  second over capacity, for 30 seconds), and they drain at only 20 a second of
  spare capacity: 75 seconds.
- **FIFO with retries never recovers.** Every timed-out request comes back as a
  retry, so offered load settles around 320 a second against a capacity of 100,
  long after the burst has gone. Goodput is zero for the remaining 210 seconds.
- **One deadline check** before starting each request fixes most of it. The
  server stops working on requests nobody is waiting for.
- **A bounded queue or adaptive LIFO** keeps goodput at the server's full
  capacity during the burst. Rejecting early is cheaper than timing out late.

</Sub>

<Sub n="6.2" title="Metastable failure">

That second row has a name. Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu's
[Metastable Failures in Distributed Systems](https://sigops.org/s/conferences/hotos/2021/papers/hotos21-s11-bronson.pdf)
(HotOS 2021) describes failures where "a trigger causes the system to enter a
bad state that persists even when the trigger is removed," held there by "a
sustaining effect—often involving work amplification". Leaving it "requires a
strong corrective push, such as rebooting the system or dramatically reducing
the load."

<Steps
  title="The life of a metastable failure, from the simulation"
  lanes={[
    { t: "Stable", s: "80% busy", icon: "◉" },
    { t: "Vulnerable", s: "retries configured", icon: "◌" },
    { t: "Trigger", s: "30 s burst", icon: "⚡" },
    { t: "Sustained", s: "retries > capacity", icon: "↻" },
    { t: "Push", s: "shed or restart", icon: "⏏" },
  ]}
  steps={[
    [0, "The server runs at 80 requests a second against a capacity of 100. Latency is fine and every request succeeds."],
    [1, "Nothing looks wrong, but the system is **vulnerable**: clients retry up to 3 times on timeout, and the queue is FIFO with no deadline check. The same load has two stable states."],
    [2, "A 30-second burst at 150 a second pushes queueing delay past the 1-second client timeout. Requests start timing out."],
    [3, "Each timeout produces a retry, so offered load climbs to over 300 a second. The burst ends, but **the retries alone exceed capacity**. The server spends all its time on requests whose clients have already given up."],
    [3, "This is the **sustaining effect**. In the simulation, goodput stays at zero for the remaining 210 seconds, while offered load from real users is back to a comfortable 80."],
    [4, "Only a strong push gets out: shed load until the queue drains, stop the retries, or restart with an empty queue. Any one of the fixes in section 6.3, in place **before** the trigger, would have prevented it."],
  ]}
/>

The AWS us-east-1 outage of 7 December 2021 had this shape. Per AWS's
[summary](https://aws.amazon.com/message/12721/), an automated scaling activity
"triggered an unexpected behavior from a large number of clients," the surge
overwhelmed networking devices between two internal networks, and the delays
caused "even more connection attempts and retries," leading to "persistent
congestion." The clients did have well-tested back-off behaviour, but "a latent issue
prevented these clients from adequately backing off during this event."
Recovery took about seven hours.

<Callout label="Design implication">
You can't test for metastability at normal load, because the healthy state is
stable there. Test the recovery: in a load test, push the service well past
capacity for a minute, drop back to normal load, and check that it comes back
by itself. If it doesn't, you've found a sustaining effect before your users
did.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="6.3" title="Shed early, shed cheaply">

A request is cheapest to refuse at the first point it can be refused. Here's
where the layers are, from the client inwards:

<Steps
  title="Where an overloaded request can be refused, cheapest first"
  lanes={[
    { t: "Client", s: "adaptive throttle", icon: "◉" },
    { t: "Proxy", s: "Envoy limits", icon: "⇆" },
    { t: "Admission", s: "concurrency limit", icon: "▣" },
    { t: "Queue", s: "CoDel · LIFO", icon: "≡" },
    { t: "Handler", s: "deadline check", icon: "⚙" },
  ]}
  steps={[
    [0, "**Client-side throttling.** The client tracks its own requests and the backend's accepts, and starts rejecting locally once requests exceed about **2 × accepts**. The backend never sees this traffic."],
    [1, "**The proxy.** Envoy's `max_pending_requests` and `max_requests` fail requests in microseconds when the cluster is saturated, with `x-envoy-overloaded` on the response."],
    [2, "**Admission control.** The server itself caps concurrent requests. Past the limit it returns 503 or `RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED` immediately, ideally shedding low-priority traffic first."],
    [3, "**The queue.** If a request must wait, bound the wait. A CoDel-style rule drops requests that have queued too long; adaptive LIFO serves the newest first when a queue has formed."],
    [4, "**The handler.** Last line: check the propagated deadline before doing expensive work, and abandon work whose client has gone. Anything refused here has already cost a queue slot and a dequeue."],
  ]}
/>

Client-side throttling comes from Google's
[Handling Overload](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/). Each
client keeps two counts over the last couple of minutes, `requests` (attempted)
and `accepts` (accepted by the backend), and rejects a new request locally with
probability

```
max(0, (requests − K × accepts) / (requests + 1))
```

Google says it "generally prefer[s] the 2x multiplier" for K. When the
backend accepts everything, the probability is zero. When it starts rejecting,
clients cut their own traffic so the backend spends less time saying no.

Both queue rules come from Ben Maurer's
[Fail at Scale](https://cacm.acm.org/practice/fail-at-scale/) (ACM Queue,
2015), on how Facebook runs its services. Its CoDel variant: if the queue
hasn't been empty in the last N milliseconds, limit time in queue to M
milliseconds; Facebook found M = 5 ms and N = 100 ms "tends to work well across
a wide set of use cases." Adaptive LIFO serves FIFO normally and switches to
newest-first once a queue forms, because the oldest request's user has probably
given up. In the simulation, the `lifo` policy uses the same 100 ms rule.

<Why>Why LIFO? Isn't it unfair?</Why>

It's unfair to the requests at the back of a queue that's already too long,
but they were probably going to time out anyway. Under FIFO everyone waits
and most fail; under LIFO the newest requests, whose clients are still
waiting, succeed. Fairness in a queue that's overflowing means spreading
failure evenly, and nobody benefits from that.

</Sub>

<Sub n="6.4" title="Not all traffic is equal">

Shedding blindly drops the checkout request as readily as the prefetch.
Google's services tag every request with a criticality, one of
`CRITICAL_PLUS`, `CRITICAL`, `SHEDDABLE_PLUS` and `SHEDDABLE`
([Handling Overload](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/)), and shed
from the bottom up. Even two classes help:

| Class | Examples | Under overload |
|---|---|---|
| User-facing, critical | Login, checkout, the page itself | Shed last |
| User-facing, optional | Recommendations, counts, previews | Shed early; degrade the UI |
| Batch and background | Reindexing, reports, prefetch | Shed first; retry later |
| Retries | Any retried request | Shed before first attempts of the same class |

<Callout label="Design implication">
Propagate priority the same way you propagate the deadline. A background job
that calls an API that calls a database should arrive at the database marked
as background, or the database can't tell it from a user waiting on a page.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="07" id="apply">Apply it to your service</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="7.1" title="A checklist, in order">

1. **Write down the SLI and SLO** for your most important user journey, counted
   at the load balancer. Check it against the product of your critical
   dependencies (section 1.3).
2. **Add the two paging burn-rate alerts** (14.4× over 1 h and 5 m; 6× over 6 h
   and 30 m) and delete the fixed-threshold error alert.
3. **Put a timeout on every outbound call**, based on the dependency's p99.9,
   and propagate a deadline where your framework supports it.
4. **Retry at one layer**, with full or decorrelated jitter, a cap, and a
   budget. Make every retried write idempotent.
5. **Bound every queue**, and check the deadline when you dequeue.
6. **Size proxy concurrency limits** from arrival rate × latency, not defaults.
7. **Run the recovery test** from section 6.2: overload for a minute, drop back,
   and confirm the service recovers without help.

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.2" title="Symptom, cause, fix">

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stays down after the trigger is gone; CPU pinned, goodput near zero | Metastable: retries or queued expired work sustain the overload | Shed load or drain the queue; then add retry budgets and deadline checks |
| One slow dependency exhausts all threads | Missing or overlong timeout | Timeout from p99.9, bulkhead the pool per dependency |
| Database load spikes to many times normal during an incident | Retries at several layers | Retry at one layer, add a retry budget |
| Clients all retry in waves | Backoff without jitter | Full or decorrelated jitter |
| 503s with `x-envoy-overloaded` on a normal day | Envoy circuit-breaker defaults too low | Size `max_requests` and `max_pending_requests` from Little's Law |
| Duplicate charges or orders after timeouts | Non-idempotent write retried | Idempotency keys |
| Pages at night for blips; slow leaks never page | Fixed-threshold error alerts | Multiwindow burn-rate alerts |
| Breaker flaps open and closed | Window too small, or half-open lets in too much traffic | Larger window and minimum calls; fewer half-open probes |

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="08" id="summary">Summary</SectionHeading>

1. **An SLO is a count of good events over a window.** Measure it where users
   feel it, and set the SLA looser than the SLO.
2. **Each nine is ten times harder.** 99.99% leaves 4.3 minutes a month, which
   has to be handled by automation, not people.
3. **Serial dependencies multiply.** Ten dependencies at 99.9% cap you near
   99%; critical ones need an extra nine or a fallback.
4. **The error budget is meant to be spent.** It decides when to ship and when
   to fix, without an argument.
5. **Alert on burn rate over two windows.** 14.4× over an hour spends 2% of a
   month's budget and deserves a page; a fixed error threshold doesn't.
6. **Every call needs a timeout, and call trees need a deadline.** Check the
   deadline before doing work, not only while waiting.
7. **Retries multiply across layers.** Four attempts at three layers is 64
   attempts at the bottom. Retry at one layer, within a budget.
8. **Backoff without jitter postpones the spike.** In the simulation it took
   the last client 90 seconds; full jitter took 0.3.
9. **Circuit breakers fail fast so the dependency can recover.** Envoy's
   "circuit breakers" are concurrency caps; its outlier detection is the state
   machine.
10. **Overload is survived by refusing work early.** A bounded queue, a
    deadline check or adaptive LIFO kept goodput at capacity where plain FIFO
    with retries stayed at zero.

<SectionHeading n="09" id="build">Build this</SectionHeading>

**A metastability test harness.**

- Write a small HTTP service with a fixed capacity (a worker pool of 4 and a
  handler that sleeps 10 ms) and a load generator that sends at a fixed rate,
  times out at 1 second and optionally retries.
- Reproduce section 6.1: normal load at 80% of capacity, a 30-second burst at
  150%, then back to 80%. Plot goodput per second. Confirm that FIFO plus
  retries never recovers.
- Add the fixes one at a time: a deadline check at dequeue, a bounded queue,
  adaptive LIFO, a client retry budget. Measure how long each takes to recover.
- Put Envoy in front with default circuit breakers, then with limits sized from
  Little's Law, and compare the overflow counters.

<SectionHeading n="10" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="What's the difference between an SLI, an SLO and an SLA?">
The SLI is the measurement: good events over valid events, such as the share of
requests that succeed in under 300 ms. The SLO is the internal target for it
over a window, such as 99.9% over 30 days. The SLA is an external contract,
usually looser than the SLO, with penalties if it's missed.
</QA>

<QA level="beginner" q="Why add jitter to exponential backoff?">
Because clients that fail together retry together. Without jitter, a thousand
clients that failed at the same moment all retry at 1 s, then 2 s, then 4 s,
and each wave hits the same limit. Jitter spreads them out. In a simulation
of 1,000 clients against a server taking 10 per millisecond, unjittered
backoff took 90 seconds to serve everyone; full jitter took 0.3 seconds with a
seventh of the calls.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="What is a burn rate, and why alert on it?">
The ratio of your current error rate to the rate that would use up the error
budget exactly at the end of the SLO window. For a 99.9% SLO, a 1.44% error rate
is a burn rate of 14.4, which spends 2% of a 30-day budget in an hour. Alerting
on burn rate ties the page to the thing you care about, how fast you're using
up reliability, instead of to an arbitrary error threshold.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="A request goes through browser, frontend, backend and database, and each of the first three retries 3 times. What's the worst case at the database?">
Each layer makes up to 4 attempts, and the attempts multiply: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64
attempts for one user action. That's the SRE book's own example. The fix is to
retry at only one layer and cap retries with a budget, so a failing database
sees close to its normal load instead of 64 times it.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="What does a circuit breaker do in each of its states?">
Closed: calls pass, and outcomes are recorded in a sliding window. When the
failure rate crosses a threshold (50% by Resilience4j's default, after at least
100 calls), it opens. Open: calls fail immediately without reaching the
dependency, for a wait period (60 s by default). Half-open: a small number of
trial calls (10) are allowed; if they succeed it closes, otherwise it reopens.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="What's a metastable failure, and how do you prevent one?">
A failure where a temporary trigger pushes the system into a bad state that
persists after the trigger is gone, because of a sustaining effect such as
retries or work on already-expired requests. Load that the system handled
before the trigger is now more than it can handle. Prevention is removing the
sustaining effect in advance: retry budgets, jitter, deadline checks at
dequeue, bounded queues and load shedding. And test recovery by overloading
and then returning to normal load.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Why would a server process its queue LIFO under load?">
Once a queue has formed, the oldest requests have waited longest, and their
clients have probably timed out. Serving them first spends capacity on work
nobody will read, so everyone fails. Serving newest first gives recent
requests, whose clients are still waiting, a good chance of success. Facebook
uses adaptive LIFO (FIFO normally, LIFO when a queue forms) together with a
CoDel-style bound on queueing time.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Envoy returns 503s with x-envoy-overloaded on a normal day. What's happening?">
A cluster circuit breaker is overflowing, most likely `max_requests` or
`max_pending_requests`, both 1,024 by default. By Little's Law a service at
20,000 requests a second and 60 ms latency has about 1,200 requests in flight,
so the default is simply too small. Check the `upstream_rq_pending_overflow`
and `upstream_rq_active_overflow` counters, then size the limits from arrival
rate × latency with headroom.
</QA>

<Quiz>
  <Q q="Your SLO is 99.95% per 30 days. How much full outage can you afford?">
    21.6 minutes: 0.05% of 43,200 minutes.
  </Q>
  <Q q="A client library retries with min(cap, base × 2^n) and no randomness. What goes wrong?">
    Clients that failed together stay synchronised and retry in waves, each wave
    meeting the same capacity limit. Add full or decorrelated jitter.
  </Q>
  <Q q="gRPC retry throttling: when does a client stop retrying?">
    When its token count falls to half of maxTokens or below. Each failure costs
    one token and each success earns tokenRatio.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Why check the deadline when dequeuing a request, not only when it arrives?">
    Because it may have expired while it waited. Processing it spends capacity
    on a response nobody will read, which is how an overload sustains itself.
  </Q>
