I timed a hundred thousand lock acquisitions per thread and looked at the whole
distribution instead of the mean.

Going from four threads to eight, the median got five times *better* — 208 ns
down to 42 ns. Over the same change the p99 got eleven times **worse**, from
2.1 µs to 23.9 µs.

Watching a median, I would have shipped that as an improvement. The worst single
acquisition in the run was 854 microseconds, for a counter increment, on an idle
laptop.

<SectionHeading n="01" cmd="cat QUESTION.md" id="question">How much load before latency falls apart?</SectionHeading>

You are trying to answer one of these, and they turn out to be the same
question:

- How much load can this take before latency falls apart?
- Why did adding servers stop helping?
- Why is my p99 forty times my p50 when CPU looks fine?

Intuition fails here in a specific, predictable way: people extrapolate
linearly. If 50% utilisation is comfortable, 90% feels like it should be
somewhat less comfortable. It isn't — it's nine times worse, and the difference
between 90% and 95% is larger than the difference between 0% and 90%.

<Callout label="Why guessing doesn't work">
Every shared resource is a queue, whether you modelled it as one or not. A
mutex, a connection pool, a thread pool, a disk, a single-threaded event loop.
Queues obey arithmetic that doesn't care how good your code is, and that
arithmetic is non-linear in exactly the region you'll be operating in.
</Callout>

<SectionHeading n="02" cmd="cat model.txt" id="model">Little's Law, the knee, and the USL</SectionHeading>

Three results. Between them they cover capacity, scaling, and the tail.

<Sub n="2.1" title="Little's Law">

```
L = λW
```

Items in the system equals arrival rate times time in the system. `L` is
concurrency, `λ` is throughput, `W` is latency.

No distributional assumptions at all. It holds for any stable queue — one that
isn't growing without bound — regardless of how arrivals or service times are
distributed. That generality is what makes it useful: given any two of
concurrency, throughput and latency, you get the third for free.

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.2" title="The utilisation curve">

```
W / S  =  1 / (1 − ρ)
```

For an M/M/1 queue, wait time in units of service time `S`, as a function of
utilisation `ρ`.

At 50% busy you wait about one service time. At 90%, nine. At 99%,

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.3" title="The Universal Scalability Law">

```
C(N) = N / (1 + α(N−1) + βN(N−1))
```

Throughput with `N` workers. `α` is **contention** — the serialised fraction.
`β` is **coherency** — the cost of workers having to agree with each other.

Amdahl's law is this with `β = 0`: a serial fraction caps your speedup and the
curve flattens. The β term is what Amdahl left out, and because it's quadratic
in N it eventually makes the curve **turn downward**. Past some size, adding
capacity removes throughput.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="03" cmd="git log --follow model.txt" id="origin">Where the three results come from</SectionHeading>

Little's Law was proved by John Little in 1961, and the proof is close to a
counting argument: over a long interval, area under the concurrency curve can be
computed two ways. It needs no assumption about the distributions, so it
survives contact with real systems that violate every other assumption.

The utilisation curve comes from M/M/1 — Poisson arrivals, exponential service,
one server. Those assumptions are wrong for most real systems, and the curve is
still approximately right, because the mechanism it captures — a server that's
busy can't start your work — doesn't depend on the distributions being neat.

The USL is Neil Gunther's, and it's fitted, not derived. You measure throughput at
several worker counts, fit α and β, and extrapolate. Its value is probably that the coefficients mean something: a large α says "find the lock", a large β says
"they're talking to each other too much".

Note the direction. Three of four make reality **worse** than `1/(1−ρ)`
suggests, so the model is an optimistic bound. Planning against it and leaving
no margin is planning to be wrong in the unsafe direction.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.2" title="Coordinated omission, which breaks your data and not the model">

Your load generator sends a request, waits for the response, sends the next. The
service stalls for a second. During that second the generator sends *nothing* —
so the stall contributes one slow sample instead of the thousand requests that
should have arrived and would all have been slow.

Gil Tene named this **coordinated omission**, and it's probably the most common
reason a latency number is wrong. The bias isn't random. It's worst precisely
when the system is behaving worst.

Fix it by measuring against an *intended* schedule. If you meant to send at
1,000 rps and a request that should have gone at t=5.000s actually went at
t=5.400s, it has already accrued 400 ms before the service saw it. `wrk2`, `oha`
and anything built on HdrHistogram with rate correction do this; `ab` and naive
loops do not.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.3" title="The model says nothing about fairness">

`1/(1−ρ)` predicts mean wait. It has nothing to say about *who* waits, and under
convoy conditions the distribution splits: some requests are served in a fast
batch while others wait milliseconds. Section 5 shows a case where the median
*improved* while the p99 got eleven times worse — both consistent with the same
mean.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.2" title="Fan-out, where the tail arithmetic bites">

If a request waits on N backends, you don't get a backend's p99 — you get the
slowest of N draws.

<Estimate
  rows={[
    { label: "One backend's p99", math: "1 call in 100 is slow", value: "0.99 fast" },
    { label: "Fan out to 10", math: "0.99¹⁰", value: "90% all-fast" },
    { label: "Fan out to 100", math: "0.99¹⁰⁰", value: "37% all-fast" },
    { label: "Fan out to 500", math: "0.99⁵⁰⁰", value: "0.7% all-fast" },
  ]}
  result={{ label: "at 100 backends, requests hitting someone's p99", value: "63%" }}
/>

A one-in-a-hundred event probably happens to two-thirds of requests at a
hundred backends. Dean and Barroso named this in *The Tail at Scale*, and it's why
large fan-out architectures invest so heavily in hedged requests and
tail-tolerant routing.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" cmd="./solve --interactive" id="apply">Apply it to your numbers</SectionHeading>

Work these in order. Each one takes a minute and rules something out.

1. Little's Law on your dashboard. You have throughput and latency. Compute
   `L = λW` and compare it against your thread pool or connection pool size. If
   `L` is at the pool limit, the pool *is* your bottleneck and no amount of
   optimising the handler will show up.
2. **Find your ρ. Utilisation of the actual bottleneck resource, not CPU. If
   it's past 0.8, the curve says your latency is dominated by queueing and
   adding headroom beats optimising.
3. Fit the USL.** Measure throughput at 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 workers and fit α and
   β. A large α means find the serialised section. A non-zero β means your
   workers are talking to each other, and more of them will eventually help
   less than none.
4. **Check your load generator.** Before trusting any of the above, confirm it
   corrects for coordinated omission. If it doesn't, your latency data is
   biased in the unsafe direction and everything else is built on it.

Then the interventions, in descending order of leverage:

- **Reduce utilisation.** Superlinear curve, so headroom buys more than
  optimisation past the knee. 90% to 80% halves the wait.
- **Shard the contended thing.** Chapter 17 measures this at 16×.
- **Shorten the critical section.** Move allocation, I/O and logging out.
- **Hedge.** Duplicate the request at p95, take the first answer. Costs maybe 5%
  extra load, can cut p99 dramatically.
- **Shed load.** Refusing 1% quickly beats serving 100% past the knee. People
  reach for this last and should reach for it third.

<SectionHeading n="08" cmd="./drill --level all" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="What does Little's Law say and when does it apply?">
`L = λW` — items in the system equals arrival rate times time in the system. It
applies to any stable queue with no assumptions about arrival or service
distributions. That generality is what makes it useful: given any two of
concurrency, throughput and latency you get the third.

In practice it's the fastest way to find out whether your connection pool is the
bottleneck. Compute L from your dashboard and compare it to the pool size.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Your p50 is flat and p99 grew tenfold. CPU is unchanged. What's happening?">
Probably a convoy on a shared resource. Threads park behind a holder, one runs a
long uninterrupted batch so its acquisitions are fast and the median looks fine,
while parked threads wait milliseconds.

I measured exactly this going from four to eight threads: p50 improved from
208 ns to 42 ns while p99 went from 2.1 µs to 23.9 µs. Look at off-CPU time and
lock contention, not a CPU profile — a blocked thread burns no cycles and never
appears in a flame graph.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Why does 90% to 95% utilisation hurt so much more than 50% to 55%?">
Because wait scales as `1/(1−ρ)`, not linearly. At 50% you wait about two
service times, at 90% ten, at 95% twenty. The same five-point increase costs
five service times at the top and a fraction of one at the bottom.

Past the knee, headroom is worth more than optimisation — and the curve is an
optimistic bound, since real traffic is burstier than Poisson.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="What is coordinated omission and why does it matter?">
A load generator that waits for each response before sending the next stops
generating load exactly when the system stalls. A one-second stall produces one
slow sample instead of the thousand requests that should have arrived, so the
tail is erased.

The bias isn't random — it's worst precisely when the system is worst. Fix it by
measuring from the intended send time: a request meant to go at 1,000 rps that
departs 400 ms late has already accrued 400 ms. Use `wrk2` or anything built on
HdrHistogram with rate correction.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Your service calls 100 backends per request, each with a 10 ms p99. What's your p99?">
Far worse than 10 ms. Probability all hundred return fast is 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.37, so
about 63% of requests wait on at least one backend's p99. Your p99 is set by the
slowest of a hundred draws.

The fixes are architectural, not code-level: hedge after p95, reduce fan-out, or
make the call tolerant of a partial answer. That's the core argument of *The Tail
at Scale*.
</QA>

<SectionHeading n="09" cmd="cat REFERENCES.md" id="resources">Go deeper</SectionHeading>

<Quiz>
  <Q q="Is it valid to average the p99s from your ten app servers?">
    No. Percentiles don't average. Merge the underlying histograms and take the
    p99 of the combined distribution.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Load test shows p99 of 3 ms. The service froze for 2 seconds during the run. Explain.">
    Coordinated omission. Your generator stopped sending during the freeze, so
    the stall contributed a handful of samples instead of thousands.
  </Q>
  <Q q="USL predicts throughput decreasing past some node count. Which term, and why?">
    The β coherency term. It's quadratic in N because every node may need to
    communicate with every other, so crosstalk eventually grows faster than the
    capacity you added.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Adding threads improved your median. Ship it?">
    Not without checking the tail. A convoy improves the median by batching
    while making p99 dramatically worse — that's the 4-to-8-thread row.
  </Q>
</Quiz>
