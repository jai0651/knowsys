Sooner or later every backend meets load it can't take. Your dashboard shows CPU
at 60% and requests are slow anyway. A load test that was fine at 70% falls
apart at 80%. Adding servers helped last quarter and does nothing this quarter.

None of that is bad luck. Anything shared in a computer (a lock, a database
connection, a disk, a thread pool) is a queue, and queues obey arithmetic that
was settled decades before your service existed. It's the arithmetic behind
sizing HikariCP or pgbouncer in front of Postgres, choosing an autoscaler's
threshold, and reading a load test.

This chapter builds that arithmetic from one barista and a line of customers.
It covers the utilisation curve, Little's Law and the Universal Scalability
Law, then what contention does to the tail, why fan-out makes the tail
everyone's problem, and how load generators hide it.

<SectionHeading n="01" id="queues">Every shared resource is a queue</SectionHeading>

Think of a coffee shop with one barista, where each order takes exactly one
minute.

<Sub n="1.1" title="The barista">

If customers arrived exactly one every two minutes, nobody would ever wait. If
they arrived exactly one a minute, still nobody would wait; the barista would
just never rest.

Anything shared in a computer behaves like that barista. The share of time
it's busy is its **utilisation**, written `ρ` (rho).

<Why>So where does waiting come from?</Why>

From randomness. Customers don't arrive on a schedule; they bunch up. Three
arrive together, then nobody for five minutes. Once arrivals are random,
waiting appears long before the barista is fully busy, and it grows in a way
that surprises almost everyone.

</Sub>

<Sub n="1.2" title="Three questions that are really one">

You're usually trying to answer one of these, and they turn out to be the same
question:

- How much load can this take before latency falls apart?
- Why did adding servers stop helping?
- Why is my p99 forty times my p50 when CPU looks fine?

Intuition fails here in a specific, predictable way: people extrapolate
linearly. If 50% utilisation is comfortable, 90% feels like it should be
somewhat less comfortable. It isn't.

<Callout label="Design implication">
Every shared resource is a queue, whether you modelled it as one or not: a
mutex, a connection pool, a thread pool, a disk, a single-threaded event loop.
Queues obey arithmetic that doesn't care how good your code is, and that
arithmetic is non-linear in exactly the region you'll be operating in.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="02" id="utilisation">The utilisation curve</SectionHeading>

In the simplest standard model, the time a request spends in the system,
waiting plus being served, is the service time divided by `(1 − ρ)`.

<Sub n="2.1" title="Time grows as 1 / (1 − ρ)">

```
W / S  =  1 / (1 − ρ)
```

That's for an **M/M/1** queue: random (Poisson) arrivals, exponential service
times, one server. `W` is time in the system and `S` is the service time.

At 50% busy, a request takes twice the work itself: one service time of
waiting, one of service. At 90% it takes ten times, nine of them waiting. At
99%, a hundred.

`1 / (1 − 0.95)` is 20. Five more points of load doubled the time. The
difference between 90% and 95% is larger than the difference between 0% and
90%. The last few percent cost more than everything before them.
</Predict>

<Why>Why do the last few percent cost so much?</Why>

Because the spare capacity is what absorbs a burst. At 50% the server has as
much idle time as busy time to catch up in. At 95% it has one part idle in
twenty, so a queue that builds up in a burst takes a long time to drain, and
every request behind it waits.

</Sub>

That's the whole mechanism: a busy server can't start your work. The busier it
is on average, the more often a burst lands on a queue that's already there.

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.3" title="Why the model is still useful, and where it's optimistic">

The M/M/1 assumptions are wrong for most real systems. Yet the curve is
probably still close, because the mechanism it captures doesn't depend on the
distributions being neat.

But note which way the errors go:

| Assumption | Reality | Effect on the prediction |
|---|---|---|
| Poisson arrivals | Traffic is bursty and correlated | Real queues are worse than predicted |
| Exponential service times | Usually long-tailed | Much worse: the tail dominates |
| One server | You have N | Better than predicted; M/M/c is kinder |
| Stable system | Overload is transient | Little's Law stops applying entirely |

Three of the four make reality **worse** than `1/(1−ρ)` suggests, so the model
is an optimistic bound.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Planning against the curve and leaving no margin. The model is optimistic, so
running at the edge of what it allows is planning to be wrong in the unsafe
direction.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="03" id="littles-law">Little's Law</SectionHeading>

Little's Law, the second result, connects the three numbers every dashboard already shows.

<Sub n="3.1" title="L = λW">

```
L = λW
```

Items in the system equals arrival rate times time in the system. `L` is
concurrency, `λ` (lambda) is throughput, and `W` is latency.

Given any two of concurrency, throughput and latency, you get the third for
free.

<Why>Why does it hold for real systems, when M/M/1 doesn't?</Why>

Because it makes no distributional assumptions at all. It holds for any stable
queue, one that isn't growing without bound, however arrivals and service
times are distributed.

John Little proved it in 1961, and the proof is close to a counting argument:
over a long interval, the area under the concurrency curve can be computed two
ways. It needs nothing about the distributions, so it survives contact with
real systems that violate every other assumption.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.2" title="Finding a pool bottleneck with it">

You have throughput and latency on your dashboard. Multiply them to get `L`,
and compare it against your thread pool or connection pool size.

If `L` is at the pool limit, the pool *is* your bottleneck. No amount of
optimising the handler will show up, because requests are waiting for a slot,
not for the handler.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="04" id="usl">The Universal Scalability Law</SectionHeading>

A third result explains why adding servers or threads eventually stops
helping, and can even make throughput go down.

<Sub n="4.1" title="Contention and coherency">

```
C(N) = N / (1 + α(N−1) + βN(N−1))
```

That's throughput with `N` workers. `α` is **contention**: the serialised
fraction. `β` is **coherency**: the cost of workers having to agree with each
other.

Amdahl's law is this with `β = 0`. A serial fraction caps your speedup, and the
curve flattens.

<Why>How can adding capacity remove throughput?</Why>

Through the `β` term, which Amdahl left out. It's quadratic in `N`, because
every worker may need to communicate with every other. Past some size, that
crosstalk grows faster than the capacity you added, and the curve **turns
downward**.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.2" title="Fitted, not derived">

Neil Gunther's USL isn't derived; it's fitted. Measure
throughput at several worker counts, fit `α` and `β`, and extrapolate.

Its value is probably that the coefficients mean something:

| Fitted result | What it's telling you | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| Large `α` | A serialised section limits you | Find the lock |
| Non-zero `β` | Workers are talking to each other too much | Coordination between workers; more of them will eventually help less than none |
| Both near zero | Scaling is close to linear | Keep adding workers |

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="05" id="tail">What contention does to the tail</SectionHeading>

Waiting is uneven. Most requests are lucky, and a few get stuck behind a burst.
So the slow end of the latency distribution moves first. The **p99**, the time
99 requests in 100 beat, can double while the median barely moves.

<Callout label="The machine" kind="note">
Apple M4, 10 cores, macOS 26.5.1, clang 21 `-O2`. 100,000 lock acquisitions per
thread, each individually timed. Timer overhead is included, so p50 reads 0 at
one thread: the clock is coarser than an uncontended acquisition.
</Callout>

Compare the 8-thread row against the 4-thread row. The median got five times
*better*, 208 ns down to 42 ns. The p99 got eleven times **worse**, 2.1 µs up to
23.9 µs.

Watching the median, you'd have shipped going from four threads to eight as an
improvement.

Worst of all is the 4-thread max: 854 microseconds, to acquire a mutex, on an
idle laptop, for a counter increment.

<Sub n="5.2" title="A convoy, step by step">

<Why>How can the median improve while the tail gets worse?</Why>

Because under contention the distribution splits in two. Step through what
happens on the lock:

  title="A lock convoy: fast for some, milliseconds for others"

    [3, "Every one of those acquisitions is fast. They're most of the samples, so they drag the **median down**."],
    [1, "The parked threads wait through the whole batch before they get a turn, and their few samples land at the **far end**."],
    [3, "Same mean, very different shape: a better p50 and a p99 eleven times worse."],
  ]}
/>

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.3" title="The model says nothing about fairness">

`1/(1−ρ)` predicts mean wait. It has nothing to say about *who* waits. Under
convoy conditions some requests are served in a fast batch while others wait
milliseconds, and both halves are consistent with the same mean.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Judging a change by its median. A convoy improves the median by batching while
making p99 dramatically worse. And a CPU profile won't show it: a blocked
thread burns no cycles and never appears in a flame graph. Look at off-CPU time
and lock contention.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" id="fan-out">Fan-out makes the tail everyone's problem</SectionHeading>

If a request waits on N backends, you don't get a backend's p99. You get the
slowest of N draws.

<Sub n="6.1" title="The slowest of a hundred">

A search page that calls a hundred backends has to wait for the slowest of
them. If each backend is slow on just 1% of calls, what are the odds a
page is fast? It's the chance that all hundred are fast: 0.99 to the hundredth
power.

<Estimate
  rows={[
    { label: "One backend's p99", math: "1 call in 100 is slow", value: "0.99 fast" },
    { label: "Fan out to 10", math: "0.99¹⁰", value: "90% all-fast" },
    { label: "Fan out to 100", math: "0.99¹⁰⁰", value: "37% all-fast" },
    { label: "Fan out to 500", math: "0.99⁵⁰⁰", value: "0.7% all-fast" },
  ]}
  result={{ label: "at 100 backends, requests hitting someone's p99", value: "63%" }}
/>

A one-in-a-hundred event happens to about two-thirds of requests at a hundred
backends. A backend's rare bad case becomes the user's normal case. That's the
argument of Dean and Barroso's *The Tail at Scale*.

</Sub>

<Sub n="6.2" title="Hedged requests">

That's why large fan-out architectures invest so heavily in hedged requests and
tail-tolerant routing. A hedge duplicates a request that's running late and
takes whichever answer comes first:

<StepSequence
  title="A hedged request to a slow backend"
  actors={["Frontend", "Backend A", "Backend B (replica)"]}
  messages={[
    { from: 0, to: 1, label: "request", note: "The frontend sends one call of its fan-out to backend A, and starts a timer at that backend's **p95** latency." },
    { from: 1, to: 1, label: "slow: GC, queue, convoy", note: "This time A is in its slow 1%, stuck behind a burst or a pause. The frontend can't tell yet." },
    { from: 0, to: 2, label: "hedge at p95", note: "The timer fires. The frontend sends **the same request** to a replica, B. Only calls slower than p95 get here, so about 5% extra load." },
    { from: 2, to: 0, label: "reply", note: "B is almost certainly not in its own slow 1% at the same moment, and answers at normal speed." },
    { from: 1, to: 0, label: "late reply (ignored)", dashed: true, note: "A's answer arrives later and is dropped. The page waited for p95 plus a normal call, not for A's tail." },
  ]}
/>

<Why>Why does a hedge cost so little?</Why>

Because it only fires for requests already slower than p95. Duplicating at p95
costs maybe 5% extra load, and it can cut p99 dramatically.

<Callout label="Design implication">
Fan-out tail problems are fixed in the architecture, not the code: hedge after
p95, reduce fan-out, or make the call tolerant of a partial answer.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="07" id="measuring">Measuring latency without fooling yourself</SectionHeading>

All of the above assumes your latency numbers are right. Often they aren't.

<Sub n="7.1" title="Coordinated omission">

Your load generator sends a request, waits for the response, then sends the
next. Now the service stalls for a second:

<StepSequence
  title="How a one-second stall becomes one slow sample"
  actors={["Load generator", "Service", "Histogram"]}
  messages={[
    { from: 0, to: 1, label: "request 1", note: "The generator is meant to send 1,000 requests a second, but it waits for each response before sending the next." },
    { from: 1, to: 0, label: "response (fast)", note: "Normal service. It records a fast sample and sends the next request." },
    { from: 0, to: 1, label: "request 2", note: "This one arrives just as the service **stalls for a second**." },
    { from: 1, to: 1, label: "stalled 1 s", note: "During that second the generator sends **nothing**. It's still waiting for request 2's answer." },
    { from: 1, to: 0, label: "response (1 s)", note: "The stall ends. Request 2 comes back after a second." },
    { from: 0, to: 2, label: "1 slow sample", note: "The histogram gets **one** slow sample. The thousand requests that should have arrived during the stall, and would all have been slow, were never sent." },
  ]}
/>

Gil Tene named this **coordinated omission**, and it's probably the most common
reason a latency number is wrong. It breaks your data, not the model.

<Why>Why is it worse than ordinary noise?</Why>

Because the bias isn't random. It's worst precisely when the system is
behaving worst, so it erases exactly the tail you were trying to measure.

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.2" title="Measuring against an intended schedule">

Fix it by measuring from the time each request *should* have been sent. If you
meant to send at 1,000 rps and a request that should have gone at t=5.000s
actually went at t=5.400s, it has already accrued 400 ms before the service saw
it.

| Tool | Corrects for coordinated omission? |
|---|---|
| `wrk2` (use `-R`) | Yes: fixed intended rate |
| `oha` | Yes |
| Anything built on HdrHistogram with rate correction | Yes |
| `ab` | No |
| A naive request loop | No |

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.3" title="Percentiles don't average">

Averaging the p99s from ten app servers doesn't give you the fleet's p99.
Merge the underlying histograms and take the p99 of the combined distribution.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Trusting a load test's p99 without checking the generator. A test that reports
a 3 ms p99 through a two-second freeze is coordinated omission: the stall
contributed a handful of samples instead of thousands.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="08" id="apply">Apply it to your numbers</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="8.1" title="Four checks, in order">

Work these in order. Each one takes a minute and rules something out.

1. **Little's Law on your dashboard.** Compute `L = λW` from throughput and
   latency, and compare it against your thread pool or connection pool size. If
   `L` is at the pool limit, the pool is your bottleneck.
2. **Find your ρ.** Take the utilisation of the actual bottleneck resource, not
   CPU. If it's past 0.8, your latency is dominated by queueing, and adding
   headroom beats optimising.
3. **Fit the USL.** Measure throughput at 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 workers and fit `α`
   and `β`. A large `α` means find the serialised section. A non-zero `β` means
   your workers are talking to each other.
4. **Check your load generator.** Before trusting any of the above, confirm it
   corrects for coordinated omission. If it doesn't, your latency data is
   biased in the unsafe direction and everything else is built on it.

</Sub>

<Sub n="8.2" title="Interventions, by leverage">

| Intervention | What it buys | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce utilisation** | The curve is superlinear, so past the knee headroom buys more than optimisation | 90% to 80% halves the wait |
| **Shard the contended thing** | Removes the serialised section | [Chapter 17](/topics/17-concurrent-data-structures) measures this at 16× |
| **Shorten the critical section** | Less time holding the lock | Move allocation, I/O and logging out |
| **Hedge** | Cuts p99 dramatically under fan-out | Costs maybe 5% extra load |
| **Shed load** | Refusing 1% quickly beats serving 100% past the knee | People reach for this last and should reach for it third |

</Sub>

<Sub n="8.3" title="Symptom, cause, fix">

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| p99 grew tenfold, p50 flat, CPU unchanged | A convoy on a shared resource | Check off-CPU time and lock contention; shard or shorten the critical section |
| Handler optimisations don't move latency | `L = λW` is at the pool limit | Grow the pool, or cut `W` elsewhere |
| Latency climbs steeply above ~80% of a resource | Past the knee of `1/(1−ρ)` | Add headroom, or shed load |
| Adding nodes or threads lowers throughput | The USL's `β` term: crosstalk | Reduce coordination between workers |
| Load test p99 looks great, users see stalls | Coordinated omission | Re-run with `wrk2 -R` or an HdrHistogram-based tool |
| Page latency far above any backend's p99 | Fan-out to many backends | Hedge at p95, reduce fan-out, accept partial answers |

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="09" id="summary">Summary</SectionHeading>

1. **Every shared resource is a queue**: locks, pools, disks and event loops
   all obey the same arithmetic.
2. **Time in the system grows as `1/(1−ρ)`.** Twice the work at 50% busy, ten
   times at 90%, twenty at 95%.
3. **The model is optimistic.** Bursty arrivals and long-tailed service make
   real queues worse than it predicts.
4. **Little's Law needs no assumptions.** `L = λW` from your dashboard tells you
   whether the pool is the bottleneck.
5. **The USL's `β` term makes scaling turn downward**, because crosstalk grows
   quadratically with workers.
6. **Contention hits the tail first.** In the test here, going from four threads
   to eight made p50 five times better and p99 eleven times worse.
7. **A convoy splits the distribution**, and a CPU profile can't see the threads
   that are waiting.
8. **Fan-out turns a backend's p99 into the user's normal case**: 63% of
   requests at a hundred backends.
9. **Hedging at p95 costs about 5% extra load** and can cut p99 dramatically.
10. **Most load generators hide stalls.** Measure against an intended schedule,
    and merge histograms instead of averaging percentiles.

<SectionHeading n="10" id="build">Build this</SectionHeading>

**A tail-latency lab on your own laptop.**

- Time 100,000 lock acquisitions per thread at 1, 2, 4 and 8 threads, and print
  p50, p90, p99, p99.9 and max. Check whether your machine shows the same split
  as section 5.1.
- Measure a toy service's throughput at 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 workers and fit the
  USL's `α` and `β`.
- Load-test it twice at a fixed rate, once with a closed loop that waits for
  each response and once with `wrk2 -R`, while you inject a one-second pause.
  Compare the two p99s.

<SectionHeading n="11" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="What does Little's Law say and when does it apply?">
`L = λW`: items in the system equals arrival rate times time in the system. It
applies to any stable queue with no assumptions about arrival or service
distributions. That generality is what makes it useful: given any two of
concurrency, throughput and latency you get the third.

In practice it's the fastest way to find out whether your connection pool is the
bottleneck. Compute L from your dashboard and compare it to the pool size.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Your p50 is flat and p99 grew tenfold. CPU is unchanged. What's happening?">
Probably a convoy on a shared resource. Threads park behind a holder, one runs a
long uninterrupted batch so its acquisitions are fast and the median looks fine,
while parked threads wait milliseconds.

I measured exactly this going from four to eight threads: p50 improved from
208 ns to 42 ns while p99 went from 2.1 µs to 23.9 µs. Look at off-CPU time and
lock contention, not a CPU profile: a blocked thread burns no cycles and never
appears in a flame graph.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Why does 90% to 95% utilisation hurt so much more than 50% to 55%?">
Because time in the system scales as `1/(1−ρ)`, not linearly. At 50% it's about
two service times, at 90% ten, at 95% twenty. The same five-point increase costs
ten service times at the top and a fraction of one at the bottom.

Past the knee, headroom is worth more than optimisation, and the curve is an
optimistic bound, since real traffic is burstier than Poisson.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="What is coordinated omission and why does it matter?">
A load generator that waits for each response before sending the next stops
generating load exactly when the system stalls. A one-second stall produces one
slow sample instead of the thousand requests that should have arrived, so the
tail is erased.

The bias isn't random; it's worst precisely when the system is worst. Fix it by
measuring from the intended send time: a request meant to go at 1,000 rps that
departs 400 ms late has already accrued 400 ms. Use `wrk2` or anything built on
HdrHistogram with rate correction.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Your service calls 100 backends per request, each with a 10 ms p99. What's your p99?">
Far worse than 10 ms. Probability all hundred return fast is 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.37, so
about 63% of requests wait on at least one backend's p99. Your p99 is set by the
slowest of a hundred draws.

The fixes are architectural, not code-level: hedge after p95, reduce fan-out, or
make the call tolerant of a partial answer. That's the core argument of *The Tail
at Scale*.
</QA>

  </Q>
  <Q q="USL predicts throughput decreasing past some node count. Which term, and why?">
    The β coherency term. It's quadratic in N because every node may need to
    communicate with every other, so crosstalk eventually grows faster than the
    capacity you added.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Adding threads improved your median. Ship it?">
    Not without checking the tail. A convoy improves the median by batching
    while making p99 dramatically worse. That's the 4-to-8-thread row.
  </Q>
</Quiz>
