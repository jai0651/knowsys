A network card receives a stream of frames off the wire and turns each one into
bytes. Your program, somewhere above, calls `read()` on a socket and gets the
payload in order, with the headers stripped and lost packets resent. This
chapter is about everything that happens in between, and it's easiest to
understand by building it up from the naive version.

The naive version is for the card to interrupt the CPU for every packet and let
the kernel copy it in. That works at dial-up speeds. A 10 Gbit/s link can carry
close to 15 million minimum-size frames a second, though, and a core that took
an interrupt for each one would do nothing else. So two fixes come first. The
card writes packets straight into memory itself, into a **ring** of buffers it
shares with its driver, without the CPU copying anything. And instead of one
interrupt per packet, the driver takes one interrupt, switches it off, and
polls the ring in batches until it's empty. Linux calls that polling scheme
**NAPI**.

Now the kernel has a packet in memory and needs to keep track of it as it moves
up the stack. It wraps each one in a `struct sk_buff`, a record that points at
the bytes and carries everything learned about them on the way. IP checks where
the packet is going. The firewall hooks (netfilter, and its connection tracker
if you use one) get a look. TCP puts the bytes in order and appends them to your
socket's receive queue, then wakes your thread if it's asleep in `epoll` or
`read()`. Sending goes the same way in reverse, through a queueing layer and a
transmit ring.

Look at what that design is made of: a chain of hand-offs, and every hand-off is
a queue with a fixed size. The ring holds so many descriptors, the per-CPU
backlog so many packets, the socket buffer so many bytes. When a queue is full,
the next packet is dropped. Almost every "the network lost it" problem is one
of those queues overflowing, and almost every one of them has a counter
somewhere.

Here's a question that shows why this matters. Your server calls `listen(fd,
128)`, and a burst of a thousand new connections arrives before it gets round
to calling `accept()`. What happens to the ones that don't fit? The kernel drops
their handshake packets. Each client resends after about a second, so those
users see an extra second or more of latency, and your application's logs show
nothing at all, because the requests never reached it. That's exactly the
backlog nginx and HAProxy ask you to size. It has cousins: a full connection
tracking table (`nf_conntrack: table full, dropping packet`) has taken down more
than one Kubernetes node, and a UDP service like DNS or statsd loses datagrams
silently when its receive buffer fills.

I found out how hidden some of this is with a forty-line server that called
`listen(fd, 4)` and never accepted. **Five** connections got in, not four. And
when I counted the refused SYNs with the tracepoint every drop-debugging guide
recommends, it saw zero of the 120 the kernel had counted. Section 4.1 explains
both. The rest of the chapter follows one packet in and one out, then goes
through where each drop is counted and what a packet costs.

<SectionHeading n="01" cmd="man 7 tcp" id="contract">What the stack promises</SectionHeading>

For TCP the promise is an ordered, reliable byte stream per socket. For UDP it's
much less: a datagram arrives intact or not at all. That's the whole contract
from the application's side, and it says nothing about *how many* packets the
kernel is allowed to throw away to keep it.

It's allowed to throw away a lot. TCP hides the loss behind retransmission and
UDP doesn't hide it at all. Between the wire and your `read()` there's a chain
of bounded queues, and each one drops when it's full:

- **The NIC's RX ring.** A fixed number of descriptors pointing at DMA buffers.
- **The per-CPU backlog**, `netdev_max_backlog` packets long (1,000 by default,
  from [`net/core/hotdata.c`](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.10/net/core/hotdata.c)).
- **The conntrack table**, if anything on the box uses netfilter state.
- **The socket's receive buffer**, `sk_rcvbuf` bytes, charged by allocation size.
- **The listener's accept queue**, sized by your `listen()` backlog and capped by `somaxconn`.

Sending has its own chain: the socket send buffer, the qdisc, and the TX ring.

<Callout label="Why 'the network dropped it' is almost never the diagnosis">
Every one of those queues has a counter. They live in at least four places:
`ethtool -S` for the ring, `/proc/net/softnet_stat` for the backlog, `nstat`
for the socket and listener, `dmesg` for conntrack. A drop you "can't see" is
usually a drop you're reading the wrong file for.
</Callout>

<SectionHeading n="02" cmd="grep -n 'struct sk_buff {' include/linux/skbuff.h" id="layout">sk_buff, and the bytes it points at</SectionHeading>

Every packet in the kernel is a `struct sk_buff`. Its name is older than most of
the code in it (it's short for "socket buffer"), and the thing to understand is
that it's *metadata about* a packet. It doesn't contain the bytes. It points at
them.

</SourceRead>

A handful of those fields do most of the work:

- **`next` / `prev`** come first so an skb can sit on a queue with no extra
  allocation. Every queue in section 1 is a list of these.
- **`head`, `data`, `tail`, `end`** bracket the actual buffer. `data` moves
  forward as each layer strips its header, so IP never copies the Ethernet
  frame. It just advances a pointer by 14 bytes.
- **`mac_header`, `network_header`, `transport_header`** are 16-bit offsets from
  `head`, not pointers. That's what my bpftrace script used to find the TCP
  port: `head + transport_header`.
- **`_nfct`** is conntrack's pointer to this packet's flow entry. It's why a
  packet carries its connection state through every later hook for free.
- **`truesize`** is what the socket gets charged for, and it isn't `len`.

Past `end`, in the same allocation as the data, sits `struct skb_shared_info`.
It holds `nr_frags` and an array of `frags[]`, each one a reference to a page
plus an offset and length. That array is how the stack avoids copying: GRO
glues packets together by appending frags, `sendfile` hands page-cache pages to
TCP as frags, and `MSG_ZEROCOPY` pins your user pages and does the same.

```text
head        data                         tail        end
 |headroom  | eth | ip | tcp | payload... | tailroom  | skb_shared_info: nr_frags, frags[] → pages
             ^mac   ^network ^transport
```

<Sub n="2.1" title="truesize, measured">

How big is the gap between `len` and `truesize`? I asked for a 64 KB receive
buffer on a UDP socket, sent it 10,000 datagrams of 100 bytes each, and didn't
read any of them. (The code is in section 4.5.)

Asking for 64 KB got me 131,072, according to `getsockopt`. It kept **158 datagrams**.
That's 131,072 / 158 ≈ **830 bytes of buffer per 100-byte payload**, because the
socket is charged for the skb struct and the whole slab allocation behind the
data, not for the bytes you'll `recv()`. So a UDP service sized with "rcvbuf
divided by message size" is off by roughly 8× for small messages.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="03" cmd="perf trace -e 'net:*' -- curl localhost" id="hot-path">One packet in, one packet out</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="3.1" title="From the wire to RAM, and the interrupt nobody wants">

The NIC owns a ring of descriptors, each one pointing at a buffer the driver
pre-allocated. A frame arrives, the NIC DMAs it into the next free buffer,
writes the length into the descriptor and raises an interrupt. No CPU has
touched a byte yet.

Its IRQ handler does almost nothing. It masks further interrupts from that
queue and calls `napi_schedule()`, which puts the queue's `napi_struct` on this
CPU's poll list and raises `NET_RX_SOFTIRQ`. Everything after this runs in
softirq context, on the CPU that took the interrupt. Hold on to that last
clause; half of section 4 follows from it.

Why mask the interrupt? At a million packets a second, one interrupt per
packet would leave no CPU for anything else. **NAPI** (the "new API"; LWN's [history](https://lwn.net/Articles/244640/) is still the best
account) turns the device into something you poll while it's busy and interrupt
on only when it goes idle.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.2" title="net_rx_action, and the budget of 300">

Here's the loop that runs when the softirq fires:

```c
static __latent_entropy void net_rx_action(struct softirq_action *h)
{
	struct softnet_data *sd = this_cpu_ptr(&softnet_data);
	unsigned long time_limit = jiffies +
		usecs_to_jiffies(READ_ONCE(net_hotdata.netdev_budget_usecs));
	int budget = READ_ONCE(net_hotdata.netdev_budget);
	/* ... */
	for (;;) {
		struct napi_struct *n;
		/* ... empty-list handling ... */
		n = list_first_entry(&list, struct napi_struct, poll_list);
		budget -= napi_poll(n, &repoll);

		/* If softirq window is exhausted then punt.
		 * Allow this to run for 2 jiffies since which will allow
		 * an average latency of 1.5/HZ.
		 */
		if (unlikely(budget <= 0 ||
			     time_after_eq(jiffies, time_limit))) {
			sd->time_squeeze++;
			break;
		}
	}
	/* ... re-queue anything unfinished, re-raise the softirq ... */
```
</SourceRead>

Two limits, whichever hits first. `netdev_budget` is 300 packets per softirq
run. `netdev_budget_usecs` defaults to two jiffies (the source says
`2 * USEC_PER_SEC / HZ`), which reads as 2000 µs on the container I used.
Inside that, each driver's `poll()` is handed a weight, `dev_weight`, 64 by
default. I read all three off the box: `300`, `2000`, `64`.

When the loop runs out of budget with work still pending, it bumps
`sd->time_squeeze`. That's column three of `/proc/net/softnet_stat`, and it's
the only direct evidence you'll get that a CPU couldn't keep up with its queues.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.3" title="Up the stack: GRO, netfilter, TCP">

Inside the driver's poll, each frame becomes an skb and goes to
`napi_gro_receive()`. **GRO** (generic receive offload; LWN covered it in
[2009](https://lwn.net/Articles/358910/)) holds packets from the same flow and
merges consecutive ones into one big skb with a frags list. About forty-five 1,500-byte
segments can go up the stack as a single 64 KB skb, paying the per-packet cost
once.

After that it's a sequence of function calls, all still in softirq:

At `PREROUTING`, conntrack looks up the packet's tuple in its hash table. A
first packet of a new flow gets an entry allocated and stored in `_nfct`. On a
Kubernetes node running kube-proxy in iptables mode, this happens to every
packet, because the service NAT rules depend on connection state.

`tcp_v4_rcv()` then finds the socket. If a user thread holds the socket lock
right now, the skb goes on the socket's *backlog* (a different backlog again)
and is processed when the lock is released. Otherwise `tcp_rcv_established()`
runs the fast path, appends the data to `sk_receive_queue`, and calls
`sk_data_ready`.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.4" title="Waking the thread: epoll">

`sk_data_ready` defaults to `sock_def_readable()`, which wakes the socket's wait
queue. If you're blocked in `read()`, that's you. If you registered the socket
with epoll, the wait-queue entry is epoll's `ep_poll_callback()`, which puts
the socket's `epitem` on the epoll instance's ready list and wakes whoever is in
`epoll_wait()`.

That's where the classic thundering herd lives. Put one listening socket in
many workers' epoll sets and a single SYN can wake all of them.
`EPOLLEXCLUSIVE` (Linux 4.5, [man page](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/epoll_ctl.2.html))
limits it to one. But which one? Cloudflare found that
[wake-ups go LIFO](https://blog.cloudflare.com/the-sad-state-of-linux-socket-balancing/):
the most recently added waiter gets the connection, so one NGINX worker took
most of the load.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.5" title="Which CPU does all this?">

Everything so far ran on the CPU that took the interrupt. Spreading that out is
the job of four separate mechanisms, all documented in
[`scaling.rst`](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.10/Documentation/networking/scaling.rst):

- **RSS** is the NIC hashing the 4-tuple into one of several RX queues, each
  with its own IRQ. It's hardware, it's free, and it's bounded by the number of
  queues.
- **RPS** does the same in software: the receiving CPU hashes the packet and
  enqueues it on another CPU's backlog, then sends an IPI. Useful when the NIC
  has fewer queues than you have cores.
- **RFS** steers to the CPU where the consuming thread last ran, so the
  softirq work and your `recv()` share a cache.
- **`SO_REUSEPORT`** (Linux 3.9, [LWN](https://lwn.net/Articles/542629/)) moves
  the fan-out to the socket layer: several sockets bind the same port and the
  kernel hashes each new flow to one of them.

My VM had none of the first one. `ethtool -l eth0` reported
`Combined: 1`, a single virtio queue, so every packet from outside lands on
whichever CPU services that one interrupt.

Loopback is its own case. `lo` has no ring and no IRQ. Its transmit function
calls `__netif_rx()`, which drops the skb onto the *current* CPU's backlog, and a
pseudo-NAPI called `process_backlog` delivers it. That's why loopback traffic
shows up in `softnet_stat`: during a five-second iperf3 run, the processed
column rose by 2,397,701 while `IpInReceives` in the same namespace rose by
2,397,255.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.6" title="The send path, down to the TX ring">

`write()` on a TCP socket lands in `tcp_sendmsg()`, which copies your bytes into
skbs on the socket's write queue. Nothing is sent yet. `tcp_write_xmit()` then
decides how much it can send (congestion window, receive window, pacing), and
with TSO it builds segments up to 64 KB for the NIC to cut into MTU-sized ones.

From there it goes `ip_queue_xmit()`, through netfilter `OUTPUT` and
`POSTROUTING`, to `dev_queue_xmit()`. That's where the **qdisc** sits: the
queueing discipline for the device, usually `fq_codel` or `fq` on a physical
NIC. XPS picks which TX queue. The driver's `ndo_start_xmit()` writes a
descriptor into the TX ring and rings a doorbell. Later, a TX-completion
interrupt (or the next NAPI poll) frees the skb.

Loopback skips the qdisc entirely. `tc -s qdisc show dev lo` in my namespace
said `noqueue` with 0 packets sent, straight after three iperf3 runs had pushed
about 250 GB through it.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.7" title="Skipping copies, and skipping the stack">

A normal `write()` copies once, user memory into skb memory. There are three
ways to avoid it, and each works at a different layer:

- **`sendfile()`** moves page-cache pages into the socket as frags. No copy
  into user space and none out. Netflix built its CDN on this, plus kernel TLS,
  on FreeBSD; Drew Gallatin's
  [400 Gb/s talk](https://papers.freebsd.org/2021/eurobsdcon/gallatin-netflix-freebsd-400gbps.files/gallatin-netflix-freebsd-400gbps-slides.pdf)
  is the reference. Linux got kTLS transmit in 4.13.
- **`MSG_ZEROCOPY`** (Linux 4.14) pins your user pages and tells you on the
  socket error queue when the kernel's done with them. The
  [kernel doc](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.10/Documentation/networking/msg_zerocopy.rst)
  says it's "generally only effective at writes over around 10 KB", because
  pinning and the completion notification cost more than a small memcpy.
- **XDP** skips the stack. A BPF program runs in the driver's poll, on the raw
  DMA buffer, before any skb exists, and returns drop, pass, transmit or
  redirect. **AF_XDP** (4.18) redirects frames into a user-space ring.

I tried `MSG_ZEROCOPY` over loopback expecting a speed-up. Every one of 64
one-megabyte sends came back flagged `SO_EE_CODE_ZEROCOPY_COPIED`. The doc
explains it: anything "looped to a local socket will incur a deferred copy". You
pay for the pinning and the notification *and* still get the copy.

XDP's appeal is the number. The
[CoNEXT 2018 paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3281411.3281443) reports
single-core processing "as high as 24 million packets per second". Cloudflare
runs its DDoS drops and its L4 load balancer there
([L4Drop](https://blog.cloudflare.com/l4drop-xdp-ebpf-based-ddos-mitigations/)),
and Facebook's [Katran](https://engineering.fb.com/2018/05/22/open-source/open-sourcing-katran-a-scalable-network-load-balancer/)
does the same. How the verifier, maps and helpers work is its own chapter:
[eBPF](/topics/48-ebpf).

Last, **busy polling**. `SO_BUSY_POLL` (3.11) lets a thread in `recv()` spin
on the NAPI queue itself instead of sleeping until the softirq wakes it. It
trades a burnt core for fewer wake-ups. The newer knobs,
`napi_defer_hard_irqs` (5.8) and `SO_PREFER_BUSY_POLL` (5.11), are described in
[`napi.rst`](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.10/Documentation/networking/napi.rst).
I didn't measure any of them: busy polling on loopback polls nothing.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.1" title="The accept queue, and the drop that isn't a drop">

Here's the program from the opening. It's forty lines and it's the fastest way
I know to make `ListenOverflows` move.

```cpp
    int c = socket(AF_INET, SOCK_STREAM, 0);
    fcntl(c, F_SETFL, O_NONBLOCK);

```

Fifteen clients, sixty overflows in four seconds: every SYN they sent,
original and retransmit, counted once. The client never sees an error. Its SYN
just goes unanswered, and from the outside that's indistinguishable from packet
loss on the wire.

Then the retransmit timing surprised me. Counting overflows once a second for a
single stuck client, they landed at t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 11 and 19 seconds.
If you learnt "1, 3, 7, 15" you learnt it before Linux 6.5, which added
`tcp_syn_linear_timeouts` ([ccce324dabfe](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/ccce324dabfe))
with a default of 4. Its first four retries are a flat second apart.

Now the invisible part. With a bpftrace script filtering bare SYNs to port 9000
on both `skb:kfree_skb` and `skb:consume_skb`, a ten-second run gave:

```text
TcpExtListenOverflows           105
@[tracepoint:skb:consume_skb, tcp_rcv_state_process]: 110
```

That's 105 refused SYNs plus the 5 that were accepted, and **not one** hit
`kfree_skb`. Here's why:

`tcp_conn_request()` increments `LINUX_MIB_LISTENOVERFLOWS`, jumps to `drop:`,
calls `tcp_listendrop()` and returns 0. Its caller ignores the return value and
frees the SYN with `consume_skb()`. So dropwatch, `perf record -e skb:kfree_skb`
and every drop-reason dashboard miss the single most common TCP overload
signal. I checked v6.12, v6.15 and v6.17: the `consume_skb` is still there.

<Callout label="Why the drop tracer lies about SYN floods">
Drop reasons (`kfree_skb_reason`, 5.17) only cover packets freed through
`kfree_skb`. A SYN refused for a full accept queue goes through `consume_skb`,
the same path as a packet delivered successfully. For listen overflows, trust
`nstat` and `ss -ltn`, not the tracer.
</Callout>

Fixing it is rarely about the backlog number. A full accept queue means the application
isn't calling `accept()` fast enough: a blocked event loop, a GC pause, a
thread pool at its cap. Raise the backlog to ride out bursts. `somaxconn` went
from 128 to 4096 in Linux 5.4
([19f92a030ca6](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/19f92a030ca6d772ab44b22ee6a01378a8cb32d4)),
and Cloudflare runs 16k
([SYN packet handling in the wild](https://blog.cloudflare.com/syn-packet-handling-in-the-wild/)).
But the application is the thing to look at.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.2" title="nf_conntrack: table full, dropping packet">

This is the one that shows up in Kubernetes postmortems. Conntrack has a
fixed-size table, and when it's full, as Cloudflare's
[Conntrack tales](https://blog.cloudflare.com/conntrack-tales-one-thousand-and-one-flows/)
puts it, "packets creating new flows will be dropped. No questions asked."
Existing flows keep working, so the symptom is new connections timing out while
dashboards for established traffic look fine.

Its default size scales with RAM. On 64-bit hosts with more than 4 GB the
bucket count is fixed at 262,144 and `nf_conntrack_max` equals it
([`nf_conntrack-sysctl.rst`](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.10/Documentation/networking/nf_conntrack-sysctl.rst)
and `nf_conntrack_core.c`). The 8 GB VM I used read exactly that: 262144 and
262144.

Smaller nodes get smaller tables. That's probably the commonest way in, and it's how
[loveholidays](https://deploy.live/blog/kubernetes-networking-problems-due-to-the-conntrack/)
hit it: they halved node RAM while connection counts doubled. Mark Betz's
[GKE incident](https://www.markbetz.net/2023/12/12/exhausting-conntrack-table-space-crippled-our-k8s-cluster/)
was a misbehaving CDN flooding an RPC client through Linkerd until the nodes
logged the message in this section's title.

One detail matters for who can fix it. From inside a network namespace (a pod is one), `echo 100 > /proc/sys/net/netfilter/nf_conntrack_max`
gave me `Permission denied`. It's a node-wide setting, so a pod can't raise it
and kube-proxy sets it at startup
([kubernetes#71261](https://github.com/kubernetes/kubernetes/issues/71261)).
Watch `nf_conntrack_count` against `nf_conntrack_max` and alert well before
100%.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.3" title="One core pinned in softirq">

Cloudflare's [How to receive a million packets per second](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-to-receive-a-million-packets/)
is the canonical version. A naive single-threaded UDP receiver got about 370k
packets per second. The limit wasn't the application. All packets from one
source hashed to one RX queue, and "CPU #4 can't do any more work - it's
totally busy just reading the 350kpps."

Adding receiver threads doesn't help much, because the bottleneck is the softirq on
the CPU that owns the queue. What helped was spreading the flows over more RX
queues, `SO_REUSEPORT` for several receiving sockets, and keeping the receiver
on the NIC's NUMA node (the wrong node cost up to 4× in their test). The end
result was 1.4 Mpps on one NUMA node.

You'll recognise it: `mpstat -P ALL 1` shows one CPU at 100% in
`%soft` and the rest idle. On a VM with a single virtio queue, like the one I
measured on, you'll hit it early. RPS is the software fix when you can't get
more queues.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.4" title="ksoftirqd, and a fix reverted seven years later">

Softirqs normally run on the tail of an interrupt, ahead of any thread. To keep
them from starving everything, `__do_softirq()` gives up after 2 ms or 10
restarts
([`kernel/softirq.c`](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.10/kernel/softirq.c#L475))
and hands the rest to the per-CPU `ksoftirqd` thread, which the scheduler
treats like any other thread.

That handoff has been argued about for a decade. In 2016 Eric Dumazet's
"softirq: Let ksoftirqd do its job"
([4cd13c21b207](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/4cd13c21b207), 4.9)
stopped softirqs from sneaking back in once `ksoftirqd` was running. Under a
UDP flood, the receiving process went from "~2,000 packets per second" to
"~900,000".

In 2023 Paolo Abeni reverted it
([d15121be7485](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/d15121be7485), 6.5)
because deferring to a normal-priority thread caused "high latencies". The
revert says the livelock can now be handled by threaded NAPI (5.12, `echo 1 >
/sys/class/net/eth0/threaded`), which gives each NAPI instance its own kernel
thread you can pin and prioritise.

If `top` shows `ksoftirqd/N` near 100%, the softirq budget on CPU N is
permanently exhausted. It's pretty much section 4.3 wearing a different hat.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.5" title="The receive buffer fills and sendto() doesn't care">

<TryIt what="A UDP socket that asks for 64 KB and never reads" lang="cpp" toolchain="g++ 13.3 -O2, linuxkit 6.10.14 aarch64">
```cpp
int rx = socket(AF_INET, SOCK_DGRAM, 0);
int want = 65536, got = 0;
socklen_t l = sizeof got;
setsockopt(rx, SOL_SOCKET, SO_RCVBUF, &want, sizeof want);
getsockopt(rx, SOL_SOCKET, SO_RCVBUF, &got, &l);
bind(rx, (sockaddr*)&a, sizeof a);             // 127.0.0.1:9001

int tx = socket(AF_INET, SOCK_DGRAM, 0);
char payload[100] = {};
for (int i = 0; i < 10000; i++)
    if (sendto(tx, payload, sizeof payload, 0, (sockaddr*)&a, sizeof a) == sizeof payload) ok++;

while (recv(rx, buf, sizeof buf, MSG_DONTWAIT) > 0) queued++;
```

<Output>
```text
asked for SO_RCVBUF=65536, kernel says 131072
sendto() succeeded 10000 times out of 10000
datagrams actually waiting in the socket: 158
UdpInDatagrams                  158
UdpInErrors                     9842
UdpRcvbufErrors                 9842
```

The kernel **doubles** what you ask for (`__sock_set_rcvbuf()` stores
`val * 2` to leave room for skb overhead), then charges each datagram its
`truesize`. The sender got success for all 10,000 packets. The receiver got 158.
</Output>
</TryIt>

That's the UDP failure in one screen: loss the sender can't see, counted in
`UdpRcvbufErrors`. For DNS servers, statsd collectors and syslog receivers it's
the first counter to check. Raising `SO_RCVBUF` needs `net.core.rmem_max`
raised too (212,992 on this box), or the request is silently capped.

TCP can't lose data this way, but it pays in time. Cloudflare's
[story of one latency spike](https://blog.cloudflare.com/the-story-of-one-latency-spike/)
traced multi-millisecond stalls to `tcp_collapse()`, which compacts a bloated
receive queue inside softirq. Cutting the `tcp_rmem` maximum from 32 MiB took
`net_rx_action` from 23 ms to 3 ms. So a bigger buffer seems like the safe choice and isn't.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="05" cmd="iperf3 -c 127.0.0.1 -t 5" id="cost">What a packet costs</SectionHeading>

<Callout label="The machines" kind="note">
**Linux:** kernel 6.10.14-linuxkit, aarch64, in a Docker Desktop VM on an Apple
M4, run in a dedicated network namespace inside a shared 4-CPU container. g++
13.3 `-O2`. Three runs each, median reported. **macOS:** the same M4, macOS
26.5.1, Apple clang 21 `-O2`. The Linux box was shared with other workloads
while these ran, so treat its numbers as a floor.
</Callout>

One sender thread, one reader thread, a loopback TCP connection, and a fixed
write size per run:

Read the ns-per-write column. From 64 bytes to 1 KB it barely moves, so at
small sizes you're paying a fixed ~250 ns per call on Linux, probably most of it
the syscall, the socket lock and skb allocation. Past 16 KB the copy dominates
and throughput flattens out. Linux inside a VM beat native macOS by about 1.8×
on small writes, on the same silicon. I haven't dug into why.

iperf3 on the same Linux loopback gave 146, 142 and 131 Gbit/s over five
seconds, median **142 Gbit/s**. `ss -tin` mid-run showed `mss:65483` and about
48 KB per data segment. So loopback isn't a network at all. It's roughly a memcpy
with a TCP state machine attached.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.2" title="The per-packet budget on real wire">

Loopback hides the number that matters on a NIC: packets per second.
Here's the arithmetic for 10 GbE with minimum-size frames:

<Estimate

That last line is why DDoS mitigation lives in XDP. A flood of small packets
through the normal stack needs dozens of cores just to decide to drop it.
Cloudflare's L4Drop post says moving drops into XDP meant softirq CPU rose "by
slightly over a factor of 2" while packet rate rose "by over a factor of 40".

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.3" title="Retransmits on a link that can't lose packets">

Every iperf3 run on loopback reported retransmits: 16, 75 and 19 in three
five-second runs. Loopback doesn't drop, so what's being retransmitted?

Counters say they were spurious. In each run `TcpRetransSegs` roughly
matched `TCPDSACKRecv` (16 and 16, 75 and 73, 19 and 17): the receiver reported
already having the data. `TCPLossProbes` fired too, and `ss -tin` showed
`retrans:0/11 dsack_dups:11`. So a tail-loss probe timer is expiring before an
ACK comes back.

I think it's scheduling. On a shared 4-CPU box the receiving thread gets
descheduled, ACKs stall past the probe timeout, and TCP probes. I haven't
proved it. Pinning sender and receiver to idle CPUs on a dedicated machine and
checking whether the count drops to zero would settle it.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" cmd="watch -d cat /proc/net/softnet_stat" id="operating">The Monday tools</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="6.1" title="Where each drop is counted">

```shell
# Ring and driver: rx_drops, rx_missed, per-queue counters, xdp counters
ethtool -S eth0 | grep -iE 'drop|miss|err|xdp'
ethtool -l eth0            # how many RX/TX queues. "Combined: 1" means no RSS
ethtool -g eth0            # ring size; -G to grow it

# Per-CPU backlog and budget. One row per CPU, hex, 15 columns on 6.10
cat /proc/net/softnet_stat   # col1 processed, col2 dropped, col3 time_squeeze

# Listener and socket counters, as deltas since the last call
nstat | grep -E 'ListenOverflows|ListenDrops|RcvbufErrors|TCPBacklogDrop|PruneCalled'

# One socket, in detail
ss -tin 'dport = :443'     # rtt, cwnd, retrans, rwnd_limited, dsack_dups
ss -ltn                    # on LISTEN rows: Recv-Q = queued, Send-Q = limit

# Conntrack headroom
cat /proc/sys/net/netfilter/nf_conntrack_{count,max}
dmesg | grep 'table full'

# Is one CPU drowning in softirq?
mpstat -P ALL 1            # %soft column
```

The 6.10 `softnet_stat` layout is in
[`net/core/net-procfs.c`](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.10/net/core/net-procfs.c).
Older guides, including packagecloud's otherwise excellent
[Monitoring and Tuning the Linux Networking Stack](https://blog.packagecloud.io/monitoring-tuning-linux-networking-stack-receiving-data/),
describe column 9 as `cpu_collision`; it's hard-coded to 0 now, and columns
13–15 are backlog length, CPU index and the two queue lengths.

</Sub>

<Sub n="6.2" title="Tracing drops, and where tracing fails">

For drops that go through `kfree_skb`, drop reasons turn a count into a
location:

```shell
# Needs tracefs mounted. Counts drops by function and reason code.
bpftrace -e 'tracepoint:skb:kfree_skb { @[ksym(args->location), args->reason] = count(); }'

# Or the older tool
dropwatch -l kas
```

Reason codes are the enum in
[`include/net/dropreason-core.h`](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.10/include/net/dropreason-core.h);
`SOCKET_RCVBUFF`, `SOCKET_BACKLOG`, `CPU_BACKLOG` and `PROTO_MEM` are the ones
you'll see under load. Two things bit me on the VM. `perf` wouldn't run at all
(Ubuntu's wrapper wants `linux-tools-6.10.14-linuxkit`, which doesn't exist), so
bpftrace is the route in. And the tracepoint is global, not per-namespace: my
first unfiltered run counted every other container's drops too, until I filtered
on the TCP port.

Plus the gap from section 4.1: listen overflows never appear here at all.

</Sub>

<Sub n="6.3" title="What to change, in order">

1. **Find the counter first.** Guessing sysctls without a moving counter is how
   people end up with 32 MB `tcp_rmem` and the latency spike from 4.5.
2. **Accept-queue overflows:** fix the application's accept rate, then raise
   the `listen()` backlog, then `somaxconn`.
3. **One hot softirq CPU:** more RX queues (`ethtool -L`), RSS indirection,
   RPS if the NIC can't, then `SO_REUSEPORT` receivers.
4. **`time_squeeze` climbing:** raise `netdev_budget`, or move to threaded NAPI
   and give the NAPI threads their own cores.
5. **Conntrack:** raise `nf_conntrack_max` on the node, shorten timeouts for
   short-lived flows, or add `notrack` rules for traffic that doesn't need state.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="10" cmd="./drill --level all" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="What's the difference between the SYN queue and the accept queue?">
The SYN queue holds half-open connections: SYN received, SYN-ACK sent, waiting
for the final ACK. The accept queue holds completed connections waiting for
`accept()`. Both are sized from the `listen()` backlog and capped by
`net.core.somaxconn`. When the accept queue is full, Linux drops incoming SYNs
and counts them in `ListenOverflows` and `ListenDrops`.
</QA>

<QA level="beginner" q="What does NAPI do, and why does it exist?">
It switches a busy NIC from interrupt-per-packet to polling. The first packet
raises an IRQ, the driver masks further interrupts and schedules a softirq that
polls the ring, up to a budget (300 packets or 2 jiffies per run by default).
Interrupts are re-enabled only when the ring is empty. Without it, a high packet
rate becomes an interrupt storm and nothing else runs.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Your UDP service loses messages but the sender sees no errors. Where do you look?">
`nstat` for `UdpRcvbufErrors`. The receive buffer is full, and `sendto()` on a
local or remote sender succeeds regardless. Remember the socket is charged
`truesize`: on my test a 100-byte datagram cost about 830 bytes of buffer. Raise
`SO_RCVBUF` (and `rmem_max`, or the request is capped), or read faster, or
spread load with `SO_REUSEPORT`.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="RSS, RPS and RFS: what does each do?">
RSS is the NIC hashing flows into multiple hardware queues with separate IRQs.
RPS is the same hash done in software by the receiving CPU, which then hands
the packet to another CPU's backlog via IPI. RFS extends RPS to pick the CPU
where the consuming application thread last ran, for cache locality. RSS is
free but limited by queue count; RPS and RFS cost an IPI and some CPU.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="A Kubernetes node starts timing out new connections, existing ones are fine. First guess?">
Conntrack. When the table's full, packets that would create a new entry are
dropped while established flows keep matching existing entries. Check `dmesg`
for "nf_conntrack: table full" and compare `nf_conntrack_count` to
`nf_conntrack_max`. It's node-wide, so the fix happens on the node, not in the
pod.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Why might dropwatch show nothing during a SYN flood that's overflowing your accept queue?">
In v6.10 (and through v6.17), `tcp_rcv_state_process()` hands the SYN to
`tcp_conn_request()`, which records the overflow and returns, and then frees
the SYN with `consume_skb()`, not `kfree_skb()`. Drop tracing hooks
`kfree_skb`. I reproduced it: 105 listen overflows in `nstat`, zero matching
`kfree_skb` events, 110 matching `consume_skb` events.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="When does MSG_ZEROCOPY make things slower?">
Below roughly 10 KB per send, where pinning pages and processing completion
notifications cost more than the copy (the kernel doc's own guidance). And on
loopback or to any local socket, where the kernel does a deferred copy anyway.
I measured that: 64 of 64 one-megabyte sends over loopback came back flagged
`SO_EE_CODE_ZEROCOPY_COPIED`.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Where does XDP run relative to the rest of the stack, and what do you lose?">
In the driver's NAPI poll, on the raw DMA buffer, before an skb is allocated,
so before GRO, tc, netfilter and sockets. That's why it can drop or forward at
tens of millions of packets per second per core. You lose everything above it:
no conntrack, no TCP, no socket lookup, and the BPF verifier's limits on what
the program may do. AF_XDP hands frames to user space when you need more logic.
</QA>

<Quiz>
  <Q q="listen(fd, 4) and no accept(). How many connections complete on Linux 6.10?">
    Five. The full check is `sk_ack_backlog > sk_max_ack_backlog`, so the queue
    holds backlog + 1. macOS admitted four in the same test.
  </Q>
  <Q q="softnet_stat column 3 is climbing. What does it mean?">
    time_squeeze: net_rx_action ran out of budget (packets or time) with work
    still queued. The CPU isn't keeping up with its RX queues.
  </Q>
  <Q q="You set SO_RCVBUF to 64 KB. What does getsockopt return?">
    128 KB. The kernel doubles the value to allow for skb overhead, subject to
    rmem_max.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Why does loopback traffic show up in /proc/net/softnet_stat?">
    lo's transmit calls netif_rx(), which queues the skb on the per-CPU
    backlog, and process_backlog delivers it like any other NAPI instance.
  </Q>
