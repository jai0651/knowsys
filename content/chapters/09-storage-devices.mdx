Sooner or later a storage problem reaches you without a code change behind it.
A Postgres box starts committing slowly months after launch. A benchmark on a
new drive looks far better than production. An NVMe drive rated for a million
IOPS delivers twenty thousand.

Each of these comes from the device below the filesystem, and from the gap
between the simple interface it offers and the flash that actually stores your
bytes. Much of the storage advice you've absorbed was written for spinning disks
and predates that gap.

This chapter starts with the block interface, then covers how flash works
underneath it, write amplification and wear, NVMe and queue depth, the ways the
abstraction leaks (drives that lie about flushing among them), and the measured
costs.

<SectionHeading n="01" id="block-device">The block device contract</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="1.1" title="A numbered array of blocks">

Below the filesystem from chapter 08 is a very simple promise. The operating
system sees a **block device**: a numbered array of fixed-size blocks, 512 bytes
or 4 KB each.

Write block 7, read block 7 later, get your bytes back. Overwrite block 7 and
the old contents are gone. A block device promises addressable, fixed-size
blocks that persist, and that's it.

</Sub>

<Sub n="1.2" title="Built for spinning disks">

That interface was designed for spinning disks, and on a spinning disk it's
natural. Block 7 is a physical spot on a platter; to overwrite it, the head
moves there and writes.

<Why>Why did everyone learn to avoid random I/O?</Why>

Because moving the head is the expensive part: a seek, plus waiting for the
platter to come round. Reading scattered blocks could be something like a
hundred times slower than reading them in order.

A great deal of storage advice comes from that one ratio: append-only logs,
Kafka's design, LSM-tree databases like RocksDB and Cassandra, "avoid random
I/O".

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="02" id="flash">Flash can't overwrite in place</SectionHeading>

Flash has no moving parts, so random reads should be cheap. Are they? Mostly.
On the NVMe drive in this laptop a random 4 KB read costs 120.8 µs against
25.7 µs sequential: **4.7 times**, not a hundred.

But flash has a constraint of its own, and it's stranger than a seek.

<Sub n="2.1" title="Pages and erase blocks">

You can write flash one small **page** at a time (a few KB), but only onto a
page that's been erased. And you can only erase a whole **erase block** of
megabytes at once.

<Why>So how would a drive overwrite block 7 in place?</Why>

It can't, not without erasing hundreds of neighbouring pages along with it.
Overwriting one 4 KB page in place is physically impossible.

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.2" title="The Flash Translation Layer">

The drive's answer is to never overwrite at all. Firmware inside the drive, the
**Flash Translation Layer** (FTL), keeps a map from your logical block numbers
to physical pages, and hides everything behind the simple block interface.

/>

Underneath an SSD, essentially none of the block contract is physically true.
Your logical block number is translated to a wildly different physical location,
which moves without telling you, on media that can't overwrite in place and
wears out.

<Callout label="Design implication">
Every surprising SSD behaviour comes out of that indirection. It's a good trick,
and it has a bill: dead pages, garbage collection, write amplification and wear.
The rest of this chapter is that bill.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.3" title="Garbage collection">

Dead pages pile up. What happens when the drive wants them back?

It has to erase their block. But that block usually still holds live pages too,
so the controller copies those somewhere else first. That's **garbage
collection**.

<Steps
  title="Reclaiming one erase block"
  lanes={[
    { t: "Victim block", s: "live + dead pages", icon: "▤" },
    { t: "Copy live", s: "to a fresh block", icon: "⇢" },
    { t: "FTL map", s: "update locations", icon: "⇄" },
    { t: "Erase", s: "the whole block", icon: "✕" },
    { t: "Free pool", s: "ready for writes", icon: "▦" },
  ]}
  steps={[
    [0, "The controller picks a block to reclaim. It holds a mix of **live** pages still in use and **dead** ones left behind by overwrites."],
    [1, "It **copies every live page** to a fresh block. These are writes to flash that you never asked for."],
    [2, "The FTL map is updated so each copied logical block points at its new home."],
    [3, "Now the block holds only dead pages, so the controller **erases the whole block** at once, megabytes in one go."],
    [4, "The block's pages go back to the free pool. The drive gained the dead pages, and paid for them with the copies in step 2."],
  ]}
/>

It happens in the background, and it competes with your I/O. That's why an
SSD's latency varies when you haven't changed anything.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="03" id="write-amplification">Write amplification and wear</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="3.1" title="The arithmetic of reclaiming space">

Because reclaiming space means copying live data, the device writes more bytes
to flash than you handed it. The ratio of flash writes to the writes you asked
for is **write amplification**.

<Predict
  q="The blocks your drive reclaims are three-quarters live. For each page you write, roughly how many pages does the flash end up writing?"
  options={["1", "1.33", "About 4", "75"]}
  answer={2}
>
Freeing one block of four pages means copying three live pages to gain one free
one. So every page you write ends up costing about **four writes** to the flash:
yours, plus three copies. That's a write amplification of about 4.
</Predict>

<Why>Why does it get worse as the drive fills?</Why>

Because fuller drives have fewer mostly-dead blocks to choose from. Every block
the controller can reclaim is mostly live, so every reclaim copies more.

That's how a Postgres box can start committing slowly months after launch with
nothing changed but free space. And it's why a benchmark on a fresh, empty
drive can look far better than the same drive will a year in.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.2" title="Write amplification by workload">

It determines both sustained write throughput and how fast the drive wears out.

<Compare
  cols={["Workload", "Typical WA", "Why"]}
  rows={[
    ["Large sequential writes", "~1.0", "Whole blocks filled and retired together"],
    ["Random 4 KB writes, drive half empty", "~2–3", "Blocks hold a mix of live and dead pages"],
    ["Random writes, drive nearly full", "10 or more", "Little free space; GC copies constantly"],
    ["Aligned, TRIM'd, overprovisioned", "closer to 1", "Controller knows what's really dead"],
  ]}
/>

Note the third row. **A nearly full SSD is dramatically slower at writes**, and
the effect is nonlinear near the end.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Treating free space on an SSD as tidiness. Keeping 10–20% free is a performance
setting: write amplification climbs sharply on a full drive.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.3" title="Wear, and its arithmetic">

Each flash cell survives a limited number of erase cycles: roughly 1,000–3,000
for consumer TLC, more for enterprise. Wear levelling spreads writes so no block
dies early, and the drive reports remaining life through SMART.

The arithmetic is simple, and you should do it once:

your monitoring would tell you until SMART started counting down.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="04" id="nvme">NVMe and queue depth</SectionHeading>

One more thing changed with flash: a drive can work on many requests at once.
**NVMe**, the protocol the OS uses to talk to modern SSDs over PCIe, gives it
deep queues of outstanding requests.

<Sub n="4.1" title="Why NVMe is fast">

| Interface | Queues | Entries per queue | Designed for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SATA** | 1 | 32 | A spinning platter, where deep queues were pointless |
| **NVMe** | Up to 65,535 | Up to 65,536 | Flash over PCIe, with the doorbell register in memory the host can write directly |

<Why>Why does a drive need that many queues?</Why>

Because flash parallelism is most of the performance story. A drive has many
independent channels and dies, and keeping them all busy needs many outstanding
requests.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.3" title="Queue depth is the variable that matters most">

A single-threaded loop of blocking `pread` calls has a queue depth of one. One
request outstanding, most of the device's parallelism idle, and you measure
*latency* while believing you measured *throughput*.

<Why>Why do rated IOPS look so far from what you measure?</Why>

Because rated figures assume deep queues. A drive rated at a million IOPS will
deliver perhaps twenty thousand at queue depth one, and both numbers are honest.

Real throughput needs depth: many threads, asynchronous I/O, or io_uring from
chapter 07.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="05" id="failure">Where the abstraction leaks</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="5.1" title="The write cliff">

Fresh SSDs are fast. They don't stay that way. Sustained random writes fill the
overprovisioned space, GC has to run continuously, and throughput can drop by an
order of magnitude, then stay there.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Benchmarking for thirty seconds on a fresh drive. If you're qualifying storage,
precondition it: fill it, then write randomly for an hour, then start measuring.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.2" title="Devices that lie about flushing">

Many drives have a volatile write cache and acknowledge writes on arrival there.
A `FLUSH CACHE` command is supposed to force persistence.

  title="fsync on a drive that ignores flushes"
  actors={["Your app", "Kernel", "Drive cache", "Flash"]}

    { from: 1, to: 2, label: "FLUSH CACHE", note: "The kernel sends a flush, which is supposed to force the cache onto the flash." },
    { from: 2, to: 1, label: "ok", note: "Some consumer drives have historically **ignored it, or treated it as a no-op**, and report success anyway." },
    { from: 1, to: 0, label: "success", note: "`fsync` returns success. Your app tells the user their data is safe." },
    { from: 2, to: 3, label: "power cut", dashed: true, note: "The power fails before the cache reaches the flash. **The data a successful fsync promised is gone.**" },
  ]}
/>

<Why>Why do enterprise drives cost more?</Why>

Partly for this. They have power-loss protection: a capacitor large enough to
flush the cache during a power failure, and that capacitor is a meaningful part
of the price.

<Callout label="Design implication">
If `fsync` durability matters, it's partly a hardware purchasing decision, and
no amount of correct software substitutes for it.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" id="cost">Random is only 4.7× worse now</SectionHeading>

<Callout label="The machine" kind="note">
Apple M4, macOS 26.5.1, APFS on the internal NVMe. A 3 GB file read with
`F_NOCACHE` and page-aligned buffers so the page cache is really bypassed.
Queue depth is **one** throughout (single-threaded blocking `pread`), so these
are latency numbers, and the throughput column is what one thread achieves, not
what the device can do.
</Callout>

<Sub n="6.1" title="Random versus sequential">

<Cost items={[
  { what: "4 KB sequential read, uncached", value: "25.7 µs", source: "3 GB file, F_NOCACHE, QD1" },
  { what: "4 KB random read, uncached", value: "120.8 µs", source: "same file, random offsets" },
  { what: "Random penalty", value: "4.7×", source: "derived" },
]} />

**4.7×.** On a 7,200 rpm spinning disk that ratio was roughly a hundred,
because a random read meant a seek and a rotation. Flash has no moving parts,
and the remaining gap is readahead and queue behaviour, not physics.

This is the single most out-of-date belief in storage engineering. "Random I/O
is catastrophic, always design for sequential access" was correct advice that is
now merely a mild preference.

<Why>Why are B-trees competitive with LSM-trees again?</Why>

LSM-trees won on spinning disks by converting random writes to sequential ones,
which was worth 100×. On NVMe that conversion is worth about 4.7×, while LSM
compaction adds its own write amplification.

The trade is close now, and modern B-tree engines are competitive again. It's a
design argument settled by hardware changing underneath it; remember that the
next time one feels settled.

</Sub>

<Callout label="Design implication">
Read in large chunks when you can. If you can't, get depth: many small reads in
flight amortise the same fixed costs concurrently. That's exactly what io_uring
exists to make easy.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="07" id="operating">Watching the device</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="7.1" title="What to watch">

```shell
# Latency, queue depth and utilisation per device. Start here.
iostat -xz 1                   # await, aqu-sz, %util, r/s, w/s

# Where is the time: device or queue?
biolatency-bpfcc               # histogram of device service time
biosnoop-bpfcc                 # per-I/O, with process attribution

# Drive health and how much life is left
smartctl -a /dev/nvme0n1 | grep -E 'Percentage Used|Data Units Written'
nvme smart-log /dev/nvme0n1

# macOS
iostat -w 1
sudo fs_usage -f diskio
```

**`aqu-sz` in `iostat` is the number to check first.** A queue depth near 1 with
poor throughput means your application isn't giving the device enough work, and
no amount of faster hardware will help. Depth of 30 with rising `await` means
the device really is the bottleneck.

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.2" title="Getting more out of the device">

1. **Bigger reads and writes.** 40× is available for free if your access pattern
   allows it.
2. **More depth.** Threads, async I/O, or io_uring. A blocking single-threaded
   loop uses a fraction of an NVMe drive.
3. **Keep 10–20% free.** Write amplification climbs sharply on a full drive.
4. **Enable TRIM** so the controller knows what's really dead. `fstrim` on a
   timer is the usual arrangement.
5. **Align to 4 KB.** A misaligned write touches two pages and doubles the work.

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.3" title="What you give up">

| You get | You pay | When the bill arrives |
|---|---|---|
| A flat array of persistent blocks | An FTL doing invisible work underneath | As latency variance during GC |
| Random I/O only 4.7× worse than sequential | Advice tuned for spinning disks is now wrong | As an over-engineered sequential design |
| Enormous parallelism via NVMe queues | Only if you supply queue depth | At 91 MB/s from a drive rated for thousands |
| Wear levelling and transparent remapping | Write amplification, and a finite life | Two years in, per the arithmetic in 3.3 |
| Fast acknowledged writes | A volatile cache that may ignore flushes | On power loss, on consumer hardware |

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.4" title="Symptom, cause, fix">

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Throughput far below the rating, `aqu-sz` near 1 | Queue depth of one | Threads, async I/O or io_uring |
| Small reads slow, device looks idle | Fixed per-request cost dominates | Read in larger chunks |
| Writes slow down months after launch | Drive filling up; write amplification climbing | Keep 10–20% free, enable TRIM |
| Benchmark great, production much slower | Fresh drive, no GC pressure | Precondition before measuring |
| SMART life dropping faster than expected | Write amplification multiplying your writes | Do the TBW arithmetic; cut random writes |
| Data lost after power cut despite `fsync` | Drive ignored the flush | Drives with power-loss protection |

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="08" id="summary">Summary</SectionHeading>

1. **A block device is a flat array of blocks.** The interface was designed for
   spinning disks.
2. **Random I/O was expensive because of the head.** A seek and a rotation made
   it about 100× slower than sequential.
3. **On NVMe, random reads are only 4.7× slower.** Most "avoid random I/O"
   advice now overstates the cost.
4. **Flash writes pages but erases blocks.** So it can't overwrite in place.
5. **The FTL never overwrites.** It writes somewhere fresh, remaps, and marks the
   old page dead.
6. **Garbage collection copies live pages before erasing.** Those copies are
   write amplification.
7. **Write amplification climbs as the drive fills.** Keep 10–20% free.
8. **It also wears the drive out.** 3× amplification turned a five-year drive
   into a two-year one.
9. **Queue depth decides what you measure.** At depth one you see latency, not
   the device's throughput.
10. **Some drives lie about flushing.** Power-loss protection is a hardware
    purchase.

<SectionHeading n="09" id="build">Build this</SectionHeading>

**Find your device's real shape**, then find out how much of it you were using.

- Sweep read size from 4 KB to 4 MB at queue depth 1, bypassing the cache. Plot
  throughput. You should see something like the 40× above.
- Now hold size at 4 KB and sweep *queue depth* from 1 to 64 using threads or
  `io_uring`. Plot throughput again.
- Compare the two curves. Depth probably buys you more than size does, and
  neither on its own gets you near the number on the box.

Then run `fio` with the same parameters and see how close your numbers are. If
they differ a lot, the interesting work is finding out why.

<SectionHeading n="10" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="Why can't an SSD overwrite a block in place?">
Flash is written in pages of a few kilobytes but erased only in blocks of
megabytes, and a cell must be erased before it can be rewritten. So changing
4 KB in place would mean erasing several megabytes.

The controller writes your new data to a fresh page, updates its mapping table,
and marks the old page dead. Garbage collection later copies the surviving pages
out of a block and erases it.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="What is write amplification and why should you care?">
The ratio of bytes actually written to flash versus bytes your application
wrote. Reclaiming space means copying live pages out of blocks being erased, so
the device does extra writing on your behalf.

It matters twice: it caps sustained write throughput, and it consumes endurance.
A workload writing 500 GB a day at 3× amplification puts 1.5 TB a day on the
flash, turning a drive rated for 1,200 TBW into roughly a two-year part.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Your NVMe drive is rated for a million IOPS and you're getting 20,000. What's wrong?">
Queue depth, almost certainly. A single-threaded loop of blocking `pread` calls
has one request outstanding, so you're measuring latency and most of the
device's channels and dies are idle.

Rated IOPS figures assume deep queues. Get depth with threads, asynchronous
I/O, or io_uring. Check `aqu-sz` in `iostat`: near 1 means the application is
the bottleneck, not the hardware.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="How much slower is random I/O than sequential, and how has that changed?">
On this NVMe I measured 120.8 µs for a 4 KB random read against 25.7 µs
sequential at queue depth one, about 4.7×. On a 7,200 rpm disk the ratio was
roughly 100×, because random meant a seek plus rotational latency.

That's a qualitative change, not a quantitative one. Design advice built around
"random I/O is catastrophic" is now overcautious, and it's part of why B-trees
became competitive with LSM-trees again: the sequential conversion LSMs perform
is worth far less than it used to be.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Your storage benchmark looked great and production is much slower. Name three likely reasons.">
First, preconditioning. A fresh drive has plenty of erased blocks and no GC
pressure; after sustained random writes, throughput can drop an order of
magnitude and stay there. Benchmarks that run thirty seconds on a new drive
measure the best case only.

Second, free space. Write amplification climbs sharply past roughly 80–90% full,
and production drives are fuller than test ones.

Third, queue depth and read size. I measured 91 MB/s at 4 KB and 3,608 MB/s at
4 MB on identical hardware: 40× from read size alone. If the benchmark used
large sequential reads and production does small random ones, you're comparing
two different devices.
</QA>

  </Q>

  </Q>
