Two threads each run `count++` a million times on a shared integer. What's
`count` at the end? You'd expect two million, and on a multi-core machine you
probably won't get it.

`count++` isn't one step. It's a load, an add and a store.
Suppose `count` is 5 and both threads load it at nearly the same moment. Each
adds one and gets 6, each stores 6, and one increment has vanished. Nothing
crashed and nothing was reported; the number is just wrong. What you need is a
rule that only one thread at a time may run that load-add-store, the **critical
section**. A **mutex** (short for mutual exclusion) is how you enforce that.

How would you build one? The first idea is a flag: wait while it's set, then set
it and go in.

```cpp
while (locked) {}   // wait
locked = true;      // take it
```

Can you see why that fails? It's the same race again. Both threads can see
`locked == false`, both fall out of the loop, and both set it. Checking and
setting have to happen as a single indivisible step, and chapter 03 showed that
processors provide exactly that: an atomic instruction that changes a value
only if it still holds what you expected, and tells you whether it worked. Loop
on that until you flip the flag from 0 to 1, and you have a working lock, a
**spinlock**.

A spinlock has a cost, and it's mostly in the waiting. A thread that can't get the lock
burns a whole core going round the loop, and if the thread holding the lock has
been descheduled (chapter 06), everyone waiting may spin for a whole time slice
while nothing useful happens. It would be better for a waiter to sleep and be
woken when the lock is free. Sleeping needs the kernel, though, and a syscall
costs hundreds of nanoseconds (chapter 07). You don't want to pay that on every
lock, when most locks are probably free when you ask for them.

Linux's answer is the **futex**, the "fast userspace mutex". The lock stays an
ordinary integer in your memory. If it's free, one atomic instruction takes it
and the kernel never hears about it. Only if another thread holds it does the
waiter make a syscall that says "sleep until this address changes". For the
unlocking thread to know whether anyone needs waking, the integer carries a
little more information than locked or unlocked: free, held, and held with
waiters. That's three states in one word, and it's the layout under
`std::mutex`, Go's `sync.Mutex` and Java's locks on Linux.

So how slow is a lock? Most of us probably picked up "locks are slow, avoid them" somewhere. On
this M4 an uncontended lock and unlock pair cost **4.25 nanoseconds**, about two
and a half times a bare atomic increment, with no kernel involved. What's slow is
**contention**, many threads wanting the same lock at once, and that's mostly what the
rest of this chapter is about. It's why Postgres reports backends waiting
on its internal `LWLock`s, and why CPython's global interpreter lock stops
CPU-bound threads running in parallel.

When I added threads, going from four to eight made the lock *faster* per
operation. Part of that was a bug in my benchmark and part was a real effect I
still can't fully explain; section 5 has both. Before that, the chapter goes
through what a mutex promises, the integer underneath it, what `lock()` does
instruction by instruction, and the ways locks fail: deadlock, priority
inversion and convoys.

<SectionHeading n="01" cmd="man 3 pthread_mutex_lock" id="contract">What a mutex promises, and what it doesn't</SectionHeading>

A mutex promises one thing: at most one thread holds it at a time. Everything
else you might assume, it does not promise.

It says nothing about who gets it next, or how long you'll wait. A thread that
just released a lock and immediately asks for it again will often win it back,
while a thread that's been queued for a millisecond waits longer. That isn't a
defect. Barging like that is what keeps the uncontended path at four
nanoseconds, and the uncontended path is the one that runs a billion times a day.

You do get two things beyond exclusion. Release/acquire ordering: everything a
thread wrote before unlocking is visible to whichever thread locks next, so you can skip extra fences inside a critical section. And conditional
progress: if holders keep releasing, some waiter proceeds. Which one is
unspecified.

<Callout label="The guarantee people bolt on that isn't there">
Ordering. `pthread_mutex_lock` with default attributes gives you no FIFO
guarantee, and neither does `std::mutex`. If your design needs fairness you have
to build it. Assuming fairness you were never given is how a background thread
ends up starved for seconds under load.
</Callout>

A few more things it isn't. It's not reentrant — locking a
`PTHREAD_MUTEX_NORMAL` twice from one thread deadlocks instantly, and
`std::recursive_mutex` existing is usually a sign the ownership model needs
rethinking. It's not priority-aware unless you ask for `PTHREAD_PRIO_INHERIT`.
And nothing associates a mutex with the data it guards; that association lives
only in your head and your reviewers'.

<SectionHeading n="02" cmd="hexdump -C /proc/self/maps" id="layout">One integer, three states</SectionHeading>

Underneath `std::mutex` on Linux is `pthread_mutex_t`, and underneath that is
one 32-bit integer with three meaningful values: 0 free, 1 held with no waiters,
2 held with waiters queued.

<Figure caption="The futex word. Three states, and a syscall that only happens when the value is 2.">

A full `pthread_mutex_t` carries more — an owner TID, a recursion count, a kind field, a
waiter list pointer — but the hot path touches only that first word. Everything
else is for error checking, recursion and robustness, and none of it is read
when the lock is uncontended.

Two states would be enough for correctness: free and held. A third exists so unlock can tell whether anyone needs waking. With two states, unlock would
have to call into the kernel every single time just in case. At roughly a
microsecond per syscall against four nanoseconds for the fast path, that's a
couple of hundred times the cost, paid on every release, to handle a case that
almost never happens. That third state turns it into a branch on a value you already have in a
register.

<SectionHeading n="03" cmd="perf record -e syscalls:sys_enter_futex" id="hot-path">What happens when you call lock()</SectionHeading>

Here's the canonical algorithm, from Drepper's paper. It's about twenty lines
and worth reading twice.

<SourceRead
  file="Futexes Are Tricky — Ulrich Drepper, 2011"
  repo="mutex3"
  tag="the canonical algorithm"
  href="https://www.akkadia.org/drepper/futex.pdf"
>
```c
void lock(int *futex) {
    int c;
    /* Fast path: 0 -> 1. If it works, we are done. No syscall. */
    if ((c = cmpxchg(futex, 0, 1)) != 0) {
        do {
            /* Someone holds it. Announce that a waiter exists (state 2)
               and sleep until the value stops being 2.                   */
            if (c == 2 || cmpxchg(futex, 1, 2) != 0)
                futex_wait(futex, 2);
        } while ((c = cmpxchg(futex, 0, 2)) != 0);
    }
}

void unlock(int *futex) {
    /* If the value was 2, someone is asleep and has to be woken.
       If it was 1, nobody is waiting and we skip the kernel entirely. */
    if (atomic_fetch_sub(futex, 1) != 1) {
        *futex = 0;
        futex_wake(futex, 1);
    }
}
```
</SourceRead>

That second function is the clever half. `atomic_fetch_sub` returns the
*previous* value, so comparing it against 1 is asking "was I the only one
interested?" When the answer is yes — overwhelmingly the common case — the
function returns without ever entering the kernel. That's where the four
nanoseconds comes from.

When a thread does have to wait, `futex_wait` hashes the *physical* address of
the futex word into a global table of wait queues and sleeps the caller on that
bucket. Hashing on the physical address is what lets two processes share a futex
in shared memory without agreeing on a virtual address. No kernel state about the lock survives between calls — the word in userspace *is* the lock, and
all the kernel manages is the queue of disappointed threads.

There's a race hiding in there that took me a re-read to see. `futex_wait(addr,
expected)` passes the value the caller expects, and the kernel compares it under
a lock before sleeping. Without that comparison, A could release the lock between
B's `cmpxchg` and B's syscall — and B would then sleep forever on a free lock
with nobody left to wake it. That value check closes the window.

macOS has no futex. `os_unfair_lock` uses `__ulock_wait`/`__ulock_wake`, and
Windows uses `WaitOnAddress`. Different syscalls, same shape: an atomic in
userspace, a kernel queue keyed by address, and a value check to close that race.

<SectionHeading n="04" cmd="gdb -p $PID -ex 'thread apply all bt'" id="failure">Deadlock, inversion, and the convoy</SectionHeading>

Deadlock everyone can picture — two threads, two locks, opposite order. The
useful part is that there's exactly one prevention strategy that scales: a
global lock order. Number your locks, document the order, never acquire
downward. `lockdep` in the kernel and ThreadSanitizer in userspace both enforce
it by watching the acquisition graph at runtime. Timeouts are not a cure; a
`try_lock` with backoff turns a deadlock into a livelock and a latency spike,
and the bug survives to production.

Priority inversion is the one that grounded a Mars lander. A low-priority thread
holds a lock, a high-priority thread wants it, and a medium-priority thread that
wants nothing preempts the holder — so the high-priority thread is now waiting
on a thread that isn't running. Pathfinder's fix in 1997 is still the fix:
priority inheritance, where the holder is temporarily boosted to the priority of
its highest waiter. Linux implements this with PI futexes, a genuinely separate
kernel path with ownership recorded in the futex word itself.

Convoys are what I find hardest to spot. A holder gets descheduled
mid-critical-section, every other thread piles into the wait queue, and when the
holder is rescheduled and releases, one waiter wakes, runs, and the pattern
repeats — the whole group now moving in lockstep, one context switch per handoff.
Throughput can look fine while latency falls apart, because the system is still
doing work. It's just doing it in a queue whose service order nobody chose.

<Sub n="5.2" title="Where my benchmark was wrong">

My first contention benchmark said this:

```
2 threads    67.1 ns per critical section
4 threads    24.6 ns
8 threads    18.9 ns
```

Contention getting *cheaper* as I added threads. I stared at that for a while — longer than I'd like to admit — before spotting
it: I'd fixed the *total* work at 2,000,000 operations and split
it across threads, so eight threads each did 250,000 while one thread did two
million. Thread startup was in the timer too. I wasn't measuring contention, I
was measuring less work per thread.

Fixed work per thread instead, so adding threads adds work:

<Estimate
  rows={[
    { label: "1 thread", math: "no contention at all", value: "4.10 ns" },
    { label: "2 threads", math: "hand-off every operation", value: "6.24 ns" },
    { label: "4 threads", math: "unstable — see below", value: "31–63 ns" },
    { label: "8 threads", math: "waiters park in the kernel", value: "15.9 ns" },
  ]}
/>

Better, and the 8-threads-beats-4 result survived the fix. That one is real.

Once enough threads are waiting they stop spinning and park, and a parked thread
doesn't fight for the cache line. One thread then runs a long batch of critical
sections unopposed. Throughput improves and fairness is destroyed. That's a
convoy, and it's the first time I've had one show up as a number instead of as
a description.

That four-thread row is the part I can't close out. It swings between 31 and 63
nanoseconds across runs while every other row is stable to two decimal places.
My best guess is probably core placement — the M4 has performance and efficiency cores,
and four threads sits right at the boundary where the scheduler's choice changes
the answer. I haven't proved it. Pinning threads to core types would settle it
and macOS makes that awkward, so it's sitting there unresolved. I'm flagging it
because an unexplained 2× is exactly the kind of thing benchmarks quietly
average away.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.3" title="The thing that costs more than the lock">

Two threads writing to *different* variables in the *same* cache line serialise
as completely as if they shared a lock, with none of the protection.

<TryIt what="Two counters, one cache line, then padded apart" lang="cpp" toolchain="clang++ 21 -O2 -pthread, Apple M4">
```cpp
constexpr int LINE = 128;                  // M-series. On x86-64 it's 64.

std::atomic<long> packed[8] = {};          // 64 bytes, all inside one line

struct alignas(LINE) Padded { std::atomic<long> v{0}; };
std::vector<Padded> padded(8);             // one counter per line

// Each thread hammers its OWN counter 5,000,000 times.
// No thread ever touches another thread's data.
for (int t = 0; t < T; t++)
    threads.emplace_back([&, t] {
        for (long i = 0; i < 5'000'000; i++)
            packed[t].fetch_add(1, std::memory_order_relaxed);
    });
```

<Output>
| threads | shared line | padded | penalty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 3.0 ns | 0.82 ns | 3.7× |
| 4 | 5.1 ns | 0.45 ns | 11× |
| 8 | 14.6 ns | 0.27 ns | **55×** |

Stable within 5% over six runs. Look at the padded column — it gets *faster*

At eight threads one `alignas(128)` is worth fifty-five times. No algorithm
changed, no lock was involved, and the only difference is which cache line the
bytes landed in.

Note the 128. I'd have written 64 out of habit — it's the x86-64 value and it's
what most padding code hardcodes — and on this chip that fixes nothing at all,
because two 64-byte-padded counters still share one line. `sysctl -n
hw.cachelinesize` took two seconds and I should have run it first.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" cmd="perf lock contention --threads" id="operating">Finding the lock that's hurting you</SectionHeading>

When a lock is hurting you, the first thing to reach for is
`perf lock contention --threads`, because it ranks contended locks by total wait
time. That answers "where is the time going" far more directly than a CPU
profile does — a blocked thread burns no cycles, so it never shows up in a flame
graph at all. Off-CPU analysis, via `perf record -e sched:sched_switch -g`, is
the other half.

```shell
perf lock record ./yourapp && perf lock contention --threads
perf record -e sched:sched_switch -g ./yourapp
perf stat -e cache-misses,cache-references ./yourapp     # lock, or line?
xcrun xctrace record --template 'System Trace' --launch ./yourapp   # macOS
```

Symptoms are distinctive enough to shortcut most of this. A high p99 with a
flat p50 and flat CPU is a convoy. Throughput falling as you add cores is false
sharing or a global lock — check cache misses per operation, then go and look at
your struct layout. One thread starving for seconds means you assumed FIFO
somewhere. A latency spike under a priority mix means checking whether
`PTHREAD_PRIO_INHERIT` is set. A hang with every thread idle is a deadlock, and
`gdb thread apply all bt` will show you the cycle.

<SectionHeading n="07" cmd="git init ./weekend-project" id="build">Build this</SectionHeading>

Write a mutex. Drepper's three-state algorithm is about twenty lines, and
implementing it against `futex(2)` or `__ulock_wait` directly teaches more than
any amount of reading it.

Then do the part people skip: make it fair and measure what fairness costs. Add
a ticket — each waiter takes a number, unlock wakes the holder of the next one.
Benchmark both against `std::mutex` at 2, 4 and 8 threads, and plot throughput
*and* the spread of per-thread completion times.

You should find the fair lock is slower on throughput and dramatically tighter
on spread. That trade is the entire reason the default is unfair, and your own
numbers for it are worth more than my telling you.

<SectionHeading n="10" cmd="./drill --level all" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="Roughly what does an uncontended mutex cost?">
A handful of nanoseconds, and no syscall. On an M4 I measured 4.25 ns for lock
plus unlock against 1.60 ns for a bare atomic increment. More important is the *no syscall* half: the fast path is a compare-exchange in
userspace, so the kernel is never involved unless another thread already holds
the lock.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Why does a futex-based mutex need three states and not two?">
So that unlock can tell whether anybody needs waking. With free and held only,
every unlock would have to make a `futex_wake` syscall just in case a waiter
existed. A third state — held-with-waiters — turns that into a check of a value the
unlocking thread already has in a register, so the common case costs a
branch instead of about a microsecond.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Your service has a flat p50 and a p99 forty times higher. CPU is unremarkable. Where do you look?">
Off-CPU time, not a CPU profile. A thread blocked on a lock burns no cycles, so
it's invisible in a flame graph. `perf lock contention` ranks locks by total
wait time; `perf sched` shows where threads went to sleep. Flat median, terrible
tail, unremarkable utilisation is what a convoy looks like from outside.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Two threads increment two different counters and never touch each other's data. Adding the second thread makes it slower. Why?">
False sharing. Both counters sit in the same cache line, and coherence works at
line granularity, so each write invalidates the other core's copy of the whole
line. Logically independent, physically serialised.

Padding each counter onto its own line with `alignas` fixes it. On an M4 at
eight threads I measured 14.6 ns per operation shared against 0.27 ns padded —
about 55×. Note the line is 128 bytes there, not 64, so padding to 64 fixes
nothing on that hardware.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Explain the race that futex_wait's value argument prevents.">
Thread B finds the lock held, sets the word to 2, and is about to call
`futex_wait`. Between those two steps thread A unlocks: it sees the 2, sets the
word to 0 and calls `futex_wake` — but B isn't in the queue yet, so the wake
hits nothing. B then calls `futex_wait` and sleeps on a lock that's free, with
nobody remaining to wake it.

`futex_wait(addr, expected)` closes this. Under the bucket lock, the kernel
compares `*addr` against `expected` and only sleeps if they still match. B
passes 2, the word now reads 0, the comparison fails, and the syscall returns
immediately instead of parking.
</QA>

<SectionHeading n="11" cmd="cat REFERENCES.md" id="resources">Go deeper</SectionHeading>

If you take four things from this chapter: an uncontended mutex is about four
nanoseconds and never enters the kernel, so "locks are slow" is describing a
path you may never hit. There's no fairness guarantee and assuming one is a real
bug. False sharing can cost fifty-five times and it isn't a locking problem at
all — check your cache line size before you pad, because 64 is wrong on a lot of
current hardware. And when latency goes bad, look at off-CPU time instead of at
a CPU profile.

<Quiz>
  <Q q="A thread unlocks and immediately relocks. Another thread has been queued for 1 ms. Who probably wins?">
    The one that just released. Barging is permitted and common — there's no
    FIFO guarantee — and the relocking thread already has the line in its cache.
  </Q>
  <Q q="You pad a struct to 64 bytes to stop false sharing. On an Apple M4, what happens?">
    Nothing improves. The line is 128 bytes, so two 64-byte-padded counters
    still land in one line.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Why is an uncontended unlock allowed to skip the kernel entirely?">
    Because `fetch_sub` returns the previous value. If it was 1, the thread was
    the only party interested and there is no queue to wake.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Does a mutex give you any memory-ordering guarantee?">
    Yes — release on unlock, acquire on lock. Everything written before an
    unlock is visible to the next thread that locks.
  </Q>
</Quiz>
