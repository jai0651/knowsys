Sooner or later you'll be handed a blank page and a sentence: "we need a
service that stores chat history", or "design a notifications system". It
happens in design reviews, in planning for a new product, and in every
system design interview.

The most common failure is probably drawing boxes first. A load balancer, some app servers,
a cache, a queue, a sharded database: it looks like architecture, and none of
it was chosen for a reason. The design that comes out is either far more
complicated than the load needs, or it breaks on the one number nobody
worked out.

This chapter is a method for avoiding both. It turns requirements into
numbers, gives you reference numbers to compare them against (measured, with
the machine named), works a full estimate, and then shows how each component
you might draw is the answer to a specific number crossing a specific line.

<SectionHeading n="01" id="method">The method in one picture</SectionHeading>

System design isn't a search for the right architecture. It's a series of
questions, each answered with a number, and each answer narrowing what the
next step can be.

<Why>Why estimate before drawing anything?</Why>

Because the numbers decide which boxes exist. A service doing 50 writes a
second and one doing 50,000 aren't the same design with different server
counts. One fits on a single Postgres instance with room to spare; the other
may need partitioning, batching and a queue. Draw first and you'll design for
whichever one you happened to imagine.

<Callout label="Design implication">
Every component in a design should have a sentence of the form "this is here
because *number* exceeds *limit*". If you can't write that sentence for a box,
it's decoration, and decoration costs you an on-call rotation.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="02" id="requirements">Requirements you can put a number on</SectionHeading>

Requirements come in two kinds. **Functional** ones say what the system does:
send a message, load a conversation, search history. **Non-functional** ones
say how well: how many, how fast, how durable. The second kind decides the
architecture.

<Sub n="2.1" title="The questions that change the design">

| Question | Example answer | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| How many users, and how active? | 20M daily active users | Every other estimate |
| Read-to-write ratio? | 3 reads per write | Whether caching or write throughput dominates |
| How much data per item, and for how long? | 300 B per message, kept forever | Storage growth, whether one node can hold it |
| Latency target, and at which percentile? | p99 under 200 ms to load a conversation | What can sit on the read path |
| Durability? | An acknowledged message is never lost | Synchronous replication, fsync on commit |
| Consistency? | A sender sees their own message at once | Where reads can be served from |
| Availability target? | 99.9% of requests succeed | Redundancy, and what degrades first |
| Peak shape? | Evening peak about 3× the daily average | Capacity you provision |

<Why>Why ask about percentiles and not just "fast"?</Why>

Because the average hides the requests that make users leave. A p99 target
says what the slowest 1% may take, and that's what decides whether a
synchronous call to a slow dependency is allowed on the path.
[Chapter 16](/topics/16-contention-tail-latency) shows how quickly the tail
grows with load and fan-out.

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.2" title="Write the assumptions down">

You'll rarely be given every number. Make an assumption, say it out loud, and
write it next to the estimate. "Assuming a 3× evening peak" is a design input
someone can correct. A silent assumption is a bug.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Designing for the average. Capacity has to cover the peak, and the peak of a
consumer product can be several times its daily average. Estimate from the
average, then multiply by a stated peak factor before comparing with any
limit.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="03" id="numbers">Numbers to reason with</SectionHeading>

An estimate is only useful if you have something to compare it with. You need
a feel for what one machine, one disk, one database node and one network hop
can do.

<Sub n="3.1" title="Time and size conversions">

A few conversions carry most estimates:

| Quantity | Exact | Round to |
|---|---|---|
| Seconds in a day | 86,400 | 10⁵ |
| Seconds in a 30-day month | 2,592,000 | 2.5 × 10⁶ |
| Seconds in a year | 31,557,600 | 3 × 10⁷ |
| 1 million requests a day | 11.6 per second | 10/s |
| 1 billion requests a day | 11,574 per second | 10⁴/s |
| 2¹⁰, 2²⁰, 2³⁰, 2⁴⁰ bytes | KiB, MiB, GiB, TiB | 10³, 10⁶, 10⁹, 10¹² |

  answer={1}
>
10M × 50 = 500M requests a day. Divide by 86,400, or by 10⁵ to estimate:
about **5,800 a second** on average. With a 3× peak you'd provision for about
17,000. Round early and you'll still land within a factor of two, which is all
an estimate needs.
</Predict>

</Sub>

widely circulated as "latency numbers every programmer should know". Its

Linux VM: linuxkit 6.10.14 aarch64 in a shared 4-CPU container on an Apple
M4, gcc 13.3 `-O2`, median of 3 runs. M4: macOS 26.5.1, median of 3.

<Why>Why is the VM's fsync 60 times faster than the Mac's?</Why>

Because they're not doing the same thing. On macOS, plain `fsync` (20 µs in
the same test) doesn't force the drive to flush its cache; `F_FULLFSYNC` does,
and that's the 3.9 ms. The VM's `fdatasync` lands in a virtual disk on the
host, so it's a floor, not a promise of durability. Real server SSDs with
power-loss protection sit in between. Measure the disk you'll actually run on
before you size anything that commits per request.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.3" title="Throughput, measured">

The other half is how much one node can do. These are one small node's
numbers, and they're meant as orders of magnitude, not as benchmarks:

<Compare
  cols={["Workload", "Throughput", "Setup"]}
  rows={[
    ["Redis GET, no pipelining", "238,000 ops/s", "redis-benchmark, 50 clients"],
    ["Redis GET, pipelined 16 deep", "2,380,000 ops/s", "redis-benchmark, 50 clients"],
    ["Postgres primary-key SELECT (pgbench -S)", "16,000 tps / 44,000 tps", "1 client / 8 clients"],
    ["Postgres: last 50 messages of a conversation", "12,000 / 37,800 queries/s", "1 client / 8; 1M rows, in RAM"],
    ["Postgres single-row INSERT, commit per row", "35,800 rows/s", "8 clients"],
    ["Same, synchronous_commit = off", "69,900 rows/s", "8 clients"],
    ["Postgres INSERT in batches of 100 rows", "278,800 rows/s", "8 clients"],
    ["Postgres pgbench TPC-B-like (scale 10)", "4,600 tps", "8 clients; contended rows"],
  ]}
/>

PostgreSQL 16.15 and Redis 7.0.15 on the Linux VM above, `shared_buffers =
128MB`, median of 3 runs of 10–15 s. The VM's cheap fsync flatters every
commit-per-row number; on a disk with millisecond flushes, group commit has to
do much more of the work.

<Why>Why is the TPC-B-like number so much lower than plain inserts?</Why>

Because every pgbench TPC-B transaction updates one of only ten `branches`
rows at scale 10, so eight clients spend their time waiting on each other's
row locks. That's the difference between a workload's raw cost and its
contention, and designs fail on the second one. (The mechanics are in
[chapter 13](/topics/13-locks).)

<Callout label="Design implication">
Batching is the biggest lever on write throughput. The same node took 7.8×
more rows a second in batches of 100 than one row per commit. Whenever a
requirement allows writes to be grouped (a queue consumer, a buffer flushed
every 50 ms), the write path gets an order of magnitude cheaper.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.4" title="Distance">

Some latency no engineering removes. Light in optical fibre travels at about
two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum, roughly 200,000 km a second, which is
about **1 ms of round trip per 100 km**.

| Hop | Distance | Round trip, physics alone |
|---|---|---|
| Same machine, loopback | — | 31 µs measured above (software, not distance) |
| Between AWS Availability Zones | "within 100 km (60 miles) of each other" ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/global-infrastructure/regions_az/)) | up to about 1 ms |
| New York to London | about 5,600 km | at least 56 ms |
| Los Angeles to Tokyo | about 8,800 km | at least 88 ms |

Real paths aren't great circles, so real round trips are longer. The point is
the floor: a design that makes three sequential cross-ocean calls on a request
has spent about a third of a second before any code runs.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="04" id="estimate">Capacity first: a worked estimate</SectionHeading>

Here's the method applied to one system: storing and serving chat message
history. The numbers are assumptions, stated, so you can change any of them.

<Sub n="4.1" title="The requirements">

- 20 million daily active users.
- Each sends 40 messages a day, averaging 300 bytes with metadata.
- Each opens 100 conversations a day; opening one loads its last 50 messages.
- Messages are kept forever. An acknowledged message must never be lost.
- p99 under 200 ms to open a conversation. Evening peak is 3× the average.

</Sub>

<Predict
  q="Compare these estimates with the measured single-node numbers in section 3.3. Which requirement is the first to rule out a single Postgres instance?"
  options={[
    "Peak writes: 28,000 a second",
    "Peak reads: 69,000 a second",
    "Storage: about 100 TB a year",
    "None; one node can do all of it",
  ]}
  answer={2}
>
Writes at 28,000 a second are uncomfortably close to the 35,800 measured on a
small node, but batching (278,800 rows a second) gives them plenty of room.
Reads at 69,000 a second exceed one node's 37,800, but a cache or two read
replicas cover that. Storage has no such trick: 99 TB in the first year, and
growing every year, doesn't fit on one machine. **Storage forces
partitioning**, and once the data is partitioned, reads and writes are spread
across the partitions too.
</Predict>

<Why>Why did bandwidth show up as a big number?</Why>

Because every conversation load returns 50 messages, so 69,000 loads a
second is 1 GB/s of payload leaving the storage tier, before protocol
overhead. That's a reason to cache recent messages close to the app servers,
return fewer messages per page, and compress responses. The request count
alone would never have told you.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.3" title="Working set and connections">

Two more estimates decide the cache and the connection pools.

**Working set.** Most conversation loads are of recent, active conversations.
If the cache holds the last 50 messages of each conversation active today,
say 20 million conversations, that's 20M × 50 × 300 B = 300 GB. If a tenth of
them take most of the reads, 30 GB of Redis covers the hot set.

**Connections.** Little's Law ([chapter 16](/topics/16-contention-tail-latency))
says requests in flight = arrival rate × time in system. At 69,000 reads a
second and 0.2 ms per query, that's about 14 queries in flight at once. At
20 ms, because the database is struggling, it's 1,400, which is more
connections than a Postgres node should hold. Size pools and set timeouts for
the slow case, not the fast one.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Stopping at requests per second. QPS is the number everyone computes and the
one that rarely decides a design. Storage growth, bytes per second, working-set
size and in-flight requests are where designs usually break, and each takes one
more line of arithmetic.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="05" id="boxes">Which box to draw</SectionHeading>

With the numbers in hand, the boxes follow. Start from the simplest thing:
one stateless service in front of one database. Then add components only when
an estimate crosses a line.

<Sub n="5.1" title="Each component answers a number">

| Component | Add it when | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| **More app servers** behind a load balancer | CPU or memory on one server is the limit, or you need to survive losing one | Almost nothing if the servers are stateless |
| **Read replicas** | Reads exceed one node, and slightly stale reads are acceptable | Replication lag; read-your-writes needs routing |
| **Cache** (Redis, memcached) | A hot working set is read far more than written, or a read is expensive | Invalidation, stampedes, a second source of truth |
| **Queue** (Kafka, SQS) | Work can happen later, bursts exceed steady capacity, or several consumers need the same events | Asynchrony: you need idempotent consumers and a way to see lag |
| **Partitioning** | Data or write rate exceeds one node | Cross-partition queries, rebalancing, hot keys |
| **Object storage** (S3) | Large blobs, or cold data read rarely | Higher latency per request, no in-place updates |
| **CDN** | The same bytes are read by many users far from the servers | Cache invalidation at the edge |
| **Search index** | Queries need full text or many filter combinations | A second copy to keep in sync |

<Why>Why not add them all up front, to be safe?</Why>

Because each one is a new way to fail. A cache can serve stale data or fall
over and send its entire load to the database at once. A queue can back up
for hours without anyone noticing. Partitioning makes every query that
crosses partitions slower and every migration harder. Complexity is a cost you
pay on every deploy and every incident, so you pay it only for a number.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.2" title="How far one box goes">

Simple designs probably go further than you'd expect. Nick Craver's
[Stack Overflow: The Architecture – 2016 Edition](https://nickcraver.com/blog/2016/02/17/stack-overflow-the-architecture-2016-edition/)
reported, for one day in February 2016, 209,420,973 HTTP requests to the load
balancers and 504,816,843 SQL queries, served by 11 web servers, 4 SQL
Servers in two clusters and 2 Redis servers. That's about 2,400 requests and
5,800 queries a second on average. Craver adds that they're "down to needing
only 1 web server", with the caveat "I'm not saying it's a good idea."

<Callout label="Design implication">
Before partitioning anything, check whether one well-indexed database on a
large machine, plus a replica for failover, meets the numbers. For most
internal systems and many products it does, and the design you don't build is
the one that never pages you.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Why>Why partition by conversation and not by user?</Why>

Because the dominant query is "the last 50 messages in this conversation".
Partitioning by conversation makes it a single-partition index scan. By user,
a group conversation's messages would be scattered across every member's
partition, and every load would be a fan-out query. The partition key should
be whatever the hottest query filters on.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" id="access-patterns">Access patterns decide storage</SectionHeading>

After the numbers, the second thing that decides a design is the shape of the
reads and writes: which queries run, how often, and what they touch.

<Sub n="6.1" title="Write the queries next to the API">

For each call in the API, write the query it runs. For the chat system:

| API call | Query | Rate at peak | Needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| `POST /conversations/:id/messages` | Insert one row | 28,000/s | Durable commit, monotonically ordered IDs per conversation |
| `GET /conversations/:id/messages?before=…` | Last 50 by `(conversation_id, id)` | 69,000/s | Composite index, cache |
| `GET /users/:id/conversations` | Conversations by last activity | Lower | A separate, per-user index |
| `GET /search?q=…` | Full-text over a user's messages | Low | A search index, updated asynchronously |

<Why>Why does the third row need a separate index?</Why>

Because it's keyed by user, and the messages are partitioned by conversation.
Any query that doesn't filter on the partition key has to ask every
partition, or be served from a second structure keyed the way it reads. That's
the general pattern: one copy of the data per access pattern that matters,
kept in sync by the write path or by a stream of changes.

</Sub>

<Sub n="6.2" title="Fan-out on write, or on read">

The biggest access-pattern decision in feed-like systems is *when* to do the
work of assembling what a user sees. Twitter's home timeline is the
well-documented example. From Raffi Krikorian's
[Timelines at Scale](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/Twitter-Timeline-Scalability/)
talk (2013), as summarised by
[High Scalability](https://highscalability.com/the-architecture-twitter-uses-to-deal-with-150m-active-users/):
"300K QPS are spent reading timelines and only 6000 requests per second are
spent on writes." With reads 50 times more frequent than writes, Twitter did
the work at write time:

<StepSequence
  title="Fan-out on write: one tweet delivered to followers' timelines"
  actors={["Author", "Write API", "Fan-out", "Timeline cache", "Follower"]}
  messages={[
    { from: 0, to: 1, label: "POST tweet", note: "The author posts. The tweet is stored once, durably, by ID." },
    { from: 1, to: 2, label: "enqueue fan-out", note: "The write API hands the tweet ID to a fan-out service and returns. The author doesn't wait for delivery." },
    { from: 2, to: 3, label: "insert ID × followers", note: "Fan-out looks up the author's followers and inserts the tweet ID into **each follower's timeline** in a Redis cluster. For an account with many followers, one tweet is many thousands of inserts." },
    { from: 4, to: 3, label: "GET home timeline", note: "A follower opens the app. Their timeline is **already assembled**: one read of a list of IDs, then a batch fetch of the tweets." },
    { from: 2, to: 2, label: "skip huge accounts", note: "For accounts with tens of millions of followers the fan-out is too slow. Their tweets are **merged in at read time** instead, a hybrid of the two strategies." },
  ]}
/>

The summary gives the details: each home timeline is capped at 800 entries in
Redis, fan-out aims to finish "under 5 seconds, but it doesn't always work,
especially when celebrities tweet," and for accounts like Lady Gaga's, with 31
million followers at the time, the answer was to "merge in her timeline at
read time".

| Strategy | Work happens | Read cost | Write cost | Breaks when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fan-out on write** | When content is created | One lookup | One insert per follower | An author has millions of followers |
| **Fan-out on read** | When a feed is loaded | One query per followed source, then merge | One insert | A reader follows thousands of sources, or reads dominate |
| **Hybrid** | Write for most authors, read for the largest | Lookup plus a small merge | Bounded | You need to maintain both paths |

<Callout label="Design implication">
Put the expensive work on the side of the ratio that happens less often. If
reads outnumber writes 50 to 1, precompute at write time. If a single write
would touch millions of rows, don't. Most real systems end up hybrid, and
the threshold between the two paths is itself a number to estimate.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="07" id="deep-dive">The deep dive: latency budgets and failure</SectionHeading>

Once the boxes are drawn, two passes finish the design: one to check the
latency target is reachable, and one to check what happens when each box
misbehaves.

<Sub n="7.1" title="Spend the latency budget">

A p99 target is a budget, and every hop on the request path spends some of
it. Walk the path of one conversation load:

<Steps
  title="Where 200 ms goes on one conversation load"
  lanes={[

    [2, "The app server authenticates the request and decides what to fetch. Keep it to one round of calls: **each sequential dependency adds its own p99** to yours."],

    [4, "A cache miss goes to the conversation's partition: **0.083 ms** for the query itself in the test above, plus the network. At p99 with a loaded database, budget milliseconds, not microseconds."],
    [0, "The budget works out: most of 200 ms is left for the user's own network and for the tail. The design has room, **as long as nothing on the path is a synchronous call to a slow or remote dependency**."],

/>

<Why>Why count sequential calls and not total calls?</Why>

Because parallel calls cost you the slowest one, while sequential calls cost
you the sum. Three sequential calls of 20 ms p99 each can take 60 ms at the
tail. Three parallel ones cost about the slowest of three, which is
still a tail, but only one of them. [Chapter 16](/topics/16-contention-tail-latency)
covers what fan-out does to the tail.

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.2" title="Walk each box's failure">

For each component, ask three questions: what if it's slow, what if it's
down, and what if it's overloaded? For the chat design:

| Component | If it's down | The design's answer |
|---|---|---|
| App server | Its requests fail | Stateless; the load balancer routes around it |
| Cache | Every read goes to the database: a stampede | Database sized for a partial cold cache; request coalescing; cache replicas |
| Database primary for one partition | Writes to those conversations fail | Synchronous standby, automatic failover; other partitions unaffected |
| Fan-out or search indexer | New messages aren't searchable yet | Asynchronous by design; alert on lag, not on errors |
| Whole region | Everything fails | A decision: accept it within the SLO, or pay for a second region |

<Why>Why does the cache row matter so much?</Why>

Because a cache that absorbs most reads means the database behind it was
sized for the few that get through. When the cache empties, after a restart
or a failover, the database suddenly gets the full read rate. A cache is a
load reducer until it fails, and then it's a load multiplier. Timeouts, retry
budgets and load shedding for moments like that are covered in
[chapter 40](/topics/40-reliability).

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="08" id="write-it-down">Writing it down</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="8.1" title="The design document">

A design that lives only in a whiteboard photo can't be reviewed. The
document doesn't need to be long; it needs to show its reasoning:

1. **Requirements**, with every number and every assumption.
2. **Estimates**: peak QPS, storage per year, bandwidth, working set.
3. **API and data model**, with the query each call runs.
4. **The design**, with the "this is here because" sentence for each component.
5. **Alternatives considered**, and the number that ruled each one out.
6. **Failure modes** from section 7.2.
7. **What you'll measure** to know it's working: the SLIs, and the capacity
   metrics that tell you when the next step in section 5.3 is due.

</Sub>

<Sub n="8.2" title="Symptom, cause, fix in design reviews">

| Symptom in the design | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Five services and a queue for a system with 50 requests a second | Boxes drawn before numbers | Estimate; start from one service and one database |
| "We'll shard later" with 100 TB a year in the estimate | Storage never estimated | Choose the partition key now, from the hottest query |
| p99 target unreachable on paper | Sequential calls on the request path | Parallelise, precompute, or move the call off the path |
| Cache hit rate assumed at 99% with no working-set estimate | Working set never computed | Estimate the hot set; size the database for a cold cache |
| Queue added "for scale" | No burst or asynchrony requirement | Remove it, or state the burst it absorbs |
| Estimates quoted as averages | No peak factor | Multiply by a stated peak before comparing with limits |
| Benchmarks borrowed from a blog | Nobody measured on the target hardware | Measure one node for the hottest query and the write path |

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Using someone else's throughput numbers. Every figure in section 3 came from
one small shared VM, and the fsync number alone differs 60× between the two
machines measured here. Run pgbench or redis-benchmark with your own query
shape on the instance type you'll deploy, before the design depends on it.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="09" id="summary">Summary</SectionHeading>

1. **Numbers decide the boxes.** Estimate before drawing, and give every
   component a reason of the form "this number exceeds that limit".
2. **Non-functional requirements shape the architecture.** Users, ratio,
   size, latency percentile, durability and peak factor are the inputs.
3. **State every assumption.** A written assumption can be corrected; a silent
   one is a bug.
4. **86,400 seconds a day, about 10⁵.** One million requests a day is about
   12 a second; one billion is about 12,000.
5. **Know one node's limits, measured.** On the test VM: Redis 238k GETs a
   second, Postgres 36k single-row commits, 279k rows a second in batches.
6. **Batching is the biggest write lever.** It was 7.8× on the same node.
7. **QPS rarely decides the design.** In the worked example storage did, at
   about 100 TB a year; bandwidth and working set came next.
8. **Access patterns choose the partition key.** Partition by what the hottest
   query filters on, and keep a second copy per other important pattern.
9. **Put expensive work on the rarer side of the ratio.** Fan-out on write
   when reads dominate, on read for the accounts where writes explode.
10. **Finish with the latency budget and the failure walk.** Sequential calls
    add their tails, and a cache that fails multiplies load.

<SectionHeading n="10" id="build">Build this</SectionHeading>

**An estimate you can check.**

- Pick a system you run. Write its requirements as in section 2.1 and estimate
  peak QPS, storage per year, bandwidth and working set.
- Measure one node: run `pgbench` with a custom script containing your hottest
  read and your write, and `redis-benchmark` with your value size. Compare with
  the estimate.
- Find the first number that crosses a single-node limit. Is it the one your
  current architecture was built around?
- Write the one-page design document from section 8.1 for the next 10× of
  growth, with a number next to every component you'd add.

<SectionHeading n="11" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="How do you turn daily active users into requests per second?">
Multiply users by requests per user per day, then divide by 86,400 (or 10⁵
for a quick estimate). 10 million users making 50 requests each is 500 million
a day, about 5,800 a second on average. Then multiply by a stated peak factor,
say 3×, before comparing with any capacity limit.
</QA>

<QA level="beginner" q="When would you add a cache?">
When a hot working set is read much more often than it's written, or when a
read is expensive to compute, and the estimate shows the database can't serve
the read rate or the latency target alone. Estimate the working set to size
the cache, and size the database for the moment the cache is cold.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="What usually forces a system off a single database?">
Often storage, not queries. Reads can be scaled with replicas and caches, and
writes with batching, but data that grows by tens of terabytes a year doesn't
fit on one node. In the chat example, 800 million messages a day at about 340
bytes each is roughly 100 TB a year, which forces partitioning, while the read
and write rates alone didn't.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="How do you choose a partition key?">
From the hottest query. If the dominant read is "the last 50 messages of a
conversation", partition by conversation ID so it's a single-partition index
scan. Then check the key spreads load evenly (no giant conversation owns a
partition) and list the queries that don't filter on it, since each needs a
second structure or a fan-out.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Fan-out on write or fan-out on read for a news feed?">
Fan-out on write when reads greatly outnumber writes and most authors have
modest audiences: precompute each reader's feed so a read is one lookup.
Fan-out on read for authors whose audience is so large that writing to every
follower is too slow. Twitter reported 300K QPS of timeline reads against 6,000
writes a second, fanned out on write, and merged the largest accounts in at
read time.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Your design meets its average latency but not its p99. Where do you look?">
At sequential dependencies on the request path, because each adds its own tail
to yours; at fan-out, where the slowest of N calls decides the response; and at
shared resources near saturation, where queueing grows the tail first. Fixes
are parallelising calls, precomputing, hedging slow calls, moving work off the
synchronous path, and adding headroom to the hottest resource.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Why is a cache a risk as well as an optimisation?">
Because the database behind it is sized for the misses. If the cache serves
95% of reads, the database sees 5%. When the cache is emptied by a restart,
failover or eviction storm, the database suddenly sees all of it, twenty times
its usual load, and can fall over, which keeps the cache from refilling. The
design has to size for a partially cold cache, coalesce concurrent misses for
the same key, and shed load while it warms.
</QA>

<SectionHeading n="12" id="resources">Go deeper</SectionHeading>

<Quiz>
  <Q q="One billion requests a day: about how many per second on average?">
    About 11,600. Divide by 86,400, or estimate with 10⁹ / 10⁵ = 10⁴.
  </Q>
  <Q q="800 million 340-byte records a day: how much storage per year for one copy?">
    About 99 TB: 272 GB a day × 365.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Why did batching 100 rows per insert raise throughput 7.8×?">
    The per-commit costs (round trip, transaction bookkeeping, WAL flush) are
    paid once per batch instead of once per row.
  </Q>
  <Q q="What does 1 ms of round trip buy you in fibre?">
    About 100 km of distance, since light in fibre covers roughly 200,000 km a
    second, there and back.
  </Q>
</Quiz>
