A core runs one instruction stream at a time. Your laptop has maybe ten cores
and, right now, a few thousand threads that would like to run. Something has to
decide who gets a core, when and for how long.

That decision explains a lot of latency no profiler ever shows you. A service's
p99 doubles under load while the CPU graph sits at 60%. A Kubernetes pod stalls
while its average usage looks comfortable. The JVM and Go had to learn to size
their thread pools from the container's limit, not the host's core count, and
Postgres, which gives every connection its own process, is usually put behind a
pooler.

This chapter starts from the crudest scheduler and builds up to what Linux
does. It walks through a time slice, a context switch and a cgroup throttle one
step at a time, measures what the primitives cost, and ends with how to watch
it all in production.

<SectionHeading n="01" id="why-a-scheduler">Why a scheduler exists</SectionHeading>

Start with the crudest answer to "who runs next": run one program until it
finishes, then the next.

<Sub n="1.1" title="Run to completion, and what breaks">

Two things go wrong at once:

- **A program that waits wastes the core.** One that stops to read from disk
  leaves the core idle for the whole wait.
- **A program that never finishes blocks everyone.** Nobody else ever runs.

Fixing the first needs a way to switch away. Fixing the second needs a way to
take the core back.

</Sub>

<Sub n="1.2" title="Threads, processes and the context switch">

You fix the first by switching away whenever a program has to wait. To do that
the kernel saves everything the core was holding for it (registers, the stack
pointer, where it was in the code) and loads someone else's.

That's a **context switch**, and the unit being switched is a **thread**: one
line of execution with its own registers and stack. A **process** is the
container around one or more threads: an address space, open files,
permissions.

<Why>So should this be a thread or a process?</Why>

Both are schedulable. What differs is what they share. Threads in one process
share an address space, file descriptors and signal handlers; processes share
nothing by default.

| | Threads in one process | Separate processes |
|---|---|---|
| Share | Address space, file descriptors, signal handlers | Nothing by default |
| Creating one | Cheaper | More expensive |
| A crash | Takes the whole process with it | Stays inside that process |
| Scheduling | Almost identical | Almost identical |

<Callout label="Design implication">
"Thread or process" is really "do I want a shared address space, or do I want
a failure boundary". Threads are cheaper to create, and easier to corrupt. The
scheduler treats them almost identically either way.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="1.3" title="Preemption and the three states">

For the second problem you need a timer. The hardware interrupts the core every
few milliseconds, and the kernel gets a chance to take the core away from
whoever has it.

That's **preemption**, and the part of the kernel that picks what runs next is
the **scheduler**. From its point of view every thread is in one of three
states:

<Compare
  cols={["State", "Meaning", "Shows up in"]}
  rows={[
    ["Running", "On a core right now", "CPU profiles, flame graphs"],
    ["Runnable", "Ready, waiting for a core", "Run queue depth. Almost nothing else."],
    ["Blocked", "Waiting on I/O, a lock, a timer", "Off-CPU analysis only"],
  ]}
/>

The middle row is the one that matters, and section 2 shows why.

</Sub>

threads, each needing 10 ms of CPU, and the scheduler hands out 4 ms slices in
turn.

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.2" title="Why a profile can't see waiting">

<Why>What does a CPU profile show for those 24 ms of waiting?</Why>

Nothing. A runnable thread burns no cycles, so it never appears in a flame
graph, and every symptom looks like something else.

**Runnable-but-not-running is where latency under load actually comes from.**
When a service's p99 doubles while the CPU graph sits at 60%, runnable time is
usually where the missing milliseconds are.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Reading a flame graph as the whole story. It only shows time on a core.
Runnable and blocked time need run queue depth (`vmstat`'s `r` column),
`perf sched latency`, or off-CPU analysis.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="03" id="linux-scheduler">How Linux picks the next thread</SectionHeading>

A scheduler promises that every runnable thread eventually runs. That's close
to the entire guarantee, and it's weaker than most people design against.

<Sub n="3.1" title="Eventually, and nothing more">

It doesn't promise when. It doesn't promise for how long. It doesn't promise
which core, and on a machine with fast and slow cores it doesn't promise they
are equivalent.

Anything your design needs beyond "eventually" probably has to come from
somewhere else: priorities, affinity, or a real-time class.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.2" title="From CFS to EEVDF">

Linux ran CFS for fifteen years and replaced it with **EEVDF** in 6.6, Peter
Zijlstra's rewrite. Both are fair-share schedulers: each thread gets CPU time
proportional to its weight, with weight derived from nice value.

| | CFS | EEVDF (Linux 6.6+) |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Fair share by weight | Fair share by weight |
| Weight comes from | Nice value | Nice value |
| Tracks | Each thread's share of CPU time | Also **lag** (how far a thread is from its fair share) and a **deadline** |
| Picks | The thread furthest behind on its share | A thread that has been starved first |

In practice EEVDF improves latency for threads that wake rarely, which covers
most interactive and I/O-bound work. It's worth knowing which one you're on,
because latency behaviour for those threads changed noticeably.

<Why>Does fair share guarantee my thread a slice?</Why>

No. Fair share is a target the scheduler converges toward, not a contract for
any individual slice.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.3" title="Heterogeneous cores">

This machine has performance and efficiency cores. So does every recent phone,
and so do Intel's P/E designs. The scheduler places threads based on
utilisation history and thermal state, and **the same thread can run at very
different speeds depending on where it lands.**

That breaks a background assumption in almost every benchmark: that threads
are interchangeable. Section 6 shows it directly. Going from one thread to five
gives 4.3× instead of the 5× you'd expect, probably because those five didn't
all get equal hardware.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="04" id="context-switch">The context switch, step by step</SectionHeading>

A context switch is the moment the scheduler's decision takes effect. The save
and restore are the cheap part.

<Sub n="4.1" title="What the kernel saves and swaps">

<Steps
  title="Switching a core from thread A to thread B"
  lanes={[
    { t: "Thread A", s: "outgoing", icon: "◉" },
    { t: "Scheduler", s: "pick next", icon: "⇆" },
    { t: "Save", s: "A's registers", icon: "▤" },
    { t: "Page table", s: "only across processes", icon: "▦" },
    { t: "Restore", s: "B's registers", icon: "▤" },
    { t: "Thread B", s: "incoming", icon: "◎" },
  ]}
  steps={[
    [0, "Thread A is running. A **timer interrupt** fires, or A makes a blocking call and has to wait."],
    [1, "The **scheduler** runs and picks the next thread. On Linux 6.6 and later that's EEVDF's choice."],
    [2, "The kernel saves A's registers and stack pointer, everything the core was holding for it."],
    [3, "If B is in a different process, the kernel **swaps the page table root**. A's cached translations no longer apply. Threads in the same process skip this step."],
    [4, "It loads B's registers and stack pointer."],
    [5, "B returns to user space, into **cold caches and a cold TLB**. Refilling them is charged to B's run time, not to the switch."],
  ]}
/>

<Why>Why is a thread switch cheaper than a process switch?</Why>

Step 4. Switching threads within one process means saving registers and the
stack pointer. Switching between processes also swaps the page table root,
which invalidates translation caching: chapter 04's TLB, refilled from
scratch.

That difference is also why Meltdown mitigations made even same-process
syscalls more expensive. Page table isolation reintroduced part of that cost on
every kernel entry.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.2" title="The cost you cannot see in the switch">

Counting the switch itself undersells it. The incoming thread finds a cache
full of somebody else's data and a TLB that no longer describes its address
space.

Those refills happen *after* the switch completes, charged to your thread's
execution time, and they can dwarf the switch.

That's the mechanism behind convoy latency in chapter 16: not the handoff cost,
but everything each woken thread has to re-warm.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="05" id="failure">Where scheduling goes wrong</SectionHeading>

Each failure here looks like something else from inside the application. The
first one is the most common in containerised services.

<Sub n="5.1" title="Throttled at 30% CPU">

Container CPU limits are enforced by quota over a period, by default 100 ms. A
container with a 0.5 CPU limit gets 50 ms of CPU per 100 ms window. Spend it in
the first 20 ms and **your process is frozen for the remaining 80**.

A multi-threaded runtime probably makes it worse:

<Steps
  title="One 100 ms period for a 0.5 CPU container with eight busy threads"
  lanes={[
    { t: "Period starts", s: "quota refilled", icon: "↻" },
    { t: "Threads run", s: "8 in parallel", icon: "⚡" },
    { t: "Quota spent", s: "50 ms of CPU", icon: "▤" },
    { t: "Throttled", s: "whole cgroup", icon: "⏸" },
    { t: "Rollover", s: "next period", icon: "↻" },
  ]}
  steps={[
    [0, "A new 100 ms period starts. The 0.5 CPU limit gives the container **50 ms of CPU** to spend in it."],
    [1, "A burst arrives, and eight threads run at once. Together they burn quota eight times faster than the wall clock moves."],
    [2, "After about **6 ms of wall time**, the 50 ms budget is gone."],
    [3, "The kernel throttles the whole cgroup. **Every thread is frozen for the remaining 94 ms**: runnable, not running. `nr_throttled` goes up by one."],
    [4, "The period rolls over, the quota refills, and the threads resume. A request that arrived during the freeze has waited for most of the period."],
  ]}
/>

<Why>Why does average CPU look fine?</Why>

Because the average includes the frozen time. The container used its 50 ms in
a burst, then did nothing for the rest of the period, so a per-second graph
shows modest usage.

The symptoms are distinctive once you know them:

| Symptom | What it means |
|---|---|
| p99 latency in clean multiples of 100 ms | Stalls quantise to the quota period |
| Average CPU usage well under the limit | The average includes the frozen time |
| No application-level explanation | The whole cgroup was paused, not your code |

Check `nr_throttled` and `throttled_time` in `cpu.stat`. This is probably the
most under-diagnosed latency problem in containerised services, and the
[throttling field note](/blog/throttled-at-forty-percent) reproduces it.

<Callout label="Design implication">
Years of incident reports about services throttled at 30% CPU moved the
Kubernetes community to a rare consensus: set requests, skip CPU limits, and
let the scheduler do its job. More threads make throttling worse, not better,
so size thread pools from the container's limit, not the host's core count.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.2" title="Oversubscription and priority inversion">

More runnable threads than cores means time-slicing, which means every thread's
latency grows even though throughput may not. Section 6 measures the point
where adding threads stops helping.

Priority inversion belongs here too. Chapter 13 covers it, but note that Linux
`nice` only affects the fair-share weight. A `nice 19` thread still runs, and
still holds any lock it took.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.3" title="Thundering herds">

Wake a hundred threads for one available unit of work and ninety-nine wake up,
context switch, find nothing, and go back to sleep.

`epoll` has `EPOLLEXCLUSIVE` and futexes have `FUTEX_WAKE` with a count
precisely to avoid this. The default for condition variables is `notify_one`
for the same reason.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Reaching for `notify_all` by reflex. When only one waiter can make progress,
it's probably a bug: every other waiter pays a context switch to find nothing.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" id="cost">What the primitives cost</SectionHeading>

<Callout label="The machine" kind="note">
Apple M4, 10 cores, macOS 26.5.1, clang 21 `-O2`. Core placement is the
scheduler's choice and this chip has both performance and efficiency cores, so
the scaling numbers reflect a heterogeneous machine. That's the normal case
now, not an exotic one.
</Callout>

<Sub n="6.1" title="The primitives">

Two findings, and the second surprised me.

<Why>Why isn't scaling linear?</Why>

Five threads gives 4.3× and ten gives 8.2×, not 5× and 10×. On a homogeneous
machine you'd blame memory bandwidth.

Here the likelier explanation is efficiency cores doing less work per unit
time: the threads aren't running on equivalent hardware. I haven't isolated
that with core pinning, which macOS makes awkward, so treat it as the leading
hypothesis and not a proven cause. On Linux it's one `taskset` away.

**Oversubscription costs almost nothing here.** Going from 10 to 40 threads,
4× the runnable threads, changed wall time by under 1%. Time-slicing overhead
on modern schedulers is small for CPU-bound work.

What it does destroy is *latency*, which this measurement doesn't show.
Chapter 16 has that side.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="07" id="operating">Watching the scheduler</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="7.1" title="Finding scheduling problems">

```shell
# Runnable-but-not-running. The number that matters, and the one nobody watches.
vmstat 1                     # 'r' column = run queue depth
perf sched latency --sort max

# Where a thread went to sleep, and for how long
perf record -e sched:sched_switch -g ./app

# Container throttling. Check this FIRST in any container
cat /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.stat  | grep -E 'nr_throttled|throttled_usec'

# Which core, and did it move?
perf stat -e context-switches,cpu-migrations ./app
taskset -c 0-3 ./app         # pin, then re-measure
```

If `nr_throttled` is climbing, stop everything else and fix that. No other
measurement in a throttled container means anything.

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.2" title="Symptom, cause, check">

| Symptom | Likely cause | Check |
|---|---|---|
| p99 in multiples of 100 ms | cgroup CPU throttling | `cpu.stat` `nr_throttled` |
| High run queue, low CPU% | Blocked on I/O, or throttled | `vmstat` `r` column, then off-CPU |
| More threads, same throughput | At the core count already | Count cores; check affinity |
| Latency varies run to run | Heterogeneous core placement | Pin with `taskset` and compare |
| Many wakeups, little work | Thundering herd | `notify_all`, or epoll without `EPOLLEXCLUSIVE` |

</Sub>

<Sub n="7.3" title="What you give up">

| You get | You pay | When the bill arrives |
|---|---|---|
| Preemptive multitasking | No control over when you run | As latency variance nobody can attribute |
| Fair-share scheduling | Fair isn't predictable | When one tenant needs a guarantee |
| Cheap oversubscription for throughput | Latency degrades even when throughput doesn't | At p99, never at the mean |
| cgroup CPU limits | Quota exhaustion freezes you for the rest of the period | As p99 in multiples of 100 ms |
| Heterogeneous cores and better battery | Threads are no longer interchangeable | As benchmarks that won't reproduce |

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="08" id="summary">Summary</SectionHeading>

1. **A thread is running, runnable or blocked.** Only running shows up in a CPU
   profile.
2. **Runnable time is where latency under load hides.** Four threads sharing a
   core took 34 to 40 ms to do 10 ms of work each.
3. **Threads and processes differ in what they share**, not in how they're
   scheduled. Choose a shared address space or a failure boundary.
4. **The scheduler promises "eventually", nothing more.** Timing guarantees
   have to come from priorities, affinity or a real-time class.
5. **Linux replaced CFS with EEVDF in 6.6.** It adds lag and deadlines, so
   rarely-waking threads get picked sooner.
6. **A process switch swaps the page table root.** That's why it costs more
   than a thread switch.
7. **The real cost of a switch comes after it**, as cache and TLB refills
   charged to the incoming thread.
8. **A CPU limit is a quota per 100 ms period.** Spend it early and the whole
   cgroup freezes for the rest; more threads spend it faster.
9. **Creating a thread cost about 127× a round trip** between live ones (13.8 µs
   against 109 ns), which is the case for pools.
10. **Past the core count, extra threads add latency, not throughput.** Forty
    threads ran within 1% of ten on a ten-core M4.

<SectionHeading n="09" id="build">Build this</SectionHeading>

**Reproduce cgroup throttling on purpose**, because recognising its signature
once is worth more than reading about it three times.

- Run a CPU-bound loop in a container limited to 0.5 CPU.
- Record per-iteration latency and plot the distribution.
- You should see clean stalls around 100 ms, the quota period.
- Now give it 4 threads at the same 0.5 limit. The stalls get longer and more
  frequent, because four threads drain the quota four times faster.

Then read `cpu.stat` and match `throttled_usec` against the gaps you plotted.
Once you've seen that shape you'll recognise it in a production dashboard in
about five seconds.

<SectionHeading n="10" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="What's the difference between a process and a thread?">
What they share. Threads within a process share an address space, file
descriptors and signal handlers; processes share none of that by default. Both
are scheduled the same way on Linux: the kernel schedules tasks and doesn't
much care which kind you made.

So what you're really choosing is a shared address space or a failure
boundary. A crashed thread takes the process with it; a crashed process doesn't.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Your containerised service has p99 latency at almost exactly 100 ms multiples and uses 30% CPU. What is it?">
cgroup CPU throttling. Limits are enforced as a quota per period, 100 ms by
default. Once the quota is spent the container is frozen until the period rolls
over, so stalls quantise to the period length.

Average CPU looks low because the average includes the frozen time. Check
`nr_throttled` and `throttled_usec` in `cpu.stat`. A multi-threaded runtime
makes it worse: eight threads drain a 50 ms budget in about 6 ms of wall time.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="You doubled threads from 10 to 20 on a 10-core box. Throughput is unchanged. Is that expected?">
Yes for CPU-bound work. Ten threads already saturate ten cores; the rest just
time-slice. I measured 4.40 ms at 10 threads and 4.42 ms at 20, under 1%
difference, so the scheduler's overhead is small.

What does change is latency. Each thread now waits behind others, so p99
degrades while throughput holds. If your work is I/O-bound the answer flips, and
more threads help until you run out of something else.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Why is a context switch more expensive than the register save suggests?">
Because the expensive part happens after it. The incoming thread finds caches
holding another thread's data and, across processes, a TLB that no longer
describes its address space. Those refills are charged to your thread's run
time, not to the switch.

Crossing processes also swaps the page table root. Meltdown mitigations made
this worse by unmapping most kernel pages from the user page table, so even a
syscall now involves page table work that used to be free.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Scaling to 5 threads gave 4.3x and to 10 gave 8.2x on a 10-core machine. Why not linear?">
On a homogeneous machine I'd suspect memory bandwidth or a shared resource. On
this one the likelier cause is heterogeneous cores: the M4 has performance and
efficiency cores, and a thread on an E-core does less work per unit time. Five
threads probably didn't all land on P-cores.

I haven't proven that. Pinning threads to core types on macOS is awkward and I
didn't do it. On Linux it's one `taskset` away, and that's the experiment that
would settle it.
</QA>
