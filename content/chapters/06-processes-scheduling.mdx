A core runs one instruction stream at a time. Your laptop has maybe ten cores
and, right now, a few thousand threads that would like to run. Something has to
decide who gets a core, when and for how long, and that decision explains a lot
of latency that no profiler ever shows you.

Start with the crudest answer: run one program until it finishes, then the next.
Two things go wrong at once. A program that stops to read from disk leaves the
core idle for the whole wait, and a program that never finishes means nobody
else ever runs.

You fix the first by switching away whenever a program has to wait. To do
that the kernel saves everything the core was holding for it (registers, the
stack pointer, where it was in the code) and loads someone else's. That's a
**context switch**, and the unit being switched is a **thread**: one line of
execution with its own registers and stack. A **process** is the container
around one or more threads: an address space, open files, permissions. Threads
in the same process share all of that. So they're cheaper to create, and
easier to corrupt.

For the second you need a timer. The hardware interrupts the core every few
milliseconds, and the kernel gets a chance to take the core away from whoever
has it. That's **preemption**, and the part of the kernel that picks what runs
next is the **scheduler**. From its point of view every thread is in one of
three states: *running* on a core, *runnable* and waiting for one, or *blocked*
waiting for something else, like disk, the network or a lock.

Here's a small example to see what sharing costs. One core, four runnable
threads, each needing 10 ms of CPU, and the scheduler hands out 4 ms slices in
turn. When does the first one finish? It runs 4 ms, waits 12 while the others
run, runs another 4, waits again, and finally finishes its last 2 ms at 34 ms.
And the last one finishes at 40. Every thread needed 10 ms of work and took more
than three times that, and the extra time wasn't spent computing anything.

Now the question that makes this matter in production: what does a CPU profile
show for a thread during those 24 ms of waiting? Nothing. A runnable thread
burns no cycles, so it never appears in a flame graph. When a service's p99
doubles under load while the CPU graph sits at 60%, runnable time is usually
where the missing milliseconds are.

Containers add a second way to be runnable and still not run. A Kubernetes CPU
limit is enforced by throttling the whole cgroup once it has used its quota for
the current period, so a pod can stall while its average usage looks
comfortable. The [throttling field note](/blog/throttled-at-forty-percent)
reproduces that. That arithmetic also explains why the JVM and Go had to learn
to size their thread pools from the container's limit and not the host's core
count, and why Postgres, which gives every connection its own process, is usually
put behind a pooler.

From here the chapter covers what the scheduler does and doesn't promise,
how Linux picks the next thread, what a context switch costs, and the ways it
fails. There's one result I couldn't fully explain, too. The same fixed work on
five threads ran 4.3× faster than on one, and on ten threads 8.2×, on a
ten-core M4 with two kinds of core. Section 5.2 has the hypothesis and the
experiment that would settle it.

<SectionHeading n="01" cmd="man 7 sched" id="contract">Eventually, and nothing more</SectionHeading>

A scheduler promises that every runnable thread eventually runs. That's close to
the entire guarantee, and it's weaker than most people design against.

It does not promise when. It does not promise for how long. It does not promise
which core, and on a machine with fast and slow cores it does not promise they
are equivalent. Anything your design needs beyond "eventually" probably has to come from
somewhere else — priorities, affinity, or a real-time class.

<Callout label="Process or thread is a memory question">
Both are schedulable. What differs is what they share. Threads in one process
share an address space, file descriptors and signal handlers; processes share
nothing by default. So "should this be a thread or a process" is really "do I
want a shared address space, or do I want a failure boundary" — and the
scheduler treats them almost identically either way.
</Callout>

<SectionHeading n="02" cmd="cat /proc/$PID/status" id="layout">Processes, threads, and the switch between them</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="2.1" title="What a context switch has to save">

Switching threads within one process means saving registers and the stack
pointer. Switching between processes additionally swaps the page table root,
which invalidates translation caching — chapter 04's TLB, refilled from scratch.

That difference is why thread switches are cheaper than process switches, and
why Meltdown mitigations made even same-process syscalls more expensive: page
table isolation reintroduced part of that cost on every kernel entry.

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.2" title="How Linux decides">

Linux ran CFS for fifteen years and replaced it with **EEVDF** in 6.6. Both are
fair-share schedulers: each thread gets CPU time proportional to its weight,
with weight derived from nice value.

EEVDF adds an explicit notion of **lag** — how far a thread is from its fair
share — and a deadline, so a thread that has been starved is picked first. In
practice it improves latency for threads that wake rarely — most interactive
and I/O-bound work.

What neither does is guarantee anything. Fair share is a target the scheduler
converges toward, not a contract for any individual slice.

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.3" title="Heterogeneous cores">

This machine has performance and efficiency cores. So does every recent phone,
and so do Intel's P/E designs. The scheduler places threads based on utilisation
history and thermal state, and **the same thread can run at very different
speeds depending on where it lands.**

That breaks a background assumption in almost every benchmark: that threads are
interchangeable. Section 5 shows it directly — going from one thread to five
gives 4.3× instead of the 5× you'd expect, because those five didn't all get
equal hardware.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.1" title="The cost you cannot see in the switch">

Counting the switch itself undersells it. The incoming thread finds a cache
full of somebody else's data and a TLB that no longer describes its address
space. Those refills happen *after* the switch completes, charged to your
thread's execution time, and they can dwarf the switch.

That's the mechanism behind convoy latency in chapter 16: not the handoff cost,
but everything each woken thread has to re-warm.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="04" cmd="cat /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.stat" id="failure">Throttled at 30% CPU</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="4.1" title="cgroup CPU throttling">

Container CPU limits are enforced by quota over a period — by default 100 ms.
A container with a 0.5 CPU limit gets 50 ms of CPU per 100 ms window. Spend it
in the first 20 ms and **your process is frozen for the remaining 80**.

This is the most under-diagnosed latency problem in containerised services. The
symptoms are distinctive once you know them: p99 latency in clean multiples of
100 ms, average CPU usage well under the limit, and no application-level
explanation. Check `nr_throttled` and `throttled_time` in `cpu.stat`.

A multi-threaded runtime probably makes it worse. Eight threads burning quota
concurrently exhaust a 50 ms budget in about 6 ms of wall time, then stall for
94.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.2" title="Oversubscription and priority inversion">

More runnable threads than cores means time-slicing, which means every thread's
latency grows even though throughput may not. Section 5 measures the point
where adding threads stops helping.

Priority inversion belongs here too — chapter 13 covers it, but note that Linux
`nice` only affects the fair-share weight. A `nice 19` thread still runs, and
still holds any lock it took.

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.3" title="Thundering herds">

Wake a hundred threads for one available unit of work and ninety-nine wake up,
context switch, find nothing, and go back to sleep. `epoll` has `EPOLLEXCLUSIVE`
and futexes have `FUTEX_WAKE` with a count precisely to avoid this. The default
for condition variables is `notify_one` for the same reason, and the reflex to reach for `notify_all` is
probably a bug.

</Sub>

Two findings, and the second surprised me.

**Scaling falls short of linear at every point.** Five threads gives 4.3× and
ten gives 8.2×, not 5× and 10×. On a homogeneous machine you'd blame memory
bandwidth. Here the likelier explanation is efficiency cores doing genuinely
less work per unit time — the threads aren't running on equivalent hardware. I
haven't isolated that with core pinning, which macOS makes awkward, so treat it
as the leading hypothesis and not a proven cause.

**Oversubscription costs almost nothing here.** Going from 10 to 40 threads —
4× the runnable threads — changed wall time by under 1%. Time-slicing overhead
on modern schedulers is genuinely small for CPU-bound work. What it does destroy
is *latency*, which this measurement doesn't show. Chapter 16 has that side.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" cmd="perf sched latency --sort max" id="operating">Watching the scheduler</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="6.1" title="Finding scheduling problems">

```shell
# Runnable-but-not-running. The number that matters, and the one nobody watches.
vmstat 1                     # 'r' column = run queue depth
perf sched latency --sort max

# Where a thread went to sleep, and for how long
perf record -e sched:sched_switch -g ./app

# Container throttling — check this FIRST in any container
cat /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.stat  | grep -E 'nr_throttled|throttled_usec'

# Which core, and did it move?
perf stat -e context-switches,cpu-migrations ./app
taskset -c 0-3 ./app         # pin, then re-measure
```

If `nr_throttled` is climbing, stop everything else and fix that. No other
measurement in a throttled container means anything.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="07" cmd="git init ./weekend-project" id="build">Build this</SectionHeading>

**Reproduce cgroup throttling on purpose**, because recognising its signature
once is worth more than reading about it three times.

- Run a CPU-bound loop in a container limited to 0.5 CPU.
- Record per-iteration latency and plot the distribution.
- You should see clean stalls around 100 ms — the quota period.
- Now give it 4 threads at the same 0.5 limit. The stalls get longer and more
  frequent, because four threads drain the quota four times faster.

Then read `cpu.stat` and match `throttled_usec` against the gaps you plotted.
Once you've seen that shape you'll recognise it in a production dashboard in
about five seconds.

<SectionHeading n="10" cmd="./drill --level all" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="What's the difference between a process and a thread?">
What they share. Threads within a process share an address space, file
descriptors and signal handlers; processes share none of that by default. Both
are scheduled the same way on Linux — the kernel schedules tasks and doesn't
much care which kind you made.

So what you're really choosing is a shared address space or a failure
boundary. A crashed thread takes the process with it; a crashed process doesn't.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Your containerised service has p99 latency at almost exactly 100 ms multiples and uses 30% CPU. What is it?">
cgroup CPU throttling. Limits are enforced as a quota per period, 100 ms by
default. Once the quota is spent the container is frozen until the period rolls
over, so stalls quantise to the period length.

Average CPU looks low because the average includes the frozen time. Check
`nr_throttled` and `throttled_usec` in `cpu.stat`. A multi-threaded runtime
makes it worse — eight threads drain a 50 ms budget in about 6 ms of wall time.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="You doubled threads from 10 to 20 on a 10-core box. Throughput is unchanged. Is that expected?">
Yes for CPU-bound work. Ten threads already saturate ten cores; the rest just
time-slice. I measured 4.40 ms at 10 threads and 4.42 ms at 20 — under 1%
difference, so the scheduler's overhead is genuinely small.

What does change is latency. Each thread now waits behind others, so p99
degrades while throughput holds. If your work is I/O-bound the answer flips, and
more threads help until you run out of something else.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Why is a context switch more expensive than the register save suggests?">
Because the expensive part happens after it. The incoming thread finds caches
holding another thread's data and, across processes, a TLB that no longer
describes its address space. Those refills are charged to your thread's run
time, not to the switch.

Crossing processes also swaps the page table root. Meltdown mitigations made
this worse by unmapping most kernel pages from the user page table, so even a
syscall now involves page table work that used to be free.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Scaling to 5 threads gave 4.3x and to 10 gave 8.2x on a 10-core machine. Why not linear?">
On a homogeneous machine I'd suspect memory bandwidth or a shared resource. On
this one the likelier cause is heterogeneous cores: the M4 has performance and
efficiency cores, and a thread on an E-core does less work per unit time. Five
threads probably didn't all land on P-cores.

I haven't proven that — pinning threads to core types on macOS is awkward and I
didn't do it. On Linux it's one `taskset` away, and that's the experiment that
would settle it.
</QA>
