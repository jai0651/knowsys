Chapter 13 built a mutex, and it works. It also has one weakness that no
amount of tuning removes: while a thread holds the lock, everyone else who wants
it waits for *that thread*. If the holder gets descheduled, or takes a page
fault, or is interrupted by a signal handler that then tries to take the same
lock, the waiters are stuck until it comes back. Usually that's a few
microseconds of latency (annoying, not fatal). For an audio callback that must produce samples every
few milliseconds, or a signal handler, it can be a glitch or a deadlock.

So here's a different question. Could threads update shared data without any of
them ever holding anything?

The tool is the same atomic instruction the lock was built from,
**compare-and-swap** (CAS): "if this word still holds the value I read, replace
it with my new one; otherwise tell me it changed". Instead of locking, a thread
reads the current value, computes the new one privately, and tries to swap it
in. If another thread got there first, the CAS fails, and it reads again and
retries (the work it did privately is simply thrown away).

Walk through a shared stack to see why that's enough. To push a node, a thread
points the node's `next` at the current head, then CASes the head from the old
value to the new node. Two threads racing to push both read the same head, and
both try the CAS. One succeeds. The other's CAS sees that the head moved, fails,
and retries with the new head. At no point is the stack half-updated. And
because nobody holds anything, a thread stopped halfway through its update
hasn't changed anything shared, so everyone else carries on without it. That
property is what **lock-free** means: some thread always makes progress, however
the others are scheduled.

Notice what that doesn't say. It's a guarantee about *progress*, not about
speed, and it's easy to confuse the two. What happens when eight threads hammer
the same word? Most of them lose most of their races and retry (and every retry is another
trip for the cache line between cores). I measured a
counter incremented three ways as threads were added. At one thread the CAS
loop beat a mutex six to one, 2.6 ns against 15.6. At eight threads it lost ten
to one, **162 ns against 16.3**, because 84% of compare-exchange attempts were
thrown away. The crossover on this machine was at four threads, and section 3
shows why in eleven instructions.

The other cost is correctness, and it's where the famous bugs live. When a
lock-free stack pops a node, when is it safe to free it? Another thread may have
read a pointer to it a moment ago and be about to use it. And if the node is
freed and its address reused, a CAS that checks "is the head still this address?"
can succeed when it shouldn't. That's the **ABA** problem. Schemes like hazard
pointers, epoch-based reclamation and the kernel's RCU (read-copy-update) exist to answer "when can I
free this?"

You rely on this more than you might think. Java's `java.util.concurrent` is
largely built on CAS, the LMAX Disruptor passes messages between threads without
a lock, audio software moves samples through lock-free ring buffers, and Linux
readers walk routing tables under RCU without taking any lock. This chapter lays
out the four ways to update shared state, from a mutex to wait-free, measures
where each one wins, and ends with the questions that settle most real cases.

<SectionHeading n="01" cmd="cat DECISION.md" id="choice">Take the lock, or don't</SectionHeading>

Several threads need to update shared state. You're deciding between taking a
lock and not taking one, and the marketing around that choice is unusually bad.

"Lock-free" is a **progress guarantee**, not a performance claim. People use it
as a synonym for fast, and that mistake is expensive enough to have its own
measurement in section 3.

<Compare
  cols={["Guarantee", "What it promises", "What it costs"]}
  rows={[
    ["Blocking", "Nothing. A suspended holder blocks everyone.", "Simplest, and often fastest under real contention."],
    ["Obstruction-free", "A thread running alone finishes.", "Livelock is permitted."],
    ["Lock-free", "At least one thread progresses, always.", "Individual threads may starve indefinitely."],
    ["Wait-free", "Every thread finishes in bounded steps.", "Usually far slower; needs helping schemes."],
  ]}
/>

Read the lock-free row twice. **The system progresses; your thread might not.** A
thread can lose a compare-exchange race forever and the algorithm is still
formally lock-free, because someone won each time.

<Callout label="What you're actually buying">
Rarely throughput. It's that **no thread can be descheduled while holding
anything**. That matters when a signal handler, an interrupt context, or a
real-time deadline cannot tolerate an arbitrary wait — and there it isn't an
optimisation, it's a correctness requirement.
</Callout>

<SectionHeading n="02" cmd="ls ./options/" id="options">Four ways to update shared state</SectionHeading>

<Approach
  n="1"
  name="std::mutex"
  breaks="A holder descheduled mid-section blocks everyone. Unusable in a signal handler or interrupt context."
  bestFor="Almost everything. Notably flat under contention — see section 3 — and trivially debuggable."
>
Chapter 13 has the mechanism. For this decision what matters is that the
uncontended path is about 4 ns with no syscall, and the contended path degrades
gracefully instead of catastrophically.
</Approach>

<Approach
  n="2"
  name="A single hardware atomic"
  breaks="Only works for operations the ISA implements — add, or, exchange, compare-exchange. No good for anything compound."
  bestFor="Counters, flags, bitsets. Should be your first thought, and often isn't."
>
`fetch_add`, `fetch_or`, `exchange`. On ARMv8.1 and later these compile to one
LSE instruction that the interconnect resolves. No retry loop exists, and section 3
shows them holding up where a CAS loop collapses.
</Approach>

<Approach
  n="3"
  name="A compare-exchange loop"
  breaks="Throughput collapses under contention — 162 ns at eight threads against a mutex's 16 ns — and reclamation becomes a research problem."
  bestFor="Low contention, or where blocking is a correctness problem. The Treiber stack and most lock-free structures are this shape."
>
Read the current value, compute the new one, swap it in if nothing changed,
retry if something did. Almost every lock-free structure is this three-line
pattern:

```cpp
void push(int v) {
    Node* n = new Node{v, head.load(std::memory_order_relaxed)};
    while (!head.compare_exchange_weak(n->next, n,
               std::memory_order_release, std::memory_order_relaxed)) {}
}
```

Note the elegance: on failure `compare_exchange_weak` writes the current value
back into `n->next`, so the retry is already set up. That's why the loop body is
empty.
</Approach>

<Approach
  n="4"
  name="Wait-free"
  breaks="Usually needs helping schemes where threads complete each other's operations. Complexity goes up sharply and throughput usually goes down."
  bestFor="Hard real-time, where a bounded worst case is the actual deliverable."
>
Every thread finishes in a bounded number of steps regardless of what others do.
Genuinely rare in production code, and you'll probably know when you need it because
someone handed you a latency budget with no asterisk.
</Approach>

<SectionHeading n="03" cmd="./bench --mutex --cas --fetchadd" id="crossover">Six times faster, then ten times slower</SectionHeading>

<Callout label="The machine" kind="note">
Apple M4, 10 cores, macOS 26.5.1, clang 21 `-O2 -pthread`, median of 3 runs,
200,000 increments **per thread** so adding threads adds work. ARMv8.6 means LSE
atomics are available.
</Callout>

Same operation — increment a shared counter — three ways.

At one thread the CAS loop is **six times faster** than the mutex. At eight it's

stable strategy.

`fetch_add` stays fast throughout because it never retries.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.2" title="Why, in eleven instructions">

<SourceRead
  file="clang++ -O2 -c, disassembled with objdump"
  repo="aarch64"
  tag="Apple M4, ARMv8.6-A"
  href="https://developer.arm.com/documentation/ddi0487/latest/"
>
```asm
;; a.fetch_add(1, relaxed)  —  ONE instruction, no branch
add:
    mov   w8, #0x1
    ldadd x8, x0, [x0]        ; LSE atomic: hardware resolves it
    ret

;; compare_exchange_weak loop  —  a SOFTWARE retry branch
cas:
    ldr   x8, [x0]
    add   x10, x8, #0x1
    mov   x9, x8
.Lretry:
    cas   x9, x10, [x0]
    cmp   x9, x8
    b.eq  .Ldone
    mov   x8, x9
    add   x10, x9, #0x1
    b     .Lretry             ;        GO ROUND AGAIN
```
</SourceRead>

A losing core in the `ldadd` case re-executes nothing; the interconnect sorts it
out. A losing thread in the CAS case reloads, recomputes and branches back.

<SectionHeading n="04" cmd="./choose --explain" id="choosing">Five questions</SectionHeading>

Five questions. Most decisions are settled by the first two.

1. **Can you not share the thing at all?** Per-thread state with a periodic
   merge beats every algorithm here. Try this first, always.
2. **Is there a single hardware atomic for it?** Counters, flags and bitsets
   want `fetch_add` or `fetch_or`, never a CAS loop.
3. **Is contention low, or the critical section long?** Use a mutex.
4. **Is blocking a correctness problem?** Signal handler, interrupt, hard
   real-time — then lock-free, and use an existing implementation.
5. **Otherwise measure both.** The crossover is machine-specific and the table
   above is probably one data point on one unusual laptop.

<SectionHeading n="05" cmd="clang++ -fsanitize=thread" id="mistakes">ABA, reclamation, and livelock</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="5.1" title="ABA">

Compare-exchange compares a *value*, not a history. If a pointer goes from A to
B and back to A, the CAS succeeds and the algorithm is wrong.

Fixes, roughly: a **tagged pointer** pairing address with a counter in one wide
CAS, or
never reusing memory until you can prove nobody holds a reference — which is the
harder problem below.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.2" title="Assuming you can just free the nodes">

Writing a lock-free stack takes an afternoon. Freeing its nodes safely is a
research area.

A thread reads `head`, gets pointer `A`, and is descheduled before dereferencing
it. Another thread pops and deletes `A`. The first wakes and reads freed memory.
No lock to hold, and no obvious moment when `A` becomes safe to free.

    ["Epoch-based", "Nearly free", "One stalled thread blocks reclamation for everyone"],
    ["RCU", "Literally zero on many builds", "Writers serialise; bad for write-heavy data"],
  ]}
/>

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.3" title="Mistaking livelock for healthy CPU usage">

Under heavy contention, threads can spend most of their time losing
compare-exchanges. The algorithm keeps its lock-free guarantee while aggregate
throughput collapses.

The cruel part: **this looks like healthy CPU utilisation.** A blocked thread shows as off-CPU time and turns up in `perf sched`. A thread spinning in a CAS
retry loop is 100% on-CPU and appears in a flame graph as a hot function someone
will conclude is "just expensive".

Count instructions retired against operations completed. If you finished a
million pushes but retired six million loop bodies, that's your answer and no
flame graph would have shown it.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.4" title="Dropping release ordering because x86 didn't care">

The `memory_order_release` in the push above is load-bearing. Downgrade it to
`relaxed` and the code compiles, passes light testing, and fails on a weakly
ordered machine under load — another core can observe the new head pointer while
the node's fields are still in the writing core's store buffer.

x86-64 hides that almost perfectly. Chapter 03 has the detail; the operational
advice is to run your tests on ARM.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" cmd="./worksheet" id="worksheet">Decide it for your case</SectionHeading>

**Write the Treiber stack, then break it on purpose.**

- Implement `push` and `pop` with `compare_exchange_weak`. Thirty lines.
- Free popped nodes immediately, run eight threads, and watch it crash
  intermittently and unhelpfully. That's 5.2, experienced instead of read.
- Add a retry counter. Print attempts-per-success at 1, 2, 4 and 8 threads, and
  find **your** crossover against a `std::mutex` stack.
- Then implement epoch reclamation and measure what the read path now costs.

The number to hunt is where your two curves cross. Mine was four threads; yours
is probably somewhere else, and knowing which side of it you're on is the whole
decision.

<SectionHeading n="08" cmd="./drill --level all" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="Does lock-free mean faster?">
No. It's a progress guarantee: at least one thread always progresses, so no
thread can be blocked by another being descheduled. Under real contention a
mutex is often faster.

On an M4 I measured a CAS retry loop at 2.6 ns with one thread against a mutex
at 15.6 ns, and at eight threads those became 162 ns and 16.3 ns — the lock-free
version was ten times slower.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="What is ABA and why doesn't compare-exchange catch it?">
Compare-exchange compares a value, not a history. A pointer goes from A to B and
back to A while your thread is descheduled; your CAS sees A, succeeds, and
installs a `next` pointer you read before any of that happened.

The classic fix is a tagged pointer — address plus a monotonically increasing
counter in a single wide compare-exchange — so the value differs even when the
address repeats.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Why can a lock-free algorithm starve a thread?">
Because the guarantee is system-wide, not per-thread. Lock-freedom requires that
*someone* completes; nothing says it can't always be someone else. A thread that
keeps losing its compare-exchange makes no progress while the algorithm remains
formally lock-free.

Wait-freedom is the stronger property that bounds each thread's steps, and it
usually costs enough that people don't.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Why is fetch_add so much faster than a compare_exchange loop under contention?">
They compile to different things. On ARMv8.1 and later `fetch_add` becomes a
single LSE instruction — `ldadd` — and the interconnect resolves contention
without the core re-executing anything. A `compare_exchange_weak` loop compiles
to a `cas`, a compare and a backward branch, so a losing thread re-reads,
recomputes and retries in software.

At eight threads I measured 6.36 attempts per successful increment: 84% of work
discarded, each discarded attempt still paying a cache line transfer. That's the
difference between 18 ns and 162 ns.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="You have a lock-free queue. Why can't you just delete popped nodes?">
Another thread may hold a pointer it read before you popped. There's no lock and
no obvious moment the node becomes unreachable, so freeing creates a
use-after-free that's timing-dependent and nearly impossible to reproduce.

You need a reclamation scheme. Hazard pointers publish in-use pointers per
thread and cost a store and a fence on every read. Epoch reclamation is cheaper
to read but a thread stalled inside an epoch blocks all reclamation and memory
grows until it leaves. RCU makes reads free and defers everything to writers,
which only works if you're overwhelmingly read-mostly.
</QA>

<SectionHeading n="09" cmd="cat REFERENCES.md" id="resources">Go deeper</SectionHeading>

<Quiz>
  <Q q="Your CAS loop is at 100% CPU and throughput is falling. Deadlock or livelock?">
    Livelock. Deadlock consumes no CPU. Threads burning cycles losing races look
    identical to useful work in a flame graph — check attempts per success.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Why does dropping release to relaxed pass on x86 and fail on ARM?">
    x86-64 is strongly ordered and won't reorder the stores publishing the node.
    ARM is weakly ordered, so another core can see the new head before the
    node's fields.
  </Q>
  <Q q="When is a tagged pointer not enough to fix ABA?">
    When the counter wraps. With a 16-bit tag on a hot structure, 65,536
    operations is maybe a millisecond.
  </Q>
  <Q q="What's the first thing to try before any of this?">
    Not sharing. Per-thread state with a periodic merge beats every algorithm in
    the chapter, and it's the option people skip past fastest.
  </Q>
</Quiz>
