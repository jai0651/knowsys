At some point in most backend careers you'll sit in a room, or a video call,
and hear "design a URL shortener" or "your service's p99 doubled overnight,
walk me through it". You have forty-five minutes, a whiteboard, and a stranger
writing notes.

Engineers who design real systems well still fail these interviews. The usual
reason probably isn't missing knowledge. It's spending the time on the wrong things:
drawing twelve boxes nobody asked about, and never reaching the one part of the
problem the question was written to test.

This chapter treats the interview as a system with a spec. It covers what's
being scored, how to budget the time, how to find the hard part of each
classic question, how to go deep on it, how the low-level "systems" questions
differ, and what changes with seniority. Most of the depth you'll need is in
the other chapters; this one is about using it under a clock.

<SectionHeading n="01" id="what-is-scored">What the interview is measuring</SectionHeading>

A design interview is a work sample. For forty-five minutes you're asked to do
a compressed version of what the job involves: take a vague request, turn it
into requirements, and propose something that would work, while someone watches
how you get there.

<Sub n="1.1" title="Why companies bother with a structured interview">

The format exists because it predicts job performance better than a chat.
Schmidt and Hunter's 1998 meta-analysis of 85 years of selection research
([Psychological Bulletin 124(2)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.124.2.262))
put the validity of structured interviews at **0.51**, against **0.38** for
unstructured ones.

"Structured" means every candidate gets comparable questions, and interviewers
score against written criteria instead of an overall impression.

<Why>What does that mean for you?</Why>

It means there's a rubric, and the interviewer is filling it in as you talk.
They can only score what they see or hear. A trade-off you considered silently
and didn't say out loud doesn't exist on their sheet.

</Sub>

<Sub n="1.2" title="The axes a design answer is scored on">

Companies don't publish their rubrics, but the widely used prep frameworks
agree on what interviewers look for. Alex Xu's framework
([ByteByteGo](https://bytebytego.com/courses/system-design-interview/a-framework-for-system-design-interviews))
lists the "ability to collaborate, to work under pressure, and to resolve
ambiguity constructively" and "the ability to ask good questions". Hello
Interview's [delivery guide](https://www.hellointerview.com/learn/system-design/in-a-hurry/delivery)
says "more senior candidates should be able to identify these places themselves
and lead the discussion".

Put together, they come to roughly four axes:

| Axis | What earns it | What loses it |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem navigation** | Clarifying scope, naming the requirements that matter, finding the hard part early | Designing before asking anything; treating every feature as equal |
| **Solution design** | A design that meets the stated requirements, with components that have reasons to exist | Boxes without purpose; over-engineering |
| **Technical depth** | Real mechanisms and numbers in the deep dive: how the index, the replication or the cache actually behaves | Buzzwords that dissolve under one follow-up question |
| **Communication** | Thinking aloud, checking in, taking a hint and using it | Long silences; defending a choice after the interviewer showed a flaw |

<Why>Why isn't "the right answer" on the list?</Why>

Because there isn't one. A feed can be built with fan-out on write or fan-out on
read; both are defensible for some requirements. What's scored is whether you
picked for a stated reason, and whether you know what your pick costs.

<Callout label="Design implication">
Say your reasoning out loud, including the options you rejected. "I considered
fan-out on read, but reads outnumber writes fifty to one here, so I'm paying on
write" earns credit on three axes in one sentence. The same decision made
silently earns it on one.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="1.3" title="The question behind the question">

Every classic prompt is written around one or two hard parts. The interviewer
knows what they are. Most of your score depends on whether you find them and
spend your deep-dive time there.

| Prompt | What it's really testing | The mechanism you'll need |
|---|---|---|
| **URL shortener** | ID generation without coordination; a read-heavy cache | Snowflake-style IDs or base-62 counters; cache hit ratio |
| **News feed / Twitter** | Fan-out on write versus read, and the celebrity problem | Precomputed timelines plus merge at read time for huge accounts |
| **Chat / messaging** | Connection state for millions of long-lived sockets; ordering per conversation | Gateway tier, per-conversation sequence numbers, presence |
| **Rate limiter** | Counting across many servers without a bottleneck | Token bucket in a shared store; what happens when the store fails |
| **Payments / checkout** | Exactly-once effects over an unreliable network | Idempotency keys, state machines, reconciliation |
| **Typeahead / search suggest** | Latency under a few tens of ms at huge read rates | Precomputed prefix results, tries, caching at the edge |
| **Distributed cache** | Partitioning, rebalancing and hot keys | Consistent hashing, replication, eviction policy |
| **Metrics / logging pipeline** | Write throughput and aggregation | Log-structured ingest, batching, downsampling |

<Why>How do you find the hard part if you don't recognise the prompt?</Why>

Ask which requirement is **most expensive to meet** at the stated scale. It's
probably the one where a naive design does work proportional to something huge:
followers per post, open connections per server, retries per failure. Name it
out loud once you've found it: "the interesting part here is the fan-out".

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="02" id="shape">The shape of a forty-five-minute answer</SectionHeading>

Both popular frameworks split the time the same way: a short scoping phase, a
broad design, and most of the remaining time on depth.

<Sub n="2.1" title="Two frameworks, one shape">

| Phase | Xu (ByteByteGo), 45 min | Hello Interview |
|---|---|---|
| Scope and requirements | 3–10 min | Requirements ~5 min |
| Entities and API | (part of high-level design) | Core entities ~2 min, API ~5 min, optional data flow ~5 min |
| High-level design | 10–15 min | 10–15 min |
| Deep dive | 10–25 min | ~10 min |
| Wrap-up | 3–5 min | (not a separate phase) |

Here's the answer as a pipeline, with what each phase should leave on the board:

    { t: "High-level", s: "~15 min", icon: "▦" },

    [2, "Name the core entities and the handful of API calls. `POST /urls`, `GET /{code}`. This pins down what the boxes will have to do."],
    [3, "Draw the simplest design that meets the functional requirements. Then walk one request through it, end to end, out loud."],
    [4, "Go deep on **the hard part** from section 1.3: the mechanism, the numbers, the failure mode. This is where most of the score is decided."],
    [5, "Summarise the trade-offs you made, the bottleneck you'd hit next, and what you'd monitor. Leave a minute for questions."],
  ]}
/>

</Sub>

<Sub n="2.2" title="Requirements: turning adjectives into numbers">

"Highly available, low latency, scalable" describes every system and constrains
none of them. A non-functional requirement is useful only when it's a number
you could be wrong about.

| Vague | Useful | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| "Low latency" | p99 under 200 ms for reads | Whether a synchronous cross-region call is allowed |
| "Highly available" | 99.9% monthly, reads only | About 43 minutes of downtime a month; writes may degrade |
| "Scalable" | 100 M daily users, 10:1 read/write | Whether one database primary can take the writes |
| "Consistent" | A user sees their own post immediately; others within 5 s | Read-your-writes, not global linearisability |
| "Durable" | No acknowledged payment is ever lost | Synchronous replication before the acknowledgement |

<Why>Why spend precious minutes on this?</Why>

Because every later decision is justified against these numbers. "I'm using
async replication because the requirement is five seconds of staleness" is a
design argument. "I'm using async replication" is a preference.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Starting to draw in the first minute. You'll build a design for a problem you
invented, and the interviewer will spend the next ten minutes steering you back
to theirs. Ask first. Two or three questions are probably enough; twenty is stalling.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="03" id="estimation">Estimation that changes a decision</SectionHeading>

The two frameworks disagree on one thing. Xu's includes back-of-the-envelope
estimation early. Hello Interview calls upfront estimation "_often_ unnecessary" and suggests
telling the interviewer you'll "do math while designing when/if necessary".

Both are right about something. Arithmetic that doesn't change a decision
is wasted time; arithmetic that does is the most persuasive thing you can put on
a whiteboard.

<Sub n="3.1" title="Numbers worth carrying">

Round everything. The goal is the order of magnitude.

| Quantity | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Seconds in a day | 86,400, call it `10⁵` | |
| L1 cache hit | 0.5 ns (Norvig); 1.2–1.5 ns measured on an M4 | [Norvig](https://norvig.com/21-days.html); [chapter 02](/topics/02-memory-hierarchy) |
| Main memory access | 100 ns (Norvig); 83 ns measured on an M4 | same |
| A real syscall | about 350 ns on an M4 | [chapter 07](/topics/07-syscalls-kernel-boundary) |
| Send 2 KB over 1 Gbps | 20 µs | Norvig |
| 4 KB random read, laptop NVMe, queue depth 1 | 120.8 µs | [chapter 09](/topics/09-storage-devices) |
| Read 1 MB sequentially from memory | 250 µs | Norvig |
| Disk seek (spinning) | 8 ms | Norvig |
| US to Europe and back | 150 ms | Norvig |

Norvig's figures are from a "typical PC" of their time; the measured ones are
from one machine. Neither is a promise about yours, but both are the right
order of magnitude, which is all an estimate needs.

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.2" title="A worked estimate: does the feed fit?">

Say the prompt is a feed for 100 million daily users, each opening the app five
times a day and posting once every ten days.

<Estimate

    { label: "Peak reads (assume 3× average)", math: "5,000 × 3", value: "≈ 15,000/s" },
    { label: "Posts per second", math: "100M / 10 / 10⁵ s", value: "≈ 100/s" },
    { label: "Fan-out writes if average follower count is 200", math: "100 × 200", value: "≈ 20,000 inserts/s" },
    { label: "Post storage per year at 1 KB per post", math: "100/s × 10⁵ × 365 × 1 KB", value: "≈ 3.6 TB" },
  ]}
  result={{ label: "Reads per post", value: "≈ 50 : 1" }}
/>

Two decisions fall out of it. Reads outnumber posts fifty to one, so it's worth
doing work at write time to make reads cheap. And 3.6 TB a year of posts is
small enough that the storage choice is about access pattern, not volume.

<Predict
  q="In 2012 Twitter served about 300,000 timeline reads a second against about 6,000 writes a second. Given that, where should the work of assembling a home timeline happen?"
  options={[
    "At read time: query everyone the user follows and merge",
    "At write time: push each tweet into every follower's precomputed timeline",
    "At write time for most accounts, at read time for accounts with huge followings",
    "It doesn't matter at that ratio",
  ]}
  answer={2}
>
Per the [High Scalability summary](https://highscalability.com/the-architecture-twitter-uses-to-deal-with-150m-active-users/)
of Raffi Krikorian's talk, Twitter fanned tweets out on write into home
timelines held in Redis, capped at 800 entries. A tweet from a user with 20,000
followers meant 20,000 inserts. For the largest accounts that became tens of
millions of inserts per tweet, so for them Twitter stopped fanning out and
merged their tweets in at read time. A hybrid is the answer that survives the
follow-up question.
</Predict>

</Sub>

<Sub n="3.3" title="The estimates that change designs">

| Estimate | If the answer is… | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Read/write ratio | Far above 10:1 | Precompute, cache, fan out on write |
| Working set size | Fits in one machine's RAM | A cache tier or even a single node may do |
| Write rate at peak | Beyond one primary's capacity | Partition the write path ([chapter 22](/topics/22-redis) on Redis Cluster slots is one model) |
| Storage growth | Petabytes a year | Tiering, object storage, retention policy |
| Latency budget vs round trips | Budget smaller than the round trips | Fewer hops, colocate, or go asynchronous |
| Fan-out factor | Unbounded (followers, recipients) | Cap it, batch it, or change direction for the tail |

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Five minutes of arithmetic that ends with "so we'll need a lot of servers". If
a number doesn't lead to a sentence starting "so I'll…", skip it and say you're
skipping it.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="04" id="high-level">The high-level design</SectionHeading>

The high-level design is where you show a working system. It should be the
simplest thing that meets the functional requirements, drawn so that one request
can be followed through it.

<Sub n="4.1" title="Walk one request through the boxes">

A box earns its place when you can say what it does to a specific request.
Here's the write path of the hybrid feed from section 3.2, walked through
component by component, as you'd narrate it:

<StepSequence
  title="Posting to a feed with hybrid fan-out"
  actors={["Client", "Post service", "Fan-out workers", "Timeline cache", "Reader"]}
  messages={[
    { from: 0, to: 1, label: "POST /posts", note: "The post service writes the post to durable storage and assigns a time-ordered ID. **The user's request can return now**; fan-out is asynchronous." },
    { from: 1, to: 2, label: "enqueue post id", note: "A message goes on a queue. If the author has a normal follower count, workers will fan it out." },
    { from: 2, to: 3, label: "push id into N timelines", note: "Workers look up the author's followers and prepend the post ID to each follower's cached timeline, trimmed to a fixed length (Twitter used 800)." },
    { from: 1, to: 1, label: "huge account: skip fan-out", note: "If the author has millions of followers, **don't fan out**. Mark the post as belonging to a celebrity source instead." },
    { from: 4, to: 3, label: "GET /timeline", note: "A reader fetches their precomputed timeline: one cache read, not a query across everyone they follow." },
    { from: 3, to: 4, label: "merge celebrity posts", note: "At read time, merge in recent posts from the few huge accounts they follow. That's a handful of extra reads instead of millions of writes." },
  ]}
/>

<Why>Why narrate a request instead of describing components?</Why>

Because it exposes gaps. Describing a "fan-out service" sounds complete.
Walking a post through it forces the questions an interviewer will ask anyway:
what happens when the author has 30 million followers, and does the user wait
for it?

</Sub>

<Sub n="4.2" title="Choosing components, with reasons">

Every component choice is a trade-off, and each should come with a one-line
reason tied to a requirement:

| Choice | Reach for it when | Cost you should name |
|---|---|---|
| **Cache in front of the database** | Read-heavy, tolerant of brief staleness | Invalidation, cold starts, thundering herd on a miss |
| **Queue between services** | Work can be asynchronous; absorb bursts | Delivery is at-least-once; consumers must be idempotent |
| **Relational database** | Transactions, joins, moderate write rate | Scaling writes needs partitioning |
| **Wide-column / KV store** | Huge write rates, simple access by key | No joins; data modelled around queries |
| **Object storage** | Large blobs, cheap durability | Latency per request in tens of ms |
| **Read replicas** | Scale reads | Replication lag breaks read-your-writes |

<Callout label="Design implication">
Name the cost with the choice. "A queue here, so posting stays fast even when
fan-out is slow; the consumers have to be idempotent because delivery is
at-least-once" is a senior sentence. It shows you know what the component does
to the rest of the system.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="05" id="deep-dives">Going deep</SectionHeading>

The deep dive is where depth is scored, and where most answers fall apart. The
interviewer picks a component, or you do, and asks follow-ups until you reach
the limit of what you know.

<Sub n="5.1" title="What a probe looks like">

Here's the rate-limiter question, a few minutes into the deep dive. Notice that
each question takes the previous answer one layer down:

<StepSequence
  title="An interviewer probing a rate limiter"
  actors={["Interviewer", "Candidate"]}
  messages={[
    { from: 0, to: 1, label: "How do you limit per user across 50 API servers?", note: "The hard part from 1.3: **counting across machines**. A per-server counter lets a user through 50 times the limit." },
    { from: 1, to: 0, label: "Token bucket, state in a shared store", note: "A good answer names the algorithm and where state lives. Stripe [describes](https://stripe.com/blog/rate-limiters) exactly this: token buckets with state in Redis." },
    { from: 0, to: 1, label: "Every request now hits Redis. Latency?", note: "The follow-up tests whether you know what the extra hop costs: a network round trip on every request." },
    { from: 1, to: 0, label: "One round trip; atomic script; local pre-check", note: "Numbers and mechanism: a single atomic read-modify-write per request, and a local approximate limit to shed obvious abuse first." },
    { from: 0, to: 1, label: "Redis goes down. What happens?", note: "The **failure-mode probe**. Every deep dive gets one." },
    { from: 1, to: 0, label: "Fail open, alert, keep a local fallback", note: "Stripe's advice: if the limiter's store fails, requests \"wouldn't be affected\"; errors \"fail open\". Saying *why* (the limiter mustn't be less available than the API) earns the point." },
  ]}
/>

<Why>What's the interviewer actually looking for in a probe?</Why>

Whether your knowledge has a floor. Anyone can say "use Redis". The follow-ups
test whether you know what that choice does to latency, what happens when it
fails, and what you'd do instead. Running out of knowledge at roughly the fourth
question is normal. Running out at the first is the signal they're checking
for.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.2" title="Three probes every deep dive gets">

You can prepare for most follow-ups because they come in three kinds:

| Probe | The question | What a strong answer includes |
|---|---|---|
| **Failure** | "This node dies. What happens?" | Detection time, who takes over, what's lost, what the user sees |
| **Scale** | "Traffic grows 10×. What breaks first?" | The first resource to saturate, with a number, and the fix |
| **Correctness** | "Two of these happen at once. What happens?" | The race, the invariant it breaks, and the mechanism that prevents it |

Answer each at the level of **mechanism**. "It fails over" isn't an answer.
"The replica notices the missed heartbeats after a few seconds, a new leader is
elected, and writes acknowledged by the old leader but not replicated are lost,
unless replication was synchronous" is.

</Sub>

<Sub n="5.3" title="Deep-dive material, by topic">

These come up constantly, and each is a mechanism you can explain from first
principles:

| Topic | The mechanism to know | Where it's covered |
|---|---|---|
| **Unique IDs without coordination** | Twitter's [Snowflake](https://github.com/twitter-archive/snowflake/tree/snowflake-2010): 41 bits of milliseconds, 10 bits of machine ID, 12 bits of sequence | This chapter |
| **Exactly-once effects** | Client-generated idempotency keys, as in Stripe's [idempotency design](https://stripe.com/blog/idempotency), plus retries with backoff and jitter | This chapter |
| **Cache behaviour** | Hit ratio, eviction, stampedes on a hot miss | [Chapter 22](/topics/22-redis) |
| **Where latency comes from** | Queueing near saturation; fan-out turns rare slowness into the common case | [Chapter 16](/topics/16-contention-tail-latency) |
| **Why a server drops connections** | Accept queues, conntrack, socket buffers | [Chapter 10](/topics/10-linux-networking) |
| **Sizing and autoscaling** | Little's Law, headroom, autoscaler lag | [Chapter 42](/topics/42-queueing-and-capacity) |
| **Network boundaries in the cloud** | VPCs, NAT, cross-zone traffic | [Chapter 33](/topics/33-vpc-networking) |

<Why>Why Snowflake's layout in particular?</Why>

Because it's a compact answer to a question most shorteners and feeds hit, and
every field has a reason. Twelve bits of sequence give 4,096 IDs per millisecond
per machine, and 41 bits of milliseconds cover 69 years from a custom epoch. The
[README](https://github.com/twitter-archive/snowflake/tree/snowflake-2010) also
says what happens when the clock runs backwards: Snowflake "will refuse to
generate ids" until time passes the last one it issued. That's the failure mode,
and interviewers ask about it.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="06" id="systems-questions">Systems questions: going down the stack</SectionHeading>

Some interviews, especially for infrastructure, SRE and backend roles, swap the
design prompt for a systems question. They're not asking what to build; they're
asking how the thing already built works, or why it's misbehaving.

<Sub n="6.1" title="Two kinds of systems question">

| Kind | Example | What it's testing |
|---|---|---|
| **Explain the path** | "What happens when you type a URL and press enter?" | Whether you can walk the layers in order and go deep where asked |
| **Debug the symptom** | "p99 latency doubled after a deploy; CPU is flat. Walk me through it." | Method: hypotheses, the evidence that separates them, and the tools |

<Why>How deep should "what happens when you type a URL" go?</Why>

As deep as the interviewer steers. Give a complete, shallow pass first: DNS,
TCP, TLS, HTTP, the server, the response, rendering. Then offer to go deeper on
one layer, or follow where they point. A complete shallow walk followed by one
deep layer beats a deep first layer that never reaches the server.

</Sub>

<Sub n="6.2" title="A debugging answer, step by step">

The debugging question is a method test. Here's the shape of a strong answer to
"p99 doubled after a deploy, CPU is flat":

<Steps
  title="p99 doubled, CPU flat: a structured answer"
  lanes={[
    { t: "Scope", s: "which, since when", icon: "?" },
    { t: "Diff", s: "what changed", icon: "Δ" },
    { t: "Split", s: "on-CPU vs waiting", icon: "◐" },
    { t: "Resources", s: "USE pass", icon: "▤" },
    { t: "Confirm", s: "one test", icon: "✓" },
  ]}
  steps={[
    [0, "Ask which endpoints, all hosts or some, and whether p50 moved too. **p99 up with p50 flat** points at waiting, not at more work per request."],
    [1, "Diff the deploy: code, config, dependency versions, instance counts. And check whether traffic changed at the same time. A rollback is a valid first mitigation; say so."],
    [2, "CPU flat plus a worse tail suggests **off-CPU time**: a lock, a pool, a slow dependency. A CPU profile won't see it ([chapter 41](/topics/41-performance-engineering))."],
    [3, "Walk the resources: connection pool saturation (Little's Law against the pool size), downstream latency, GC pauses, container CPU throttling, retransmits."],
    [4, "Name the one measurement that would separate your top two hypotheses, and what you'd do if it came back each way."],
  ]}
/>

What scores isn't guessing the cause. It's the structure: narrowing the space,
naming the evidence, and ordering hypotheses from cheap to check to expensive.

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Jumping to the most interesting cause. "It's probably GC" in the first thirty
seconds sounds like experience and scores as guessing. Scope first, then
narrow. You can still end up at GC, with a reason.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="07" id="levels">What changes with seniority</SectionHeading>

The same prompt is given to mid-level, senior and staff candidates. The bar
moves in who drives and how far past the obvious the answer goes.

<Sub n="7.1" title="The same question at three levels">

Hello Interview's guide makes the key distinction: "More junior candidates can
expect the interviewer to jump in here and point out places where the design
could be improved. More senior candidates should be able to identify these
places themselves and lead the discussion." Extending that:

| | Mid-level | Senior | Staff |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who drives** | Interviewer steers; you respond well | You drive; interviewer probes | You drive, and reframe the problem when it's the wrong one |
| **Depth** | A working design; depth in one area when asked | Finds the hard part unprompted; deep on it with numbers | Deep in several areas; knows where the design will break in a year |
| **Trade-offs** | Knows the options | Picks with reasons tied to requirements | Weighs operational cost, team ownership, migration path |
| **Failure** | Handles single-node failure | Handles partitions, overload, retries | Handles cascading failure and what the organisation does about it |

<Why>Does "staff" mean a bigger design?</Why>

No. It usually means a more *selective* one. Staff answers are often smaller
than senior ones, because they cut features the requirements don't need and
spend the saved time on migration, operability and how the system fails.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="08" id="failure-modes">Why good engineers fail these interviews</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="8.1" title="Symptom, cause, fix">

| Symptom in the interview | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Interviewer keeps redirecting you | You designed before scoping | Ask two or three questions and write the requirements down first |
| Ran out of time before the deep dive | Too long on estimation or on boxes | Budget by the clock: high-level done by minute 25 |
| Deep dive stalls at the first follow-up | Knowledge of names, not mechanisms | Study how one system of each kind works inside |
| "Why did you choose that?" and you can't say | A default, not a decision | Tie every choice to a requirement, out loud |
| Interviewer hints and you don't take it | Defending the design | Treat hints as requirements you missed; adopt, and say why |
| Design is huge and shallow | Listing every component you know | Cut to what the requirements need; go deep on the hard part |
| Silence for minutes | Thinking privately | Think aloud, even when unsure; say what you're weighing |

<Callout label="Common mistake" kind="warn">
Memorising designs. Interviewers change one requirement ("messages must be
deleted after reading", "the celebrity has 200 million followers") precisely to
see whether you can re-derive instead of recite. Learn why each design choice
was made, so you can remake it when a requirement changes.
</Callout>

</Sub>

<Sub n="8.2" title="Handling what you don't know">

You'll hit questions past your knowledge. The scored behaviour is how you handle
the edge:

- **Say where your knowledge ends.** "I haven't operated Cassandra, but I'd
  expect a write here to go to the commit log and memtable first. Is that the
  part you want to explore?"
- **Reason from principles.** You may not know a system's exact default, but you
  can probably reason about what it must do and what that would cost.
- **Don't invent specifics.** A made-up number or config flag is worse than "I'd
  measure it". Interviewers often know the real value.

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="09" id="practice">How to practise</SectionHeading>

<Sub n="9.1" title="A practice plan">

1. **Pick eight prompts** from section 1.3 and, for each, write down the hard
   part and the mechanism before you design anything.
2. **Rehearse against a clock.** Forty-five minutes, out loud, with the phase
   budget from section 2.1. Record it, or practise with someone who'll interrupt.
3. **Learn one system deeply per category**: one database, one cache, one
   queue, one load balancer. Deep-dive answers come from knowing one real thing
   well, not ten things by name.
4. **Drill the three probes** (failure, scale, correctness) against every design
   you practise.
5. **Keep a numbers sheet** like section 3.1, filled in with numbers you've
   measured yourself where you can.
6. **Review each practice run** against the four axes in section 1.2. Which
   one lost points?

</Sub>

<SectionHeading n="10" id="summary">Summary</SectionHeading>

1. **It's a structured work sample.** Structured interviews predict job
   performance better than unstructured ones (0.51 against 0.38 in Schmidt and
   Hunter), and they're scored against a rubric.
2. **Only what you say gets scored.** Think aloud, including the options you
   rejected.
3. **Every prompt hides one or two hard parts.** Find the requirement most
   expensive to meet at scale and name it.
4. **Budget the clock.** About five minutes of scope, fifteen of high-level
   design, and the largest block on the deep dive.
5. **Requirements are numbers.** "p99 under 200 ms" constrains a design; "low
   latency" doesn't.
6. **Estimate only what decides something.** A 50:1 read/write ratio justifies
   fan-out on write; "a lot of servers" justifies nothing.
7. **Walk a request through the boxes.** It exposes gaps before the interviewer
   does.
8. **Deep dives get three probes**: failure, scale and correctness. Answer at the
   level of mechanism.
9. **Seniority changes who drives**, and how selective the design is, more than
   how big it is.
10. **Learn why designs were made, not the designs.** Interviewers change a
    requirement to see whether you can re-derive.

<SectionHeading n="12" id="interview">Interview questions</SectionHeading>

<QA level="beginner" q="Design a URL shortener. What's the hard part?">
Generating short, unique codes without a coordination bottleneck, and serving
a read-heavy workload fast. For IDs: a Snowflake-style scheme (timestamp,
machine ID, sequence) or pre-allocated ranges from a counter, encoded in base
62. For reads: a cache in front of the store, since popular links are read far
more than they're written. Follow-ups are usually custom aliases (a uniqueness
check), expiry, and analytics without slowing the redirect.
</QA>

<QA level="beginner" q="Why ask clarifying questions instead of starting to design?">
Because the requirements decide the design, and the prompt deliberately leaves
them out. Two or three questions (scale, read/write mix, latency and
consistency needs, what's out of scope) turn "design a chat app" into a problem
with a checkable answer. They also score directly: resolving ambiguity is one of
the things interviewers are asked to look for.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Fan-out on write or fan-out on read for a news feed?">
It depends on the read/write ratio and the follower distribution. Fan-out on
write makes reads one cache lookup but costs a write per follower; fan-out on
read makes posting cheap but every read queries everyone you follow. With reads
far outnumbering writes, as at Twitter (about 300k reads against 6k writes a
second in 2012), fan out on write, except for accounts with huge followings,
whose posts are merged in at read time.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="How would you make a payment API safe to retry?">
Idempotency keys. The client generates a unique key per logical operation and
sends it with each attempt. The server records the key with the result in the
same transaction as the effect, and on a repeat returns the stored result
instead of charging again. Clients retry with exponential backoff and jitter so
a recovering server isn't flattened. That's the design Stripe describes publicly.
</QA>

<QA level="intermediate" q="Your rate limiter keeps its state in Redis. Redis fails. What happens?">
Decide in advance, and usually fail open: let requests through, alert, and fall
back to a coarse per-server limit. The limiter exists to protect the API, so
it mustn't become the API's worst dependency. Stripe's guidance is that
limiter errors should "fail open" so the API stays functional. Fail closed only
where letting traffic through is worse than an outage, such as a brute-force
login limit.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="Walk me through what happens when a Snowflake-style ID generator's clock goes backwards.">
IDs are time-ordered because the top bits are milliseconds since an epoch. If NTP
steps the clock back, the generator could reissue timestamps it already used,
and with the same machine ID and a reset sequence, produce duplicate IDs.
Snowflake refuses to generate IDs until the clock passes the last timestamp it
issued. The costs: a stall for the size of the step, and the need for unique
machine IDs, which is its own coordination problem at deploy time.
</QA>

<QA level="deep" q="How do you answer a question at a staff level instead of a senior level?">
Same prompt, different emphasis. Drive the discussion, and be more selective:
cut what the requirements don't need. Spend the time on where the design breaks
under growth, how it fails and recovers, what it costs to run, and how you'd
migrate to it from what exists. Weigh options by operational burden and
ownership as well as by performance. Often the answer is smaller, not bigger.
</QA>

<SectionHeading n="13" id="resources">Go deeper</SectionHeading>

<Quiz>
  <Q q="What are the three probes almost every deep dive includes?">
    Failure (this node dies), scale (traffic grows ten times) and correctness
    (two of these happen at once). Answer each with a mechanism and a number.
  </Q>
  <Q q="When should you skip a back-of-the-envelope estimate?">
    When no plausible answer would change a decision. Estimation earns time when
    it ends in "so I'll…".
  </Q>
  <Q q="Why did Twitter stop fanning out tweets from its largest accounts?">
    A tweet from an account with tens of millions of followers meant tens of
    millions of timeline inserts. Merging those accounts' tweets in at read time
    is a few extra reads per timeline instead.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Why does a structured interview use a rubric?">
    So candidates are scored on the same criteria. It's a large part of why
    structured interviews predict job performance better than unstructured ones.
  </Q>
</Quiz>
